Joshua 12:9

the king of Jericho, one;
Jericho was the first Canaanite city conquered west of the Jordan and became a decisive testimony that the land belonged to Israel by God’s gift, not merely by military strength. Its walls fell only after Israel obeyed the Lord’s unusual command to march around the city for seven days (Joshua 6). The city was placed under judgment because of Canaan’s entrenched wickedness, while Rahab and her household were spared through faith and allegiance to the God of Israel. Jericho therefore joins divine judgment with divine mercy, anticipating the salvation granted to all who trust the Lord.

the king of Ai, which is near Bethel, one;
Ai’s defeat followed Israel’s earlier humiliation caused by Achan’s disobedience. Though Ai was smaller than Jericho, Israel could not prevail while sin remained unaddressed in the camp (Joshua 7). After judgment, cleansing, and renewed submission to God, Joshua led Israel to victory through an ambush commanded by the Lord (Joshua 8). Located near Bethel, a place closely associated with Jacob’s worship and God’s covenant promises, Ai’s fall demonstrated that possession of the promised land required not self-confidence but faithful obedience to God.

the king of Jarmuth, one;
Jarmuth stood in the Shephelah, the foothills between Judah’s high country and the coastal plain. Its king, Piram, joined the Amorite coalition against Gibeon after Gibeon made peace with Joshua (Joshua 10:3–5). The coalition’s attack brought decisive judgment when the Lord fought for Israel, routing the kings before Joshua’s army. Jarmuth’s fortified position and regional influence could not withstand the God who had promised the land to His people. Its defeat records not merely military success, but the Lord’s faithfulness to act against entrenched opposition and to preserve those who sought peace with His covenant people.

the king of Lachish, one;
Lachish was a major fortified city in the Shephelah and an important guardian of routes leading into Judah’s hill country. Its king, Japhia, also marched with the alliance against Gibeon and was defeated in Joshua’s southern campaign (Joshua 10:23, 31–32). Archaeological remains testify to Lachish’s strength and long importance, while its later history includes Sennacherib’s Assyrian siege during Hezekiah’s reign. Joshua’s conquest of Lachish showed that strong walls, military power, and political alliances could not overturn the Lord’s purpose. The city’s inclusion among the defeated kings proclaims that Israel’s inheritance rested ultimately upon God’s power and promise.

Joshua 12:16

the king of Makkedah, one;
Makkedah lay in the southern region of Canaan, probably near the Shephelah of Judah. After the Amorite coalition attacked Gibeon, its five kings fled from Joshua’s army and hid in a cave at Makkedah. Joshua sealed the cave until Israel had completed its pursuit, then brought the kings out for public judgment (Joshua 10:16–27). Their execution confirmed that the Lord had defeated the powers opposing His people and had given Israel the land He promised. Makkedah thereafter became one of the cities taken in Joshua’s southern campaign (Joshua 10:28).

the king of Bethel, one;
Bethel, meaning “House of God,” was an important Canaanite city north of Jerusalem, associated with Abraham’s altar and Jacob’s vision of the stairway to heaven. Jacob named the place Bethel after the Lord reaffirmed the covenant promises to him there (Genesis 28:10–19). The defeat of its king marked the removal of another Canaanite stronghold from the land assigned to Israel. Bethel later held a prominent place in Israel’s worship and history, though it became a center of idolatry under Jeroboam. Its history warns that possession of a place blessed by God does not preserve a people who turn from Him.

the king of Tappuah, one;
Tappuah (apple or fruitful) was one of the Canaanite city-states overcome during Joshua’s campaigns. A town named Tappuah stood near the Ephraim-Manasseh border in the central hill country, while another lay in Judah’s lowlands; the king listed here may have ruled either settlement. Israel’s victory removed another fortified center of Canaanite resistance and further established the inheritance God had promised to Abraham and his descendants.

Joshua 12:21

the king of Taanach, one;
Taanach stood on the southern edge of the Jezreel Valley near important routes linking the coastal plain with the Jordan Valley. Joshua’s victory over its king demonstrated that even fortified Canaanite city-states could not withstand the Lord’s purpose to give Israel the land promised to Abraham. Taanach later appears in the song of Deborah, near the waters of Megiddo, where the Lord again defeated Israel’s enemies (Judg. 5:19). Although Manasseh later failed to drive out all the Canaanite inhabitants from Taanach, the earlier conquest testified to God’s faithfulness and exposed the cost of Israel’s incomplete obedience (Judg. 1:27).

the king of Megiddo, one;
Megiddo controlled a strategic pass on the Via Maris, the major international route through the Jezreel Valley. Its position made it a valuable military and commercial center, repeatedly contested throughout history. The fall of Megiddo’s king showed that the strength of Canaan’s alliances, defenses, and wealth could not overturn God’s judgment or prevent His promised inheritance from being established. Megiddo’s association with later battles, including the prophetic gathering at Armageddon, points beyond Israel’s conquest to the final certainty that every earthly power will answer to the Lord (Revelation 16:16).

Joshua 12:22

the king of Kedesh, one;
Kedesh stood in the hill country of Naphtali in northern Canaan and later became one of Israel’s cities of refuge (Joshua 20:7). Its conquest removed Canaanite authority from an important northern stronghold and prepared the land for Israel’s judicial and religious order. The city of refuge provided protection for one guilty of unintentional manslaughter until a just hearing could be held, pointing to the refuge God provides for those who flee to Him for mercy. The defeat of Kedesh’s king testifies that God’s promised inheritance was secured by His power.

the king of Jokneam in Carmel, one;
Jokneam lay near Mount Carmel, overlooking a fertile and strategically valuable region. The city was later assigned to Zebulun and given to the Levites (Joshua 19:11; 21:34). Its king’s defeat extended Israel’s control into the Carmel area, where God would later vindicate His name through Elijah’s confrontation with the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18). The conquest of Jokneam affirmed that the Lord alone ruled the land and would not share His glory with Canaanite gods.

Joshua 12:24

and the king of Tirzah, one.
Tirzah was a fortified Canaanite city in the central hill country, later identified with Tell el-Farah north of Shechem. Its king’s defeat demonstrated Israel’s victory over another independent Canaanite city-state. Tirzah later became the royal residence of the northern kingdom under Baasha and remained so until Omri established Samaria as his capital. Its later prominence makes its inclusion here a reminder that God had already subdued places that would hold major importance in Israel’s future history.
