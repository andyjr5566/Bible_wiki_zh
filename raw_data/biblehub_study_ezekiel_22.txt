Manasseh’s reign had especially filled Jerusalem with innocent blood, and later reforms did not remove the nation’s deep-rooted guilt (2 Kings 21:16; 24:3–4). Bloodshed defiled the land and demanded divine judgment under God’s law (Numbers 35:33–34). The city’s destruction was not arbitrary; its own sin had brought the day of accountability near.

False worship was joined to moral decay because rejection of the true God removed the foundation for justice, holiness, and mercy. Jerusalem had defiled herself by seeking life from what could neither see, hear, nor save, while rejecting the Lord who alone gives life.

Sabbath-breaking stood with idolatry and violence as evidence of covenant rebellion and contributed to the judgment that culminated in exile. God’s gift of holy rest ultimately points to the rest found in Christ, who brings believers into reconciliation and peace with God (Hebrews 4:9–10).

The Refining Furnace

Ezekiel 22:17

Then the word of the LORD came to me, saying,
Ezekiel received this message from the LORD during Israel’s exile in Babylon. God was about to portray Jerusalem and the house of Israel as dross gathered into a furnace, exposing the corruption of priests, princes, prophets, and people alike. The coming judgment was not Ezekiel’s personal assessment of the nation but God’s righteous declaration against covenant unfaithfulness. The LORD continued to speak through His prophet even as Jerusalem moved toward destruction, providing both a warning of divine wrath and a summons to recognize the holiness Israel had despised.

Ezekiel 22:18

“Son of man,
God addressed Ezekiel as a mortal man entrusted with the words of the living God. The title keeps the prophet mindful of his dependence on the Lord while emphasizing the authority of the message he must deliver. Ezekiel stood before a rebellious people as God’s appointed watchman, announcing judgment but also preserving hope for future restoration.

the house of Israel has become dross to Me.
Israel’s covenant people had become spiritually corrupt through idolatry, bloodshed, oppression, and disregard for God’s law. Rather than reflecting the holiness for which they had been chosen, the nation had accumulated the impurities of surrounding nations. God’s judgment was not arbitrary; it answered generations of hardened rebellion, especially in Jerusalem before its fall to Babylon.

The inhabitants of Judah and Jerusalem were like mixed and inferior metals gathered for smelting. God would use the Babylonian siege and destruction of Jerusalem as a furnace of judgment. No class of society would escape accountability: princes, priests, prophets, merchants, and common people had shared in the nation’s guilt.
