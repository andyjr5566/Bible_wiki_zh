2 Kings 15:1

In the twenty-seventh year of Jeroboam’s reign over Israel,
Jeroboam II ruled during a season of renewed political strength and territorial expansion in the northern kingdom, as God had spoken through Jonah son of Amittai (2 Kings 14:23–29). Israel’s outward prosperity, however, concealed deep covenant unfaithfulness. Amos and Hosea exposed the nation’s idolatry, injustice, and false security. The dating of Judah’s king by Israel’s reign underscores the divided condition of God’s people after Solomon, even as the Lord continued to govern the course of both kingdoms.

Azariah son of Amaziah
Azariah, also called Uzziah, succeeded his father Amaziah in Judah. His long reign brought military success, building projects, agricultural development, and national stability, especially during the years when he sought the Lord (2 Chronicles 26:3–15). Yet later prosperity fostered pride. Azariah unlawfully entered the temple to burn incense, a ministry appointed to the Aaronic priests, and God struck him with leprosy. His life warns that strength and success are safe only under humble obedience to the Lord.

Uzziah, also called Azariah, succeeded his father Amaziah and began ruling Judah while still young. His long reign began in a period when Judah needed stable leadership after Amaziah’s troubled final years. God used the early years of Uzziah’s rule to bring strength and prosperity to Judah, especially while he sought the Lord under the guidance of Zechariah (2 Chronicles 26:5).

and he reigned in Jerusalem fifty-two years.
Uzziah’s reign was among the longest in Judah’s history. He strengthened Jerusalem’s defenses, developed agriculture, expanded Judah’s military power, and restored Elath as a Judean port (2 Kings 14:22; 2 Chronicles 26:6–15). Yet lasting success became a spiritual danger. Uzziah later entered the temple to burn incense, a duty reserved for the priests, and the Lord struck him with leprosy. He lived separated from public life until his death, while his son Jotham governed in his place (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Isaiah received his temple vision in the year Uzziah died, seeing the holiness of the true King who reigns above every earthly throne (Isaiah 6:1).

His alliance with Rezin of Aram against Judah led to the Syro-Ephraimite conflict described in 2 Kings 16 and Isaiah 7.

Jotham son of Uzziah became king of Judah.
Jotham continued the Davidic line after Uzziah’s long reign. Having already governed while his leprous father lived in seclusion, Jotham brought experienced and faithful leadership to Judah. He walked uprightly before the Lord, strengthened the nation, and maintained the temple-centered worship that distinguished Judah from Israel’s persistent idolatry. His reign preserved the covenant line through which the promised King would come.

2 Kings 15:34

And he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD,
Jotham governed Judah in obedience to the Lord’s covenant and did not turn to the idolatry that marked many surrounding rulers. His reign demonstrates that royal success and national security were to be measured first by faithfulness to God’s commands. Deuteronomy 6:18 calls God’s people to do what is right and good in His sight, grounding blessing and stability in obedient trust.

Uzziah’s earlier faithfulness provided Jotham with a godly example of seeking the Lord, though Uzziah later fell through pride when he unlawfully entered the temple to burn incense (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Jotham did not repeat that presumptuous act; he recognized the boundaries God had established for the king and the priesthood (2 Chronicles 27:2). Yet Judah’s people continued in corrupt practices, showing that a righteous king could not substitute for the personal obedience required of the nation.

2 Kings 15:35

Nevertheless, the high places were not taken away;
Jotham followed the Lord as his father Uzziah had done, yet he did not remove the unauthorized places of worship scattered throughout Judah. God had commanded Israel to destroy Canaanite worship sites and to bring sacrifices to the place He chose for His name, ultimately Jerusalem (Deuteronomy 12:2–6). The continued existence of the high places revealed that Judah’s reforms remained incomplete.

Many of the people may have intended to honor the LORD, but worship offered according to human preference rather than God’s command opened the door to compromise. The high places had long been associated with pagan practices, and their preservation encouraged a divided religious life. Judah maintained the temple and its ordained worship while tolerating rival altars throughout the land.
