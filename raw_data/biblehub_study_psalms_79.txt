A Prayer for Deliverance

A Psalm of Asaph.
The superscription associates the psalm with Asaph’s worshiping ministry and the prophetic tradition of the Asaphite singers. Israel’s grief is voiced before God rather than concealed from Him.

Psalm 79:2

They have given the corpses of Your servants as food to the birds of the air,
Jerusalem’s devastation left many of God’s people without burial, a grievous dishonor in Israel and a visible sign of national humiliation. The scene recalls the covenant curse of Deuteronomy 28:26 and was fulfilled in the judgments announced by Jeremiah (Jeremiah 7:33). Though Babylonian invaders were the immediate agents of destruction, the psalm recognizes that Judah’s rebellion had brought God’s righteous discipline. Yet the slain are still called God’s servants, showing that divine judgment did not cancel His covenant claim upon His people.

Deu 28:26 Your corpses will be food for all the birds of the air…

1Sa 17:46 This day the LORD will deliver you into my hand.…

the flesh of Your saints to the beasts of the earth.
Those set apart to the Lord suffered the same horrors that fell upon the nation, revealing the far-reaching consequences of sin and invasion. Their desecration intensifies the psalmist’s appeal for God to vindicate His holy name, judge the nations that defiled His inheritance, and restore His people. The disgrace of unburied bodies also points beyond judgment to the hope that God does not abandon His servants to death, a hope ultimately secured in Christ, who endured humiliation and death yet rose in victory.

Psalm 79:3

They have poured out their blood like water
The slaughter of Jerusalem’s inhabitants is pictured as blood flowing freely and without restraint. The Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC brought the covenant curses warned in Leviticus 26:33 and Jeremiah 19:7–9. Blood that should have been treated with reverence in sacrifices was instead spilled in violence, displaying the horror of sin, judgment, and human cruelty.

Ezk 22:2-4 “As for you, son of man, will you judge her?”…

all around Jerusalem,
The devastation extended beyond the city’s walls into the surrounding region. Jerusalem, the place of the temple and David’s throne, appeared abandoned to its enemies. Its fall demonstrated that outward religious privilege could not shield a rebellious people from God’s judgment. The ruined city also anticipates later judgments upon Jerusalem, including the destruction foretold by Jesus and carried out in AD 70.

and there is no one to bury the dead.
Unburied bodies marked utter defeat and disgrace, since burial was a sacred responsibility in Israel. Death, exile, and fear had left no one able to perform that duty, fulfilling warnings such as Jeremiah 7:33. The scene magnifies the need for the Lord’s mercy and points beyond judgment to the hope secured by Christ, who conquered death and will raise His people to everlasting life.

Psalm 79:4

We have become a reproach to our neighbors,
Jerusalem’s destruction and the defilement of the temple brought public shame upon the covenant people. Israel’s disobedience had invited the covenant judgments warned in Deuteronomy 28:37, yet the nations’ mockery also dishonored the name of Israel’s God. The lament appeals to the Lord to vindicate His holiness and restore His people.

Deu 28:37 You will become an object of horror, scorn, and ridicule…

a scorn and derision to those around us.
Edom, Moab, Ammon, and other neighboring peoples rejoiced over Judah’s collapse, treating Israel’s defeat as proof that her God had failed. Lamentations records similar taunts against ruined Jerusalem. The contempt borne by God’s afflicted people anticipates the rejection endured by Christ, who was despised by men and bore reproach in order to bring redemption.

Psalm 79:5

How long, O LORD?
The survivors of Jerusalem’s devastation appeal to the covenant God who had allowed His people to be disciplined for their sin. Similar cries rise from Habakkuk and from the martyrs awaiting God’s judgment in Revelation. Faith does not deny the length or pain of suffering; it brings that anguish directly before the Lord while trusting that He remains faithful to His promises.

Will You be angry forever?
Israel’s history showed that rebellion brought God’s righteous judgment, yet His anger was never His final purpose for His repentant people. The Lord who disciplines also restores, forgives, and renews. The question rests on the assurance that God is merciful and that His covenant love endures beyond deserved chastening.

Will Your jealousy burn like fire?
God’s jealousy arises from His holy and exclusive claim upon His people, whom He redeemed to worship Him alone. Israel’s idolatry had provoked the consuming fire of divine judgment, but the same holy zeal calls them back from false gods to covenant faithfulness. Their only hope lay not in national strength, but in the Lord’s mercy and their return to Him.

Psalm 79:6

Pour out Your wrath on the nations that do not acknowledge You,
Israel’s devastation had exposed the violence of peoples who neither knew nor honored the Lord. The psalmist appeals to God as the righteous Judge, asking Him to vindicate His holy name and defend His afflicted people. Though God used foreign powers to discipline Judah, those nations remained accountable for their pride, idolatry, cruelty, and opposition to Him.

on the kingdoms that refuse to call on Your name,
Calling on the Lord’s name signifies worship, dependence, and submission to His rule. The kingdoms surrounding Israel trusted false gods and military strength rather than the Creator. The prayer places judgment in God’s hands and anticipates the day when every nation will acknowledge His sovereignty, ultimately fulfilled in the universal lordship of Christ.

Psalm 79:7

for they have devoured Jacob
Foreign invaders had overwhelmed Israel with ruthless violence, treating God’s covenant people as prey. The Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC stands behind the psalm’s grief: many were killed, the survivors were exiled, and the nation’s strength was broken. Israel’s suffering fulfilled covenant warnings for persistent rebellion, yet the psalm appeals to the Lord as Israel’s true defender and Redeemer.

Psa 14:4 Will the workers of iniquity never learn?…

and devastated his homeland.
The promised land, including Jerusalem and its temple, lay ruined under Babylonian judgment. The devastation wounded Israel’s national and spiritual life, since the land and sanctuary testified to God’s covenant presence among His people. Even amid desolation, the prayer assumes that the Lord has not abandoned His inheritance and can restore what judgment has laid waste.

Psalm 79:8

Do not hold past sins against us;
Israel confessed that present distress flowed from generations of covenant unfaithfulness. The Law had warned that persistent rebellion would bring judgment, yet it also promised mercy when the people confessed their sin and returned to the Lord (Leviticus 26:40–42). The appeal rests not on Israel’s innocence but on God’s willingness to forgive. David voiced the same dependence upon mercy in Psalm 51:1–2. Full forgiveness is ultimately secured through Jesus Christ, in whom believers have redemption through His blood (Ephesians 1:7).

Psa 130:3-4 If You, O LORD, kept track of iniquities,…

Isa 43:25 I, yes I, am He who blots out your transgressions…

Jer 31:34 No longer will each man teach his neighbor or his brother…

let Your compassion come quickly,
The devastated people needed God’s intervention without delay. Jerusalem’s ruin and the humiliation of exile revealed the severe consequences of sin, but they did not cancel God’s covenant compassion. The Lord had declared Himself “compassionate and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in loving devotion and truth” (Exodus 34:6). His mercy exceeds the judgment His people deserve and gives hope to those who seek Him in repentance.

for we are brought low.
National disaster had reduced Israel to weakness, grief, and disgrace. Such humiliation exposed the emptiness of self-reliance and called the people to humble themselves before God. The Lord dwells with the contrite and lowly and restores those who turn to Him (Isaiah 57:15). Jesus likewise blesses the poor in spirit, who recognize their need for God’s kingdom (Matthew 5:3).

Psalm 79:9

Help us, O God of our salvation,
Israel appeals to the Lord as the only Savior able to rescue His covenant people from judgment, enemies, and the consequences of their rebellion. The God who delivered Israel from Egypt remains their hope when human strength and political alliances fail (Exodus 15:2; Isaiah 12:2).

for the glory of Your name;
Deliverance would reveal the Lord’s holiness, power, and faithfulness before the nations that had mocked His people and defiled His sanctuary. God’s concern for His name is inseparable from His commitment to fulfill His covenant promises (Ezekiel 20:9; 1 Samuel 12:22).

National calamity had exposed Israel’s guilt before God. The psalmist seeks not merely relief from suffering but cleansing and reconciliation. The sacrifices appointed under the Law pointed forward to Christ, whose blood secured eternal redemption and complete forgiveness for all who believe (Leviticus 16; Hebrews 9:12).

Psalm 79:10

Why should the nations ask, “Where is their God?”
Israel’s suffering had emboldened surrounding peoples to mock Yahweh as powerless or absent. The psalmist appeals to God’s covenant honor, knowing that the disgrace of His people was used to dishonor His name. Similar taunts arose during Israel’s oppression and exile, and Hezekiah faced the same challenge when Assyria claimed that no god could rescue a nation from its power.

Before our eyes,
The plea seeks a public act of God that His afflicted people can witness. Israel had seen the LORD judge Egypt, divide the sea, and defend His people in the wilderness. Such visible intervention would strengthen the faithful and silence those who treated Israel’s calamity as evidence against Yahweh.

make known among the nations Your vengeance
God’s vengeance is His righteous judgment against those who defy Him and cruelly attack His people. The request is not for personal retaliation but for the Lord to uphold justice, vindicate His holiness, and reveal His rule over every nation. God’s judgments against hostile powers repeatedly demonstrated that He alone is God.

for the bloodshed of Your servants.
The servants of God had been killed in Jerusalem during the devastation described in the psalm. Their blood was not forgotten before the righteous Judge. The cry anticipates the appeal of the martyrs in Revelation, who ask when God will judge those who shed innocent blood. The Lord sees the suffering of His people and will bring final justice in His appointed time.

Psalm 79:11

May the groans of the captives reach You;
Israel’s suffering under hostile nations rose before the Lord as a plea for mercy. The lament recalls Israel’s bondage in Egypt, when God heard the groaning of His people and acted according to His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Exodus 2:23–25). The psalmist asks God not to overlook those imprisoned, displaced, or crushed by the violence of Jerusalem’s enemies.

by the strength of Your arm
God’s arm represents His irresistible power to save and to judge. The Lord redeemed Israel from Egypt “with an outstretched arm” (Exodus 6:6), and the same power remained able to overthrow the nations that had devastated His inheritance. Human strength could not restore the captives; their hope rested in the God who rules armies, nations, and death itself.

The petition includes those facing execution or destruction at the hands of their captors. God had repeatedly shown Himself able to preserve His servants from death, as in the deliverance of Daniel and the protection of His people in Esther’s day. The cry also points beyond temporal rescue to the greater salvation accomplished by Christ, who bore death for sinners and grants eternal life to all who trust in Him.

Psalm 79:12

Pay back into the laps of our neighbors
The psalmist appeals to the Lord as Judge to return upon the surrounding nations the evil they inflicted on His covenant people. The lap, formed by the fold of an outer garment, represented a place where a person received what was measured out to him. God’s people are not called to seize vengeance for themselves; they entrust judgment to the Lord, who declares, “Vengeance is Mine; I will repay” (Deuteronomy 32:35; Romans 12:19).

sevenfold
A sevenfold repayment calls for a full measure of righteous judgment proportionate to the nations’ persistent rebellion, cruelty, and contempt. The number emphasizes completeness, not personal spite. Scripture presents God’s judgment as holy and exact, while His patience gives sinners opportunity to repent.

The nations’ scorn against Israel was ultimately directed against the God who chose, redeemed, and defended Israel. After Jerusalem’s devastation, the enemies of Judah mocked the Lord as though He were powerless to save His people. God’s honor is bound to His name, and He acts to vindicate His holiness before the nations. Jesus likewise identified Himself with those who belong to Him, telling Saul, “Why do you persecute Me?” (Acts 9:4).

Psalm 79:13

Then we Your people, the sheep of Your pasture,
Israel belonged to the Lord by covenant, redeemed from Egypt and sustained through the wilderness. The shepherd image portrays God’s attentive rule, provision, and protection over a dependent people. David later confessed the same care in Psalm 23, and Jesus identified Himself as the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (John 10:11).

Deliverance from enemies moved the faithful remnant to gratitude rather than self-exaltation. God’s mercy toward His people was not temporary, and their worship anticipated the unending praise of the redeemed before His throne.
