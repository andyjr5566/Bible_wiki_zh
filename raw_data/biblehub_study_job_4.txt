Eliphaz begins by recalling Job’s former ministry of counsel. Job had used wise and compassionate speech to restore people weakened by grief, fear, or failure. In a world where instruction was commonly passed on through spoken counsel, such words served as vital support within the community. Proverbs 12:25 likewise teaches that a good word can lift a burdened heart. Job’s past conduct showed that he understood how truth, spoken with mercy, could help others regain their footing.

Eliphaz’s reminder carries painful irony, since Job now needs the comfort he once gave. The Lord remains the true source of strength for the weak, and believers are called to reflect His sustaining grace by bearing one another’s burdens.

Job 4:6

Is your reverence not your confidence,
Eliphaz appeals to Job’s known fear of God, which the Lord Himself had affirmed in describing Job as blameless, upright, and one who feared God (Job 1:1, 8). Reverence for the Lord rightly produces confidence because God is faithful, wise, and sovereign over every trial. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), and those who delight in Him need not be ruled by fearful news (Psalm 112:1, 7). Yet Eliphaz wrongly treats Job’s suffering as evidence that his reverence must be defective.

Pro 14:26 He who fears the LORD is secure in confidence…

Isa 50:10 Who among you fears the LORD and obeys the voice…

and the uprightness of your ways your hope?
Job’s conduct had displayed genuine integrity, not sinless perfection or confidence in his own merit. Eliphaz assumes that the upright will always prosper outwardly and that severe affliction must reveal hidden wickedness. The book of Job exposes the error of that conclusion: righteous people may suffer deeply under God’s sovereign purposes. Uprightness remains the proper path of hope, but hope finally rests not in human righteousness, but in the Lord who vindicates His servants and, through Christ, gives peace with God.

Job 4:7

Eliphaz urges Job to examine his suffering through the lens of traditional wisdom. His appeal carries the force of a solemn challenge: Job should reconsider whether hidden sin has brought God’s discipline. Scripture commends honest self-examination, yet Eliphaz wrongly assumes that Job’s affliction can be explained by personal wrongdoing.

Who, being innocent, has ever perished?
Eliphaz argues from the usual pattern of God’s moral government, in which evil brings judgment and righteousness receives blessing. Job’s calamity exposes the limits of applying that pattern mechanically. Job was blameless and upright before God (Job 1:1), yet he suffered severely. The truly innocent One, Jesus Christ, also suffered and died—not for His own sins, but as the sacrifice for sinners.

Eliphaz treats the preservation of the righteous as an absolute rule for earthly life. God does care for His people, but He may permit suffering, persecution, and death without abandoning them. The resurrection of Christ confirms that destruction does not have the final word over those who belong to God, and it assures the righteous of final vindication before Him.

Eliphaz appeals to the lessons he believes life has taught him. Personal experience must remain subject to God’s fuller revelation and purposes.

God does not overlook evil, and unrepentant wrongdoing will ultimately meet His judgment. Proverbs 22:8 similarly teaches that one who sows injustice reaps calamity. Eliphaz rightly states a general moral reality, but he wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves Job has secretly sown sin. Job’s trials arise not from hidden wickedness but from God’s sovereign purpose, proving that earthly suffering cannot always be read as a direct measure of personal guilt.

Isaiah 40:7 likewise portrays the breath of the Lord causing the grass to wither, reminding humanity of its frailty before the Creator. Eliphaz’s words anticipate the final judgment in which God will judge all people in righteousness, yet Job’s suffering shows that divine discipline, testing, and mystery cannot be reduced to a simple equation of suffering with guilt.

The message he received contained truth about mankind’s uncleanness before God, but Eliphaz applied that truth without understanding God’s purpose in Job’s trial.

Job 4:17

‘Can a mortal be more righteous than God,
Eliphaz reports words heard in a night vision, affirming God’s absolute holiness and justice. No human being can stand above the Creator or judge Him as though His ways were morally deficient. Isaiah 64:6 and Romans 3:23 likewise testify that all people are stained by sin and fall short of God’s glory. Yet Eliphaz wrongly applies this true principle to Job’s suffering, assuming that severe affliction must reveal secret personal wickedness. Job was blameless in conduct, though not sinless, and his trials were not proof that God had abandoned justice.

God alone possesses uncreated and perfect purity; every human life derives from Him and remains accountable to Him. The question exposes the folly of self-righteousness and prepares the way for the need of a righteous mediator. Jesus Christ shared fully in human temptation yet remained without sin, making Him able to bear the sins of His people and clothe them in His righteousness before God.
