The Ministry of the Twelve

Luke 9:1

Then Jesus called the Twelve together
Jesus gathered the apostles whom He had chosen to be with Him and to share in His work. Their number pointed to the twelve tribes of Israel and testified that God was fulfilling His covenant purposes through the Messiah, forming a people centered on faith in Him. The gathering marked a decisive step in their preparation to represent Christ publicly.

Power equipped them to perform works beyond human ability, while authority gave them the right to act as His appointed representatives. Their mission would not rest on personal influence or religious training, but on the authority of the Son of God.

Jesus commissioned the Twelve as His authorized representatives, extending His ministry beyond His immediate presence. Having given them power and authority over demons and diseases, He entrusted them with a mission that reflected God’s pattern of sending servants to speak and act in His name, as with Moses and the prophets. Their mission anticipated the later worldwide commission to make disciples of all nations.

The Twelve announced that God’s promised reign had drawn near in the person and work of Jesus Christ. Israel’s hope for divine deliverance, foretold by prophets such as Isaiah and Daniel, was being fulfilled through the Messiah. Their message called people to recognize God’s rightful rule, repent, and receive the salvation He provides.

The miracles fulfilled expectations associated with the Messiah’s coming, including the healing foretold in Isaiah 35:5–6. Physical restoration pointed beyond itself to the deeper deliverance Jesus brings from sin, Satan, and death.

Luke 9:3

Jesus sent the Twelve to proclaim the kingdom of God and heal the sick with urgency and undivided purpose. Travelers commonly carried provisions for food, protection, and lodging, but the disciples were to depend on the Father who had commissioned them. Their needs would be met through God’s care and through the hospitality of receptive households. Israel had learned in the wilderness that God could provide daily bread, and the disciples were now called to live by that same faith while serving as heralds of the Messiah.

A staff could aid a traveler over rough ground and offer some defense against animals or thieves. For this particular mission, Jesus required the disciples to go without customary safeguards so that their security would rest in God rather than in human preparation. Mark records that they were permitted to carry a staff, while Luke emphasizes that they were not to obtain additional travel equipment. The differing details reflect the focused instructions for their immediate assignment, not a contradiction in the Lord’s command.

God had fed Israel with manna in the desert, and He remained fully able to sustain those sent in His name. The disciples’ physical dependence corresponded to their spiritual calling: doing the will of God was to govern their priorities above comfort and self-sufficiency.

A household that welcomed the apostles also received their testimony concerning the kingdom of God.

The disciples were not to retaliate or force acceptance, but to leave the rejection before God.

Herod Tries to See Jesus

Luke 9:7

When Herod the tetrarch heard about all that was happening,
Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea under Roman authority. He was the son of Herod the Great, whose attempt to destroy the infant Jesus stained the beginning of Jesus’ earthly life with royal hostility. Reports of Jesus’ healings, authority over demons, and proclamation of God’s kingdom spread throughout Galilee, reaching even Herod’s court. Jesus’ ministry could not remain a private religious movement; it confronted the conscience of rulers and exposed the inadequacy of earthly power before God’s saving work.

Mat 14:1 At that time Herod the tetrarch heard the reports…

Mrk 6:14 Now King Herod heard about this, for Jesus’ name…

he was perplexed.
Herod’s confusion was intensified by guilt. He had ordered John the Baptist’s execution after rashly binding himself by an oath before his guests, though he knew John was righteous and feared the people’s response. The works of Jesus awakened fears Herod had tried to silence by killing John. A ruler could imprison and behead a prophet, but he could not restrain the word of God or prevent God from raising up further testimony.

Mrk 6:20 Herod feared John and protected him, …

For some were saying that John had risen from the dead,
John’s powerful ministry had left a lasting impression upon the nation. Many recognized him as God’s prophet who called Israel to repentance and prepared the way for the Messiah. The rumor that John had risen reflected both the people’s awareness of God’s power over death and their effort to explain Jesus’ extraordinary works. Herod would later insist that Jesus was John raised from the dead, revealing how deeply his sin troubled him. Yet Jesus was not John returned; He was the promised Christ, greater than John, whose own resurrection would conquer death and confirm the kingdom John had announced.

Elijah had been taken alive into heaven, and Malachi foretold his return before the day of the Lord (2 Kings 2:11; Malachi 4:5–6). Many in Israel therefore expected Elijah to reappear in connection with the coming Messiah. John the Baptist came “in the spirit and power of Elijah,” calling Israel to repentance and preparing the way for Christ (Luke 1:17). The crowds recognized that Jesus’ ministry carried marks of God’s promised visitation, though they had not yet understood that He was the Messiah whom Elijah’s ministry was sent to announce.

Jesus’ miracles, authority, and proclamation of God’s kingdom recalled the great prophets through whom God had spoken to Israel. Some concluded that one of those prophets had returned from death, perhaps Moses, Jeremiah, or another servant of God. Their response showed that they recognized Jesus as more than an ordinary teacher, yet they still placed Him within the category of a prophet. Jesus was indeed the Prophet like Moses promised in Deuteronomy 18:15, but He was also greater than every prophet: the eternal Son of God, the One to whom all the prophets bore witness.

Although Herod feared John and recognized him as a righteous man, he yielded to Herodias’s hatred and to the pressure of a reckless oath made before his guests (Mark 6:17–28). His words reveal a guilty conscience. Reports of Jesus’ mighty works stirred the memory of the prophet he had silenced.

Jesus’ ministry had become known throughout Galilee, reaching even the palace of the ruler who had rejected God’s messenger.

The Feeding of the Five Thousand

Luke 9:10

The Twelve had gone out under Jesus’ authority to proclaim the kingdom of God and heal the sick (Luke 9:1–6). Their report demonstrated faithful accountability to the One who had sent them. Jesus did not delegate ministry and remain distant from its results; He received His disciples, heard their experiences, and continued to form them through service. Their mission anticipated the later commission to make disciples among all nations, yet its power and purpose already rested wholly in Christ.

Ministry requires dependence upon God rather than continual activity, and the disciples needed time with their Master after representing Him among the villages. Jesus Himself often withdrew for prayer, showing that solitude before God strengthens faithful service. The private setting also prepared the apostles to understand more deeply what they had witnessed and done in His name.

He withdrew to a town called Bethsaida.
Bethsaida lay near the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee and was associated with Peter, Andrew, and Philip (John 1:44). Its name, meaning “house of fishing,” reflects the region’s livelihood and the ordinary setting from which Jesus called several of His disciples. Bethsaida later became the location of Jesus’ healing of a blind man (Mark 8:22–26). Even in seeking rest there, Jesus remained accessible to the needy crowds, revealing that His compassion did not diminish when His disciples required renewal.
