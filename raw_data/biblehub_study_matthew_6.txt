Giving to the Needy

Matthew 6:1

“Be careful not to perform your righteous acts before men to be seen by them.
Jesus addresses the danger of practicing obedience to God for public admiration. Almsgiving, prayer, and fasting were recognized duties of Jewish devotion, yet even good works could be corrupted by a desire for status. Religious leaders such as the Pharisees were often honored for visible rigor, but Jesus exposes the heart-level issue beneath outward piety. God sees motives that human observers cannot see, looking beyond appearance to the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Isaiah likewise condemned worship that honored God outwardly while remaining far from Him inwardly (Isaiah 29:13). Righteous acts are to arise from love for God and concern for others, not from a desire to build a reputation.

Mat 23:5 All their deeds are done for men to see.…

If you do, you will have no reward from your Father in heaven.
Those who seek recognition from people receive the reward they desired: human notice and praise. Such temporary approval cannot compare with the reward given by the Father, who knows every hidden act of faithful obedience. Jesus does not reject the hope of heavenly reward; He redirects it away from self-exaltation and toward God’s approval. The Father’s reward reflects His gracious care for His children and His perfect judgment of their lives. Believers are therefore called to seek treasures in heaven rather than earthly acclaim (Matthew 6:19–20), trusting that God rewards those who earnestly seek Him (Hebrews 11:6).

God commanded Israel to be openhanded toward the needy (Deuteronomy 15:11), and the early church shared its resources so that believers were cared for (Acts 2:44–45). Generosity is an act of obedience that reflects God’s compassion.

Matthew 6:4

so that your giving may be in secret.
Jesus directs His disciples away from charitable acts designed to win public admiration. In first-century Judea, almsgiving was widely regarded as a righteous duty, yet even a good deed could be corrupted by the desire for status. Hidden generosity protects the giver’s heart from seeking human praise and directs attention to the needs of the poor. God looks beyond visible actions to the motives that govern them, as He did when He chose David by examining the heart rather than outward appearance (1 Samuel 16:7). Giving in secret reflects love for one’s neighbor and trust that God receives what no one else sees. Proverbs 19:17 likewise teaches that kindness to the poor is lending to the Lord.

The believer may therefore serve faithfully without needing public acknowledgment, knowing that the Father sees both the gift and the love behind it.

Jesus teaches believers to confess their weakness and depend on the Father’s preserving grace. God never entices anyone to sin (James 1:13), yet He may permit testing that reveals faith and calls His people to obedience. Israel’s wilderness trials exposed unbelief and taught the necessity of trusting God for daily bread, protection, and direction (Exodus 16–17). Adam and Eve fell when they distrusted God’s word in the garden (Genesis 3), while Jesus remained faithful when Satan tempted Him in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1–11). God’s children therefore ask not to be placed in circumstances where their sinful desires and the enemy’s attacks would overwhelm them, but to be guarded in every trial.

Proper Fasting

Matthew 6:16

When you fast,
Jesus speaks of fasting as an expected practice for His disciples, not merely an occasional religious custom. Scripture connects fasting with prayer, repentance, grief, earnest intercession, and seeking God’s direction. Fasting does not earn God’s favor; it humbles the believer and directs attention from physical appetite to dependence upon the Lord.

Religious hypocrites used spiritual practices to cultivate a reputation for holiness. Their concern was not communion with God but the impression they created before others. Jesus calls His followers to integrity, in which outward conduct agrees with a heart devoted to the Father.

Abstaining from food is meant to humble the heart and turn attention from physical appetite to dependence upon God.

Jesus does not promise luxury or freedom from hardship, but He assures His children that their needs are known to God and will not be neglected. Trust in the Father’s provision frees believers from anxious pursuit of material security and enables faithful service to His kingdom.

Matthew 6:34

Therefore do not worry about tomorrow,
Jesus concludes His teaching on God’s faithful care by forbidding anxious preoccupation with the future. The command rests on the Father’s knowledge of His children’s needs, demonstrated in His provision for birds, flowers, and human life (Matthew 6:25–32). In a land marked by poverty, unstable harvests, taxation, and Roman rule, such assurance addressed genuine material fears. Trusting God does not deny wise planning or faithful labor; it rejects the unbelief that assumes tomorrow rests outside His providence. Believers are to seek God’s kingdom and righteousness first, confident that the Father rules over what lies ahead.

for tomorrow will worry about itself.
Tomorrow is presented as carrying its own burdens when it arrives. God ordinarily gives grace, strength, and provision for the day at hand rather than supplying strength in advance for troubles that may never come. Israel learned this dependence through manna in the wilderness, receiving what was needed each day and trusting God for the next (Exodus 16:4–5). The future remains under God’s sovereign care, though its details are hidden from human sight.

Today has enough trouble of its own.
Jesus acknowledges the reality of suffering in a fallen world. Faith does not promise freedom from hardship, but it keeps present burdens from being multiplied by imagined future distress. Each day calls God’s people to depend on Him, obey Him, and receive His mercy anew. The Lord’s care is sufficient for daily trials, and His people may rejoice in the day He has made while entrusting every tomorrow to Him (Psalm 118:24).
