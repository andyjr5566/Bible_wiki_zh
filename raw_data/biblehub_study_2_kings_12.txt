2 Kings 12:16

The money from the guilt offerings and sin offerings
In the context of ancient Israel, guilt offerings and sin offerings were part of the sacrificial system established in the Mosaic Law (Leviticus 5-7). These offerings were made to atone for specific sins and unintentional transgressions. The guilt offering, or “asham,” was typically required when someone had committed a sin against the holy things of the LORD or had wronged another person. The sin offering, or “chatat,” was for purification from sin and was necessary for maintaining a right relationship with God. These offerings were integral to the spiritual and communal life of Israel, emphasizing the seriousness of sin and the need for atonement.

2 Kings 12:19

As for the rest of the acts of Joash,
Joash reigned over Judah for forty years, beginning as a child under the protection and instruction of Jehoiada the priest. His early years showed concern for the worship of the Lord and for the condition of the temple. The account in Kings records only selected events from his reign, while 2 Chronicles adds that, after Jehoiada’s death, Joash turned from the Lord, tolerated idolatry, and ordered the death of Zechariah, Jehoiada’s son, when he rebuked the nation’s unfaithfulness.

Joash’s greatest recorded achievement was the repair of the temple, which had been neglected and damaged during the influence of Athaliah and the worship of Baal. Funds gathered from the people were placed in a chest and used responsibly for the workmen, restoring the house devoted to the name of the Lord. Yet outward reform could not preserve Joash when his heart departed from God. His later rebellion brought judgment upon Judah and ended in conspiracy and assassination.

The royal annals of Judah preserved fuller records of each king’s administration, military affairs, building projects, and national events. That source is now lost and is distinct from the biblical books of Chronicles. Its mention shows that the biblical account deliberately records what reveals God’s covenant dealings with His people, including both Joash’s early faithfulness and his tragic decline.

His servants Jozabad son of Shimeath and Jehozabad son of Shomer struck him down, and he died.
Joash was murdered by members of his own household, exposing the deep disorder that followed his rejection of the Lord. After Jehoiada’s death, Joash abandoned faithful worship, listened to corrupt leaders, and approved the murder of Zechariah, Jehoiada’s son, who called the nation back to God (2 Chronicles 24:17–22). Chronicles identifies the assassins with slightly different forms of their names and notes their foreign maternal backgrounds, underscoring the fractured condition of the kingdom.
