Their role often involved monitoring religious practices to ensure compliance with Jewish law. The context here is that Jesus and His disciples were walking through grainfields on the Sabbath, and the disciples began to pick heads of grain to eat. The Pharisees, observing this, were quick to challenge Jesus, reflecting their ongoing scrutiny of His ministry.

The Sign of Jonah

Matthew 12:38

Then some of the scribes and Pharisees said to Him,
Scribes were trained interpreters of the Law and guardians of the written Scriptures, while Pharisees were influential lay leaders devoted to Israel’s religious traditions and ceremonial purity. Many among them had already witnessed Jesus’ healings, exorcisms, and authority over disease and nature. Their opposition had intensified because Jesus exposed hypocrisy, forgave sins, and exercised authority they believed belonged to their institutions. The approach came after they had accused Him of casting out demons by Satan’s power, revealing that their demand did not arise from a lack of evidence but from resistance to the evidence already given.

The respectful title concealed a testing heart. Jesus was widely recognized as a teacher, yet He taught with an authority greater than that of the established rabbis, declaring the true meaning of God’s Law and calling people to repentance and faith. The leaders addressed Him courteously while refusing to submit to the truth He taught.

They sought a spectacular, undeniable display from heaven that would meet their own conditions. Jesus had already given abundant signs of God’s kingdom through mercy, healing, deliverance, and fulfilled prophecy. Their request resembled Israel’s repeated testing of God in the wilderness, where visible acts of divine power did not produce lasting faith. Jesus would point them to the sign of Jonah: His death, burial, and resurrection on the third day. The resurrection would be God’s decisive testimony to Christ’s identity, leaving unbelief without excuse.

Matthew 12:40

For as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish,
Jonah’s confinement followed his flight from God’s command to preach in Nineveh. God preserved Jonah in the fish and brought him forth alive, displaying both judgment against disobedience and mercy toward His servant. The three days and three nights designate a period embracing portions of three calendar days, according to Jewish reckoning. Jonah’s deliverance became the sign that authenticated his warning to Nineveh and foreshadowed a greater deliverance yet to come.

Jon 1:17 Now the LORD had appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah…

Jon 2:1 From inside the fish, Jonah prayed to the LORD his God,

Jon 2:2 “In my distress I called to the LORD, and He answered me.”…

so the Son of Man will be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.
Jesus identified Himself as the Son of Man, the promised Messiah who would suffer, die, and reign with divine authority. His death was real, His body was placed in Joseph’s tomb, and He remained under the power of death until His resurrection on the third day. From His burial before the Sabbath to His resurrection on the first day of the week, parts of Friday, Saturday, and Sunday were counted as three days. Jonah emerged from the fish by God’s power; Christ rose from the grave in victory over sin and death. The sign of Jonah therefore points to the central truth of the gospel: the crucified and buried Son of Man was raised, bringing salvation to all who believe.

Yet when Jonah announced God’s coming judgment, its people humbled themselves, fasted, and turned from their evil ways. Their repentance did not erase the reality of their former wickedness, but it demonstrated submission to God’s warning. At the final judgment, their response to the limited light given through Jonah will expose the guilt of those who rejected far greater light in the presence of Christ.

Jesus’ contemporaries heard the truth from the Messiah Himself, witnessed His works, and still resisted Him.
