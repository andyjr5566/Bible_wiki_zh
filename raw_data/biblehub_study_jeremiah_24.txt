The Good and Bad Figs

Jeremiah 24:1

After Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon had carried away Jeconiah son of Jehoiakim king of Judah,
Nebuchadnezzar II deported Jeconiah, also called Jehoiachin, in 597 BC after Jerusalem surrendered to Babylon (2 Kings 24:10–16). The young king’s removal fulfilled the Lord’s judgment on Judah’s persistent rebellion and marked a decisive stage in the Babylonian exile. Though Jeconiah was taken captive, the Davidic line was preserved, and his descendants later appear in the genealogy of Christ (Matthew 1:11–12).

Babylon removed Judah’s governing leaders, soldiers, and skilled laborers in order to cripple the nation’s strength and prevent future revolt. What appeared to be imperial strategy was also the outworking of God’s warning that Jerusalem’s pride, security, and resources would be brought low because of sin.

The vision was set before the temple, the visible center of Judah’s covenant worship, even as the nation faced judgment. The two baskets would portray two distinct outcomes under God’s sovereign hand: the captives sent to Babylon would be regarded as good figs, preserved for restoration, while those who remained in Judah in hardened rebellion would be like bad figs, fit only for judgment.

Jeremiah 24:3

“Jeremiah,” the LORD asked, “what do you see?”
The LORD addressed Jeremiah personally and directed his attention to a vision that revealed His purposes for Judah. God’s prophets were called not only to speak His word but also to perceive His judgments rightly. As with Isaiah, Ezekiel, and Zechariah, visible images communicated realities that lay behind current events.

Jeremiah saw two baskets of figs set before the temple after Nebuchadnezzar had carried King Jeconiah, the royal family, skilled craftsmen, and leading citizens of Judah into Babylon in 597 BC. The vision interpreted that first deportation in a way that contradicted common expectations. Those taken from Jerusalem appeared to be abandoned, yet God had not forgotten them.

The good figs represented the Judean exiles whom God would watch over in Babylon. Though their captivity was a judgment for sin, it also removed many from the final destruction soon to fall on Jerusalem. Among the exiles were faithful servants such as Ezekiel and Daniel, and from this disciplined remnant God would preserve His covenant people and eventually restore them to the land.

The bad figs represented Zedekiah, his officials, and those remaining in Jerusalem who resisted God’s warning through Jeremiah. Many considered themselves fortunate to have escaped exile, but their continued rebellion, idolatry, and reliance on foreign alliances showed that they had not humbled themselves under God’s discipline.

Worthless figs could not be used or preserved; they were fit only to be discarded. So the inhabitants of Jerusalem who persisted in refusing God’s word would face sword, famine, pestilence, and exile. The vision warned that outward nearness to the temple and land could not protect a people whose hearts remained hardened against the LORD.

Jeremiah 24:4

Jeremiah received God’s interpretation of the vision of the two baskets of figs. The message did not arise from political observation or personal opinion but from the Lord who governed Judah’s exile and future. God had already allowed King Jeconiah, the royal court, skilled workers, and many residents of Jerusalem to be carried to Babylon in 597 BC. Though the exile appeared to be a disaster, the Lord would identify those taken away as the good figs and preserve them for restoration. The people remaining in Jerusalem, who trusted in their circumstances and resisted God’s discipline, would face judgment.

Jeremiah 24:6

God directs His favorable and protective attention toward the Judean exiles carried to Babylon in 597 B.C. Though they had endured the discipline of exile, they had not been abandoned. The Lord who watches over His people neither slumbers nor sleeps, and His care remains active even when His people are far from the land of promise. His watchful oversight rests on covenant mercy and prepares them for repentance, preservation, and restoration.

Cyrus king of Persia issued the decree permitting their return after Babylon fell, fulfilling God’s word through Jeremiah and other prophets. The return to the land also served God’s larger purpose of preserving the people through whom the Messiah would come.

The Lord who had uprooted Judah through judgment would also restore what sin had ruined. Jerusalem, its walls, its temple worship, and its community would be rebuilt under His providence. God’s restoring work reached beyond buildings and national life, directing His people toward the new covenant in which He would write His law upon their hearts and forgive their sin.

The spoiled figs portray Judah’s hardened rebellion and its utter unfitness for blessing. God’s judgment was not arbitrary; persistent idolatry, injustice, and rejection of His word had brought the nation to ruin. The image recalls Israel’s failure to bear fruit worthy of covenant faithfulness.
