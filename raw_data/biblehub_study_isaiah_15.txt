The Burden against Moab

Isaiah 15:1

This is the burden against Moab:
God announces judgment against Moab, Lot’s descendants east of the Dead Sea. Though related to Israel, Moab repeatedly opposed God’s people and trusted in idols such as Chemosh. Isaiah’s oracle reveals that the Lord rules over every nation and holds them accountable for pride, violence, and false worship.

Isaiah 15:2

Dibon goes up to its temple to weep at its high places.
Dibon, a leading Moabite city north of the Arnon River, turns to its sanctuaries in national disaster. The high places associated with Chemosh and other Moabite deities cannot provide rescue when the Lord brings judgment. Tears offered before idols reveal Moab’s desperation and the emptiness of worship directed away from the living God. Jeremiah later echoes Moab’s mourning and foretells judgment upon Chemosh and his priests (Jeremiah 48:7, 35, 46).

Jer 48:22 upon Dibon, Nebo, and Beth-diblathaim,

Moab wails over Nebo, as well as over Medeba.
Nebo and Medeba stood on the elevated plateau of northern Moab and represented important centers of Moabite life. Nebo lay near Mount Nebo, from which Moses viewed the Promised Land before his death (Deuteronomy 34:1–4). The lament spreading from city to city shows that the calamity has overwhelmed the whole land, not merely one defeated fortress.

The nation that had exalted itself in pride is reduced to mourning, demonstrating that no people can stand securely against the judgment of God.

Isaiah 15:3

In its streets they wear sackcloth;
Moab’s people publicly adopt the coarse garment associated with mourning, humiliation, and desperate grief. Sackcloth marked calamity that could not be concealed, as when Jacob mourned Joseph (Genesis 37:34) and when Nineveh responded to Jonah’s warning (Jonah 3:5–6). The judgment announced against Moab reaches every level of society and leaves the nation stripped of its former security and pride.

Jer 6:26 O daughter of my people, dress yourselves in sackcloth…

on the rooftops and in the public squares
Flat rooftops provided visible places for lament, while public squares were the centers of trade, civic life, and community gathering. Grief fills both household spaces and the places where Moab’s people ordinarily conducted daily business. Jeremiah likewise records that lamentation would be heard on Moab’s rooftops and in its squares when the Lord shattered the nation like a worthless vessel (Jeremiah 48:38).

Jer 48:38 On all the rooftops of Moab and in the public squares, everyone is mourning;…

they all wail, falling down weeping.
The united crying portrays a people overwhelmed by invasion, destruction, and loss. Moab’s proud cities, fortified places, and wealth cannot protect its inhabitants from the judgment of God. Their tears reveal the terror of deserved devastation, yet they also show the human need for repentance before the Lord, who alone is able to humble nations and show mercy to the contrite.

Heshbon, once the capital of Sihon the Amorite and later assigned to Reuben, stood near Elealeh on Moab’s northern plateau. Cities associated with former conquest and settlement now become centers of mourning as Moab faces destruction.

Isaiah 15:5

My heart cries out over Moab;
Isaiah’s lament reveals sorrow within the announcement of God’s judgment. Moab descended from Lot through his son Moab (Genesis 19:37) and had repeatedly opposed Israel, yet its coming ruin was not treated with cold indifference. God’s holiness requires judgment, but He takes no pleasure in destruction. Isaiah’s grief anticipates Christ’s tears over Jerusalem as He pronounced judgment upon a city that had rejected its King (Luke 19:41–44).

her fugitives flee as far as Zoar,
Moab’s people would be driven from their homes in panic, fleeing southward toward Zoar near the region where Lot once escaped Sodom’s overthrow (Genesis 19:22–23). A place associated with former refuge becomes the limit of Moab’s desperate flight. The nation’s fortified cities, wealth, and national pride could not shield it when God’s appointed judgment came.

as far as Eglath-shelishiyah.
The fugitives’ flight extended across Moab’s territory, showing that devastation would not be confined to one city. Eglath-shelishiyah, meaning “the third Eglath,” marked part of the mournful path of survivors escaping the invading army. The judgment described in Isaiah 15 would leave Moab’s land filled with displaced people rather than secure inhabitants.

The steep road to Luhith would be climbed amid tears, turning a route of escape into a public procession of grief. Moab’s sorrow came from the loss of homes, cities, possessions, and loved ones. Human strength offers no lasting security when people exalt themselves against the Lord.

Cries of anguish would rise along the road to Horonaim as news of ruin spread through the land. Jeremiah later repeated this lament over Moab, confirming the seriousness and certainty of the judgment (Jeremiah 48:3–5). Yet Isaiah’s tears also show that divine warnings call sinners to recognize their need for mercy before destruction falls.

Isaiah 15:6

The waters of Nimrim are dried up,
Nimrim’s life-giving waters, likely in southern Moab near the Dead Sea, had become a sign of the land’s ruin. Springs and streams sustained fields, flocks, settlements, and travelers; their loss exposed the helplessness of Moab’s wealth and defenses before the Lord’s judgment. Jeremiah also includes Nimrim in his lament over Moab’s devastation (Jeremiah 48:34).

The repeated picture of vanished growth completes the scene of total desolation. Human strength, fertile land, and national prosperity cannot endure when a people exalts itself against the Lord; yet God’s judgments also reveal the need for humble repentance and the life He alone can restore.

Isaiah 15:9

Dimon, likely Dibon in Moab, stands under the consequences of war and divine judgment. Waters that should sustain life are stained by violent death, portraying the collapse of Moab’s security, prosperity, and strength. The devastation follows the lament throughout Isaiah 15, where Moab’s cities, sanctuaries, and refugees are overwhelmed.
