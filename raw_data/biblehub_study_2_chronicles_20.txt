So Jehoshaphat reigned over Judah.
Jehoshaphat succeeded Asa as Judah’s fourth king and ruled during a period when the divided kingdoms followed increasingly different spiritual paths. While Israel repeatedly embraced idolatry under the house of Ahab, Jehoshaphat sought to lead Judah in covenant faithfulness. The summary follows the Lord’s miraculous deliverance of Judah from its enemies, showing that the kingdom’s preservation rested ultimately on God’s power rather than military strength.

Local shrines on hills and other elevated places continued to shape the religious life of Judah. Although some had been used in the worship of the Lord, God had appointed worship around His altar and sanctuary rather than according to human preference. Such places easily became centers of mixed worship, where devotion to the Lord was joined to practices borrowed from surrounding nations. Jehoshaphat had opposed idolatry and strengthened instruction in God’s law, yet his reforms did not produce lasting, complete obedience throughout the land.

Ahaziah, the son of Ahab and Jezebel, ruled the northern kingdom in the same idolatrous pattern as his parents. The alliance concerned a commercial fleet built at Ezion-geber for trade with Tarshish. Jehoshaphat had previously allied himself with Ahab in war; this later partnership showed the continuing danger of seeking security or prosperity through fellowship with rulers opposed to the Lord.

2 Chronicles 20:36

They agreed to make ships to go to Tarshish,
Jehoshaphat entered a commercial alliance with Ahaziah of Israel, whose wicked rule stood in contrast to Judah’s covenant calling. Tarshish was renowned as a distant center of maritime wealth, and the parallel account identifies Ophir as the intended destination (1 Kings 22:48). The project revealed Jehoshaphat’s desire for prosperity but also his willingness to join himself again to a king whom God opposed, despite the earlier rebuke for aiding Ahab (2 Chronicles 19:2).

Ezion-geber stood at the northern end of the Gulf of Aqaba and provided access to Red Sea trade routes. Solomon had used this port for voyages connected with Ophir (1 Kings 9:26–28). Jehoshaphat sought to revive that prosperity, but the enterprise rested on an ungodly partnership rather than dependence upon the Lord. God sent Eliezer to declare judgment, and the ships were wrecked before they could sail (2 Chronicles 20:37).
