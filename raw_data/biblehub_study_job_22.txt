Eliphaz: Can a Man Be of Use to God?

Job 22:1

Then Eliphaz the Temanite replied:
Eliphaz begins his third recorded speech to Job, speaking from the reputation for wisdom associated with Teman in Edom. Rather than being softened by Job’s suffering and protests of innocence, Eliphaz will press the belief that severe affliction must reveal serious hidden sin. His counsel contains true statements about God’s holiness and justice, yet he wrongly applies them to Job’s circumstances. The Lord had already declared Job blameless, and Job’s trials were not divine punishment for secret wickedness.

Job 22:2

“Can a man be of use to God?
Eliphaz rightly affirms God’s complete independence from human strength, service, or achievement. The Creator lacks nothing, for all things belong to Him and are sustained by His power (Psalm 50:12; Acts 17:24–25). Yet Eliphaz wrongly applies that truth to Job’s suffering, treating adversity as proof that Job’s life has no favor with God. Job’s faithfulness cannot place God in debt, but God freely delights in the obedience, prayers, and love of His people.

Can even a wise man benefit Him?
Human wisdom cannot increase the knowledge or counsel of the Lord, whose ways stand infinitely above human understanding (Isaiah 55:8–9). Wisdom is nevertheless valuable because it teaches people to fear the Lord and live rightly before Him. Eliphaz’s question exposes the limits of even the wisest human perspective, including his own. True wisdom is ultimately revealed in Christ, the wisdom of God, through whom believers learn to trust God when His purposes remain hidden.

Job 22:3

Does it delight the Almighty that you are righteous?
Eliphaz rightly recognizes that God is complete in Himself and does not need human virtue to supply what He lacks. Yet his argument wrongly treats Job’s suffering as proof that Job’s righteousness is false. The Lord truly delights in those who fear Him and walk uprightly (Psalm 147:11; Proverbs 11:20), not because He depends on them, but because righteousness reflects His own character and honors His will.

Does He profit if your ways are blameless?
God gains no advantage from human obedience, for all creation already belongs to Him. His blessings are therefore not wages paid because He needs mankind’s service, but gifts flowing from His grace, wisdom, and covenant love. Eliphaz uses God’s self-sufficiency to press Job toward an unfounded confession of hidden sin. Job’s blameless conduct could not put God in his debt, but neither did God’s freedom from human obligation mean that Job’s affliction was punishment for secret evil.

God does rebuke and discipline His people for sin, calling them to repentance and restoration. Yet Job’s losses were not divine punishment for hidden wickedness. Eliphaz assumes that God’s judgment against Job must expose personal guilt. His argument reflects the belief that prosperity proves righteousness and suffering proves sin. The book overturns that conclusion: God remains just, but human suffering cannot always be explained by personal wrongdoing.

Job 22:5

Is not your wickedness great?
Eliphaz assumes that Job’s severe losses, sickness, and grief prove the presence of serious hidden sin. His accusation follows the retributive view that prosperity reveals God’s favor and suffering exposes divine punishment. Yet the opening account has already identified Job as blameless and upright, and the reader knows that his trials arose from Satan’s challenge under God’s sovereign permission, not from great wickedness. Eliphaz therefore speaks confidently about matters he does not understand. Scripture repeatedly shows that suffering is not always a direct judgment on personal sin, as Joseph suffered unjustly before God raised him up, and Jesus, the sinless Servant, bore suffering for the sins of others.

Are not your iniquities endless?
Eliphaz escalates from suspicion to an unproven charge of continual guilt, treating Job’s affliction as sufficient evidence that his life must be marked by innumerable sins. Job does need no false confession; he needs faith to endure before the God whose wisdom exceeds human judgment. Human sin is indeed pervasive and deserving of judgment, but neither Eliphaz nor any other person may claim to read another’s heart from outward circumstances. God’s grace ultimately answers the problem of sin more fully than Eliphaz understood: where sin increased, grace abounded all the more through Christ.

Job 22:6

For you needlessly demanded security from your brothers
Eliphaz charged Job with exploiting fellow Israelites by taking pledges without just cause. The Law permitted a creditor to receive security for a debt, but it forbade harsh treatment of the poor and required compassion toward a needy borrower (Exodus 22:26–27; Deuteronomy 24:10–13). Calling the debtors “brothers” intensifies the accusation, since covenant members were to deal with one another in mercy rather than use poverty for personal gain. Eliphaz treated Job’s suffering as proof that such hidden sins must have existed, yet Job later denied withholding kindness from the needy or abusing the vulnerable (Job 31:16–23).

and deprived the naked of their clothing.
A garment often served as a poor person’s only covering by night and protection from the elements. Taking such clothing permanently would leave the borrower exposed and ashamed, directly opposing God’s command to return a pledged cloak before sunset. Scripture repeatedly joins true righteousness with care for the destitute, including clothing the naked and defending the afflicted (Isaiah 58:7; Ezekiel 18:7). Eliphaz’s charge portrays Job as merciless, though the unfolding account shows that Job’s trials cannot be explained by the simplistic claim that suffering always reveals secret oppression.

Job 22:7

You gave no water to the weary
In the dry lands of the ancient Near East, offering water to a traveler or exhausted person was a basic duty of hospitality and mercy. Eliphaz charged Job with heartless neglect toward the vulnerable, using such alleged conduct to explain Job’s suffering. Job later denied withholding help from the needy and described his home as open to strangers (Job 31:16–22, 31–32). The Lord’s rebuke of Eliphaz and the other friends confirms that they had wrongly judged Job’s character (Job 42:7).

and withheld food from the famished,
Feeding the hungry was required by love for one’s neighbor and reflected God’s own care for the poor. Israel’s laws protected the needy through provisions such as gleaning from fields and vineyards (Leviticus 19:9–10). Eliphaz portrayed Job as one who used his wealth selfishly, yet Job’s own testimony presents him as a man who shared his bread with the poor (Job 31:17). The accusation warns against indifference to physical need, while its place in Job’s account warns against assuming that suffering proves hidden sin.

Eliphaz portrays a society in which power secured property and influence, while the poor were allegedly denied help. Land was a primary source of wealth, stability, and inheritance, yet Israel’s laws required landowners to remember that the land ultimately belonged to the Lord and to protect the vulnerable (Leviticus 25:23; Deuteronomy 15:7–11).

Social honor could accompany wealth and position, but public esteem did not prove righteousness before God. Eliphaz uses these charges to accuse Job of favoring the influential while neglecting the needy. The book’s opening, however, has already established Job’s integrity, exposing the error of judging a man’s hidden guilt merely from his suffering.

Job 22:9

You sent widows away empty-handed,
Eliphaz accused Job of withholding help from women who had lost their husbands and lacked family protection or economic security. Israel’s law later commanded God’s people to defend widows, orphans, and foreigners rather than exploit their need (Deuteronomy 24:17–21). Yet Eliphaz offered no evidence for this charge, and the Lord ultimately rebuked Job’s friends for speaking wrongly about Him and His servant (Job 42:7). God Himself is the protector of widows and the judge of those who oppress them (Psalm 68:5).

and the strength of the fatherless was crushed.
Eliphaz further charged Job with actively destroying the security and prospects of children without fathers. Scripture repeatedly condemns those who take advantage of the fatherless and promises divine judgment against such cruelty (Exodus 22:22–24; Isaiah 1:17). The accusation shows how seriously God regards the treatment of the powerless. True righteousness reflects His compassion and justice, caring for those who cannot repay help and defending those without earthly advocates (James 1:27).

Job 22:10

Therefore snares surround you,
Eliphaz interprets Job’s crushing losses as evidence that hidden sin has trapped him in its consequences. Snares were hunting traps, often concealed until escape was impossible, and Scripture uses them to portray the deceit of the wicked, the danger of temptation, and the ruin sin brings (Psalm 91:3; Proverbs 22:5). Yet Eliphaz wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves personal guilt. The heavenly account reveals that Job’s trials came through Satan’s assault under God’s sovereign permission, not as punishment for secret wickedness.

and sudden peril terrifies you;
Job had indeed endured sudden disaster: raiders destroyed his servants, fire consumed his flocks, a storm killed his children, and disease afflicted his body. Eliphaz treats such terror as divine retribution, reflecting a common belief that calamity always exposed moral failure. Scripture recognizes that disaster can come unexpectedly (Proverbs 3:25; 1 Thessalonians 5:3), but it does not permit believers to judge suffering people as guilty before God. Job’s anguish points beyond himself to the innocent suffering of Christ, who faced betrayal, abandonment, and death without sin, accomplishing redemption through suffering rather than being condemned by it.

Eliphaz asks whether Job will continue in the long-established way of those who rebel against God. Unlike the “ancient paths” that lead to faithfulness in Jeremiah 6:16, this old way is a settled pattern of defiance and moral ruin.

Job 22:24

and consign your gold to the dust
Eliphaz calls Job to abandon reliance on earthly security as part of repentance and renewed submission to God. Gold, often buried or guarded as protection against hardship, was to be regarded as no more secure than dust. The counsel anticipates the Lord’s teaching that treasures on earth are vulnerable and temporary, while treasure in heaven endures (Matthew 6:19–21).

and the gold of Ophir to the stones of the ravines,
Ophir supplied gold of exceptional quality and was associated with Solomon’s great wealth (1 Kings 9:28; 10:11). Even such prized wealth was to be counted like common stones scattered in a ravine when compared with fellowship with God. Eliphaz’s larger appeal was that Job should seek the Almighty Himself as his treasure, for God alone provides lasting security and blessing (Job 22:25–26).

Job 22:25

then the Almighty will be your gold
Eliphaz urges Job to value God above the wealth he had lost. Though Eliphaz wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves hidden sin, his appeal points to a true reality: the Lord Himself is the believer’s highest treasure. Earthly riches can vanish, but God remains sufficient, faithful, and unchanging. Jesus likewise commanded His followers to store treasure in heaven, where loss and decay cannot reach (Matthew 6:19–21).

His counsel rightly identifies the Almighty as the believer’s true treasure, though Eliphaz wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves unconfessed wickedness. Genuine delight in God grows through trusting His character, obeying His word, and resting in His sovereign care.

Job 22:28

Your decisions will be carried out,
Eliphaz tells Job that restored fellowship with God would bring stability and success to his purposes. His counsel rightly calls for repentance, humility, and trust in the Lord, yet it wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves hidden sin. Scripture teaches that committing one’s work to the Lord brings established purposes (Proverbs 16:3), but it does not promise that every righteous plan will prosper without hardship. God’s wisdom directs the lives of His people even when His purposes remain hidden, as Job’s own trial demonstrates.

and light will shine on your ways.
God’s light represents His truth, favor, protection, and guidance amid uncertainty. The Lord’s word gives light for the path of those who fear Him (Psalm 119:105), and Christ is the true Light who leads His people out of spiritual darkness (John 8:12). Job would ultimately learn that divine guidance is not always shown through immediate relief from suffering, but through steadfast faith in the God whose ways are perfect.

Job 22:30

He will deliver even one who is not innocent,
Eliphaz envisions God showing mercy to the guilty through the intercession of a righteous servant. Although Eliphaz wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves hidden sin, his words reflect the biblical reality that God hears intercession and extends mercy to those who deserve judgment. Abraham pleaded for Sodom, and Moses stood before the Lord on behalf of Israel. God’s fullest deliverance comes through Jesus Christ, who died for sinners and continually intercedes for His people.

rescuing him through the cleanness of your hands.”
Clean hands portray a life marked by integrity before God. Eliphaz urges Job toward repentance, yet Job’s later vindication shows that he had not committed the wickedness charged against him. God eventually commands Job to pray for Eliphaz and his friends, and He accepts Job’s intercession on their behalf (Job 42:8–9). Job’s mediation anticipates Christ, whose perfect righteousness alone provides cleansing and salvation for guilty people.
