Psalm 78:2

I will open my mouth in parables;
Asaph introduces Israel’s history as wisdom instruction, using memorable comparisons and patterns from the past to expose the spiritual realities beneath Israel’s events. The generation that witnessed God’s works repeatedly responded with unbelief, rebellion, and forgetfulness, while God remained faithful to His covenant purposes. Jesus later used parables to reveal the mysteries of the kingdom to receptive hearers and to confront hardened hearts (Matthew 13:34–35).

The hidden matters are not secret doctrines apart from Scripture, but God’s purposes and lessons displayed throughout Israel’s history. The exodus, wilderness rebellions, judgments, and deliverances reveal truths about human sin, divine justice, mercy, and covenant faithfulness that later generations must understand. Matthew applies these words to Christ, whose teaching disclosed truths prepared in God’s plan from the foundation of the world.

Psalm 78:13

He split the sea and brought them through;
At the Red Sea, the Lord delivered Israel from Pharaoh’s pursuing army by opening a path through the waters on dry ground (Ex. 14:21–22). Israel’s escape from Egypt was not secured by human strength but by God’s direct intervention for His covenant people. Their passage marked the decisive break with slavery and the beginning of their journey toward the promised inheritance. Paul identifies the crossing as a baptismal pattern, as Israel passed through the sea under the cloud and was united to Moses’ leadership (1 Cor. 10:1–2). God’s redemption of Israel anticipates the greater deliverance accomplished through Christ, who frees His people from sin, death, and the dominion of Satan.

Psalm 78:64

His priests fell by the sword,
Hophni and Phinehas died when Israel was defeated by the Philistines and the ark was captured (1 Samuel 4:10–11). Their deaths fulfilled God’s judgment on Eli’s corrupt house, foretold because they despised the Lord’s sacrifices and led Israel into sin (1 Samuel 2:27–34). The loss of the priests and the ark revealed the seriousness of covenant unfaithfulness. God would not be manipulated by outward religious forms while His holiness was openly disregarded.

The disaster at Shiloh was so overwhelming that normal public mourning was swallowed up by terror, grief, and national collapse. Phinehas’s wife went into labor at the news and named her son Ichabod, declaring that the glory had departed from Israel because the ark had been taken (1 Samuel 4:19–22). Israel’s failed priests contrast with Jesus Christ, the sinless and faithful High Priest, who offered Himself willingly and now brings comfort, forgiveness, and lasting access to God.

Psalm 78:65

Then the Lord awoke as from sleep,
God’s apparent silence during Israel’s judgment did not mean He had abandoned His covenant purposes. After the ark was captured and Shiloh was forsaken because of Israel’s sin, the Lord rose to judge the Philistines and defend the honor of His name. Psalm 44:23 and Isaiah 51:9 likewise portray God’s people pleading for Him to arise and act. His delay is never indifference; He acts in perfect holiness and at the appointed time.

The Lord entered the conflict with irresistible power, as He had done at the Red Sea, where He was revealed as a man of war (Exodus 15:3). Israel’s strength did not secure deliverance, nor did the ark function as a charm; God Himself saved according to His sovereign purpose. The warrior imagery ultimately points forward to Christ, who will openly defeat every enemy and establish His righteous reign (Revelation 19:11–16).

The Lord’s intervention brought shame upon the Philistines and showed that no human power can withstand His judgment.

Israel’s weakness did not diminish God’s power. He defended His holiness and demonstrated that no enemy could possess His glory or triumph over His purposes.

Psalm 78:67

He rejected the tent of Joseph
God removed the central sanctuary from Shiloh, located in Ephraimite territory, after Israel’s persistent rebellion and the loss of the ark in the days of Eli’s sons. Joseph’s tent represents the tribal prominence of Ephraim and Manasseh, Joseph’s sons, whose inheritance had long held a leading place in Israel. God’s judgment showed that covenant privilege did not excuse idolatry, corruption, or unbelief.

Psalm 78:68

God set aside the northern sanctuary at Shiloh and established the royal and worshiping center of Israel in Judah. Though Judah was not Jacob’s firstborn, God had promised that the ruler’s scepter would belong to Judah’s line (Genesis 49:10). From this tribe He raised David, whose house received the covenant promise of an enduring throne. Jesus Christ, the greater Son of David, came from Judah and will reign forever.

Zion became the location of David’s city, the ark’s resting place, and later Solomon’s temple. God’s love for Zion reflected His covenant purpose to dwell among His people and make Jerusalem the center of their worship. Zion also points beyond the earthly city to the heavenly Jerusalem, where the redeemed will dwell in the presence of God (Hebrews 12:22–24).

He built His sanctuary like the heights,
God chose Zion as the place of His dwelling among His people and established the temple there as a visible sign of His kingship. Its splendor reflected the majesty of the God who reigns in heaven, yet no earthly structure could contain Him. The sanctuary anticipated Jesus Christ, in whom God came to dwell with humanity in fullness.

Psalm 78:70

He chose David His servant
God rejected Saul’s disobedient rule and chose David, Jesse’s youngest son, according to His own purpose. David’s selection showed that the Lord sees the heart rather than outward stature or human expectations (1 Samuel 16:7). Though David’s failures were serious, he was marked by faith, repentance, and devotion to God. His election established the Davidic line through which the promised Messiah would come (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Luke 1:32–33). Jesus, the Son of David, is God’s perfect Servant and eternal King.

Israel needed a king who would serve under God’s authority rather than rule for himself. David’s life began in the obscurity of Bethlehem’s fields, caring for vulnerable sheep and protecting them from danger. The work trained him in vigilance, courage, patience, and faithful responsibility. God had already used those years to prepare David for the greater trust of governing a nation. Like Moses, who was tending sheep when God called him, David was raised from humble service to fulfill God’s appointed purpose.

His shepherd’s heart was to seek the good of the flock rather than his own advancement. Though David’s life included grievous failure, he returned to the Lord in repentance and remained devoted to God’s purposes. His reign points forward to Jesus Christ, the greater Son of David, who is the Good Shepherd and gives His life for His sheep.
