The LORD Has Heard My Voice

Psalm 116:1

I love the LORD,
Love for the LORD rises from the psalmist’s personal experience of God’s saving care. Biblical love includes devoted trust, grateful worship, and obedient allegiance. The LORD’s deliverance calls forth the wholehearted love commanded in Deuteronomy 6:4–5 and displayed in the life of every believer redeemed by His grace.

for He has heard my voice—
The God of Israel is not distant from those who call upon Him. He heard Israel’s groaning in Egypt and acted to deliver them (Exodus 2:24), and He remains attentive to the cries of His people. The psalmist’s confidence rests not in the strength of his prayer but in the compassionate character of the LORD.

my appeal for mercy.
Distress had brought the psalmist to plead for God’s favor and help. Such prayer recognizes human weakness and dependence upon divine compassion. God’s mercy finds its fullest revelation in Jesus Christ, whose death and resurrection secure forgiveness, deliverance from death, and lasting fellowship with all who call on the Lord.

Psalm 116:2

Because He has inclined His ear to me,
The psalmist rejoices that the living God has deliberately attended to his cry. Israel’s God is not distant like the lifeless idols of the nations; He hears, remembers His covenant, and acts for His people, as He did when He heard Israel’s groaning in Egypt (Exodus 2:24). His attentive ear reveals personal mercy toward those who seek Him.

God’s gracious hearing leads to continued dependence rather than self-reliance. Prayer becomes the fitting response of one who has known deliverance, joining worship, thanksgiving, and petitions for help. The Lord’s eyes are upon the righteous, and His ears are open to their cry (Psalm 34:15).

Psalm 116:3

The ropes of death entangled me;
Death seemed to have seized the psalmist like a hunter’s cords, leaving no human means of escape. Psalm 18:4–5 uses the same image of death’s cords and the snares of the grave closing in. Such language portrays real mortal danger, not merely passing discouragement. Christ entered death willingly at the cross, submitting to its curse for sinners, yet death could not hold Him.

the anguish of Sheol overcame me;
Sheol represents the grave and the realm of the dead, the destination toward which the psalmist believed he was being driven. His distress included the terror of losing life and being removed from the worshiping assembly of God’s people. Jonah likewise cried to the Lord “from the belly of Sheol” when all hope of rescue appeared gone (Jonah 2:2). Jesus truly entered the grave, but rose bodily on the third day, conquering death and securing resurrection for those who belong to Him.

I was confronted by trouble and sorrow.
Affliction pressed upon the psalmist outwardly, while grief consumed him inwardly. His lament gives faithful words to those whose suffering seems inescapable. The Lord does not despise such cries; the next verse records the psalmist calling on His name for deliverance.

Psalm 116:4

Then I called on the name of the LORD:
The psalmist turns to the covenant God in helpless dependence, trusting the LORD who revealed Himself to Moses as the faithful God of His people (Exodus 3:14–15). Calling on the LORD acknowledges that deliverance comes from Him alone, as David also testified in distress (Psalm 18:6). God invites His servants to seek Him in trouble and promises to deliver them for His glory (Psalm 50:15).

“O LORD, deliver my soul!”
The plea asks God to preserve the psalmist’s whole life from death and ruin. The LORD’s rescue reaches bodily danger, inward anguish, and the threat of separation from His blessing. Such cries anticipate the salvation fulfilled in Jesus Christ, who came to save His people from their sins (Matthew 1:21). Those who call on the name of the Lord in faith find mercy and salvation through Him (Romans 10:13).

Psalm 116:5

The LORD is gracious and righteous;
God’s grace reveals His undeserved favor toward sinners, while His righteousness declares that all His ways are morally perfect and just. Israel’s history repeatedly displays both attributes: the Lord judged rebellion, yet preserved a remnant, forgave the repentant, and remained faithful to His covenant promises. At Sinai, He proclaimed Himself “compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness” (Exodus 34:6). Grace never compromises God’s justice; at the cross, Jesus Christ bore the penalty of sin so that God might be “just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Romans 3:26).

our God is full of compassion.
The Lord’s compassion flows from His faithful love and reaches those who are weak, afflicted, and unable to save themselves. He hears the cries of His people, remembers their frailty, and draws near in distress. Scripture compares His tender care to the devoted love of a mother for her child, yet God’s compassion surpasses even the strongest human affection (Isaiah 49:15). Jesus revealed this same divine compassion as He healed the sick, fed the hungry, welcomed the outcast, and gave His life for sinners. Those who call upon the Lord may rest in the certainty that His mercy is abundant and His care is personal.

Psalm 116:7

Return to your rest, O my soul,
The psalmist directs his own inner life to settle again in the peace God provides. Having cried to the LORD in distress and experienced His deliverance, he refuses to let fear retain mastery over his heart. Such rest is not escape from trouble but confidence in God’s care, like the sheep led beside quiet waters by the Shepherd in Psalm 23. It anticipates the deeper rest Christ gives to those who come to Him in faith.

Remembering His works guards the soul against despair and strengthens trust that the God who has delivered His people will continue to sustain them.

The psalmist praises the Lord for preserving his life amid danger and distress. God’s saving power recalls His deliverance of Israel from bondage in Egypt and testifies that no threat lies beyond His care. Such rescues point forward to Christ’s victory over death through His resurrection, by which eternal life is secured for all who believe.

Psalm 116:9

I will walk before the LORD
The psalmist responds to God’s rescue with a renewed commitment to live openly under His sovereign gaze. Walking before the LORD describes a continuing life of obedience, gratitude, and accountability rather than a momentary response to trouble. Like Enoch and Noah, who walked with God (Genesis 5:24; 6:9), the worshiper desires a life ordered by faith in God’s revealed will. Deliverance from death creates an obligation to honor the Deliverer with faithful conduct.

God preserved the psalmist’s earthly life so that he might worship, testify, and serve among God’s people. The land of the living stands in contrast to Sheol, where the dead no longer participate in public praise (Psalm 115:17). Having been brought near death, the psalmist expects to declare the LORD’s goodness in the present world, as David also hoped to see the LORD’s goodness “in the land of the living” (Psalm 27:13).

Psalm 116:10

Faith in the LORD sustained the psalmist amid danger and distress. Like Abraham, whose faith was credited to him as righteousness (Genesis 15:6), he trusted God’s character and covenant mercy before deliverance was fully seen. Paul cites Psalm 116:10 in 2 Corinthians 4:13 to show that believers who trust God are compelled to bear witness to His truth.

The statement recognizes the unreliability and sinfulness of fallen humanity, not an absolute denial that anyone can speak truth. God alone remains utterly faithful: “Let God be true, and every man a liar” (Romans 3:4). Jesus also knew betrayal and false witness, yet He remained the faithful and true witness. Psalm 116:12

How can I repay the LORD
The psalmist recognizes that God’s mercy cannot be earned or repaid. Deliverance calls not for a transaction but for grateful devotion, public worship, obedience, and faithful fulfillment of vows. Micah 6:6–8 likewise teaches that God desires humble, covenant faithfulness rather than offerings imagined to place Him in one’s debt.

The psalmist answers God’s deliverance with public thanksgiving. The cup represents the salvation the LORD has granted—rescue from death, answered prayer, covenant mercy, and the blessings received from His hand. In the setting of Israel’s worship, thanksgiving offerings were shared before the LORD, and lifting the cup proclaimed grateful trust in the God who saves. The image reaches forward to the Passover remembrance of redemption and to the cup of the new covenant, by which Christ secured eternal salvation through His blood.

Vows made to the LORD in times of need were binding commitments and could include sacrifices, offerings, or acts of devoted service. Ecclesiastes 5:4–5 warns that vows must not be made carelessly or left unpaid. Faithful fulfillment of such commitments honored the God who had heard the psalmist’s cry and demonstrated covenant loyalty.

Public worship provided accountability and allowed the whole community to hear of God’s mercy. Psalm 116:16

Truly, O LORD, I am Your servant;
The psalmist openly confesses willing allegiance to the covenant God who heard his cry and delivered him from death. Serving the LORD is not degrading bondage but the proper response of one redeemed by grace. Moses, David, and the prophets bore the title “servant of the LORD,” marking lives set apart for His purposes. Jesus Christ fulfilled this calling perfectly, taking the form of a servant and obeying the Father even unto death (Philippians 2:7–8).

Psalm 116:17

I will offer to You a sacrifice of thanksgiving
The sacrifice of thanksgiving belonged to the peace offering and gave worshipers a prescribed way to respond to God’s mercy with grateful worship (Leviticus 7:11–15). Unlike offerings associated with guilt or atonement, this offering celebrated restored fellowship, divine deliverance, and the Lord’s generous provision. The psalmist does not treat gratitude as a private feeling but as worship offered before God and among His people. Thanksgiving recognizes that rescue from death, sorrow, and weakness came from the Lord alone. Believers now offer praise, obedience, generosity, and public confession as spiritual sacrifices through Jesus Christ, whose sacrifice has secured lasting peace with God (Hebrews 13:15; 1 Peter 2:5).

and call on the name of the LORD.
Calling on the name of the LORD joins praise with dependent prayer. The psalmist will publicly acknowledge the covenant God who heard his cry and delivered him. From the days of Seth’s descendants, people called upon the name of the LORD in worship and reliance on His mercy (Genesis 4:26). Israel was to seek the Lord rather than trust idols, human strength, or political alliances. The same dependence lies at the heart of the gospel: everyone who calls on the name of the Lord in faith will be saved (Romans 10:13). Gratitude for God’s past faithfulness leads His people to seek Him with confidence in every present need.

Gratitude for mercy is expressed through obedient worship, thanksgiving, and faithfulness to the God who has heard His servant.
