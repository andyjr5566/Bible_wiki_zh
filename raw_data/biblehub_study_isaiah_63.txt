God’s Vengeance on the Nations

Isaiah 63:1

Who is this coming from Edom,
Edom, descended from Esau, stood southeast of Judah and repeatedly opposed Israel. Its hostility, pride, and violence made it a fitting object of God’s judgment (Obadiah 10–18). The Lord’s advance from Edom presents Him as the Judge who confronts nations that defy His purposes and oppress His people.

from Bozrah with crimson-stained garments?
Bozrah was an important Edomite city, associated with the judgment announced against Edom in Isaiah 34:6. The blood-stained clothing depicts a victorious warrior returning from the defeat of His enemies. Isaiah later explains that the garments are stained through the treading of the winepress of divine wrath (Isaiah 63:3–6), imagery fulfilled ultimately in Christ’s righteous judgment (Revelation 19:13–15).

The warrior’s appearance reveals majesty rather than exhaustion or uncertainty. God’s judgment is not an uncontrolled act of anger but the holy exercise of His sovereign authority. The splendor belongs to the Lord who is glorious in holiness and mighty in power (Exodus 15:11).

The speaker identifies Himself as the One whose words establish righteousness and whose actions uphold justice. God does not overlook violence, oppression, or rebellion. His vindication includes the rescue of His covenant people and the public demonstration that His judgments are true.

Isaiah 63:2

Why are Your clothes red,
The returning divine Warrior appears with garments stained as though by blood, announcing that He has come from executing judgment against the enemies of God. The imagery anticipates the bloodshed described in Isaiah 63:3 and recalls the seriousness of sin before a holy God.

and Your garments like one who treads the winepress?
A winepress worker emerged stained by crushed grapes, and the Lord uses that familiar sight to portray His crushing judgment upon the nations, especially Edom. Revelation 19:13 and 15 presents Christ in similar imagery, wearing a robe dipped in blood and treading the winepress of God’s wrath. The blood that secures redemption for those who trust Him also testifies that unrepentant rebellion will not escape His righteous judgment.

Isaiah 63:3

“I have trodden the winepress alone,
The Lord appears as the divine warrior returning from judgment against Edom, a representative enemy of His people. A winepress required grapes to be crushed beneath the feet; here the image portrays the crushing of rebellion under God’s righteous judgment. No human ruler, army, or deliverer can accomplish what belongs to Him alone.

and no one from the nations was with Me.
The nations neither aid the Lord nor restrain His purpose. When Israel lacked a righteous intercessor and no human deliverer could be found, the Lord Himself acted to save and to judge (Isaiah 59:16). His redemption is independent of human strength, alliances, or merit.

I trampled them in My anger and trod them down in My fury;
God’s anger is not uncontrolled passion but His holy opposition to evil, violence, idolatry, and persistent rebellion. The judgment described is decisive and personal. Joel likewise portrays the nations gathered for judgment as grapes placed in the winepress (Joel 3:12–13), and Revelation presents Christ as the One who treads the winepress of God’s wrath (Revelation 19:15).

The vivid picture emphasizes the deadly reality of divine judgment. The blood is not sacrificial blood bringing atonement for the guilty; it is the evidence of judgment falling upon those who oppose the Lord. Yet the imagery also directs attention to the necessity of atonement, for sin brings death unless God provides a substitute.

The stained garments reveal that the work of judgment has been carried through completely. Revelation echoes this scene in its vision of Christ clothed in a robe dipped in blood as He comes to defeat His enemies (Revelation 19:13). The One who alone executes final judgment is also the One whose blood was shed to cleanse all who turn to Him in faith.

Isaiah 63:4

For the day of vengeance was in My heart,
God’s judgment had been fixed in His righteous purpose against the nations that exalted themselves against Him and oppressed His people. Isaiah 63 portrays the Lord as the divine Warrior coming from Edom and Bozrah, historic enemies of Israel, to execute justice. His vengeance is never impulsive or unjust; it proceeds from His holiness, faithfulness, and commitment to vindicate His name. The Day of the Lord will bring final judgment upon unrepentant rebellion, fulfilled in Christ’s return in power and glory (Isaiah 34:8; 2 Thessalonians 1:7–9; Revelation 19:11–16).

and the year of My redemption had come.
The same divine action that judges Israel’s enemies also delivers God’s redeemed people. Redemption includes release from oppression, restoration to covenant blessing, and the Lord’s faithful preservation of those who belong to Him. The language recalls the Jubilee pattern of liberation and restoration, while looking beyond it to the full salvation accomplished through Christ. Jesus inaugurated the acceptable year of the Lord at His first coming and will complete the redemption of His people when He returns (Leviticus 25; Luke 4:18–21; Ephesians 1:7).

Isaiah 63:5

I looked, but there was no one to help;
As the divine warrior returns from judging Israel’s enemies, the Lord declares that no human ruler, army, or covenant partner was able to establish justice or deliver His people. Israel’s history had repeatedly shown the failure of kings, nations, and even the people of God to accomplish righteousness by their own strength. Ezekiel likewise records that the Lord sought one who would stand in the gap for the land and found no one (Ezekiel 22:30). Judgment and redemption therefore rest on God’s initiative alone.

I was appalled that no one assisted.
Human indifference to evil and inability to restrain it stand in sharp contrast to the Lord’s holy commitment to righteousness. The nations had opposed God’s purposes, and Israel had often proved unfaithful to its calling. Unlike the false gods of surrounding nations, who were imagined to depend on other deities or human support, the Lord needs no helper. His astonishment exposes humanity’s moral failure while magnifying His self-sufficient power.

God’s “arm” portrays His mighty personal intervention. As He redeemed Israel from Egypt with an outstretched arm (Exodus 6:6), He acts decisively to secure victory over His enemies and deliverance for His people. Isaiah 59:16 closely parallels this declaration and prepares for the coming Redeemer. The New Testament reveals the fullest saving work of the Lord in Jesus Christ, who accomplishes what no sinful human being could accomplish through His death and resurrection.

and My own wrath upheld Me.
Divine wrath is God’s righteous and settled opposition to sin, violence, and rebellion. His judgment against evil is not opposed to His saving mercy; it clears the way for the vindication and restoration of His people. The same holiness that judges the oppressor also guarantees that God will keep His covenant promises. Isaiah’s vision anticipates the final judgment in which the Lord defeats evil completely and establishes His righteous reign.

Isaiah 63:6

I trampled the nations in My anger;
The divine Warrior crushes every power that persists in rebellion against God. The winepress imagery portrays total defeat, as grapes are trodden underfoot until their juice runs out. Edom stands prominently in the immediate context as a historic enemy of Israel and a representative of all nations that exalt themselves against the Lord. The judgment reaches beyond any single ancient conflict to the final overthrow of the world’s opposition to God, fulfilled when Christ treads “the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God Almighty” (Revelation 19:15).

God gives rebellious nations the full cup of judgment, leaving them disoriented, powerless, and unable to escape their downfall. Jeremiah likewise saw the nations drinking the cup of God’s wrath until they staggered and fell (Jeremiah 25:15–16). Assyria, Babylon, Edom, and every kingdom that has raised itself against the Lord demonstrate that political strength and military power cannot avert His righteous judgment.

and poured out their blood on the ground.”
The blood of the defeated confirms the finality of divine judgment against hardened evil. God does not overlook violence, oppression, idolatry, or contempt for His holiness. Revelation 14:19–20 returns to the same winepress picture in describing the coming judgment of the nations. The bloodshed of God’s enemies stands in solemn contrast to the blood of Christ, willingly poured out to provide forgiveness and deliver all who turn to Him in faith.

God’s Mercies Recalled

Isaiah 63:7

I will make known the LORD’s loving devotion and His praiseworthy acts,
Isaiah begins a prayer of remembrance by declaring God’s covenant faithfulness before recounting Israel’s failures and suffering. The Lord’s works were not to be hidden or treated as distant history. His people were called to testify publicly to His mercy, redemption, provision, and righteous acts, as Israel was commanded to do from generation to generation.

The prophet speaks as part of the nation, acknowledging that Israel’s survival and blessings came from the Lord rather than from human strength. He recalls the God who delivered His people from Egypt, guided them through the wilderness, gave them the land, and repeatedly preserved them despite their rebellion. Remembering God’s past works strengthens faith when His people seek His help in present need.

Their blessings included the covenants, the law, the priesthood, the land, godly deliverers, and the line of David. Even Israel’s history of discipline testified to God’s commitment to restore a people who would bear His name and receive His promised King.

God’s goodness toward Israel arose from His own merciful character, not from Israel’s deserving. Though the nation often turned to idolatry and unbelief, the Lord remained compassionate and faithful to His covenant. His mercy ultimately reached its fullest revelation in Jesus Christ, the promised Son of David, through whom forgiveness, redemption, and covenant blessing extend to all who believe.

Israel’s suffering was not hidden from the Lord or treated as insignificant. He saw their bondage in Egypt, heard their cries, and came down to deliver them (Exodus 3:7–8). God’s covenant love joined His concern to their condition without diminishing His sovereign holiness. Jesus Christ reveals this same divine compassion as the High Priest who sympathizes with human weakness and entered suffering to redeem His people (Hebrews 4:15).

Isaiah 63:10

But they rebelled and grieved His Holy Spirit.
Israel answered God’s covenant mercy with persistent unbelief, complaint, idolatry, and disobedience during the wilderness years and throughout her history. Though the Spirit guided God’s people and empowered leaders such as Moses, Israel resisted His holy direction. Their sin brought grief to the Spirit, revealing that rebellion against God is personal, not merely the breaking of impersonal rules. Believers are likewise commanded not to grieve the Holy Spirit through sinful conduct (Ephesians 4:30).

The Lord who had delivered Israel from Egypt and defended her against her enemies withdrew His protection and opposed their rebellious ways. The covenant warnings of Deuteronomy anticipated such discipline, and Israel later experienced it through military defeat, foreign oppression, and exile.

God’s discipline upheld His holiness and called His people back to humble repentance, preparing the way for the redemption and reconciliation ultimately accomplished through Christ.

Isaiah 63:11

Then His people remembered the days of old, the days of Moses.
Israel looked back to the Exodus as the defining proof of God’s covenant faithfulness. Through Moses, the Lord delivered His people from Egypt, gave them His law, and led them toward the land He promised. Remembering those days exposed the contrast between God’s steadfast mercy and Israel’s later rebellion, calling the nation to humble repentance and renewed trust. Moses served as God’s appointed leader and mediator, yet his ministry anticipated the greater deliverer, Jesus Christ, who brings His people into the blessings of the new covenant.

Moses and Aaron cared for the people as shepherds under the authority of the Lord, the true Shepherd of Israel. Passing through the sea marked Israel’s separation from slavery and their identification with God’s covenant people. Scripture later connects that deliverance with baptism, portraying salvation from bondage and entrance into a new life under God’s rule.

A Prayer for Mercy

Isaiah 63:15

Look down from heaven and see,
Isaiah appeals to the God who rules over all nations and sees every condition on earth. Israel’s distress had not escaped His knowledge, yet the prophet pleads for the Lord to act as He had acted in the Exodus, when He heard His people’s groaning and delivered them from Egypt. Heaven’s distance from a devastated land intensifies the cry for divine intervention.

God remains holy and glorious even when His people suffer under the consequences of sin and judgment. Though the temple in Jerusalem had represented His dwelling among Israel, no earthly sanctuary could contain Him. His heavenly throne assures the repentant that judgment has not removed God’s sovereign authority or His covenant faithfulness.

Where are Your zeal and might?
Israel remembered the Lord’s mighty works in the Red Sea, the wilderness, and the conquest of the promised land. His zeal had defended His name and preserved His covenant people despite their repeated rebellion. The question arises from the apparent absence of such deliverance in Isaiah’s day, when Jerusalem faced ruin and the people experienced exile and humiliation.

The prophet speaks for the afflicted people as a child pleading for a father’s mercy. Isaiah 63:17

Why, O LORD, do You make us stray from Your ways
Israel’s confession recognizes that persistent rebellion had brought God’s disciplinary judgment. The people had repeatedly rejected His law, pursued idols, and resisted the prophets He sent. God’s sovereignty did not make Him the author of their sin; rather, He gave the rebellious nation over to the hardness it had chosen, exposing the ruinous outcome of forsaking His ways.

and harden our hearts from fearing You?
A heart that no longer fears God becomes indifferent to His holiness and resistant to correction. As with Pharaoh, hardening followed sustained defiance of divine revelation and also served God’s righteous purposes in judgment. Israel’s leaders and people had witnessed God’s covenant faithfulness, yet their continued unbelief left them spiritually blind and unable to respond rightly to His warnings.

The prayer appeals to God’s covenant mercy rather than Israel’s merit. God’s servants include the faithful remnant who continued to seek Him amid the nation’s apostasy, as well as the patriarchs and prophets through whom He had bound Himself to His people. The request for God to return asks for the restoring presence, favor, and protection that sin had forfeited.

Isaiah 63:18

For a short while Your people possessed Your holy place,
Israel received the Promised Land through God’s faithfulness and established the temple in Jerusalem under Solomon as the appointed center of worship. Yet covenant blessing required faithful obedience. Persistent idolatry and rebellion brought discipline, and the land and sanctuary that testified to God’s presence became scenes of judgment.

but our enemies have trampled Your sanctuary.
Babylon’s destruction of Solomon’s temple in 586 BC exposed the grievous cost of Judah’s sin and brought national exile. The ruined sanctuary represented more than military defeat; it revealed that God would not permit His holy name to be honored outwardly while His people rejected Him inwardly. Even amid the lament, the sanctuary remained God’s, preserving hope that He would restore His people and renew their worship.

Isaiah 63:19

We have become like those You never ruled,
Israel’s exile made the covenant people feel indistinguishable from the nations that had never received God’s law, temple, priesthood, or promised land. God had ruled Israel as King through His covenant, yet their persistent idolatry and rebellion brought the judgment warned of in Deuteronomy 28. The lament does not deny God’s covenant faithfulness; it appeals to that faithfulness, pleading for Him to act again for His people.

Psa 44:9 But You have rejected and humbled us; You no longer go forth…

like those not called by Your name.
Israel bore the Lord’s name as His chosen possession, set apart to display His holiness among the nations. Removal from Jerusalem and the destruction of the temple seemed to strip away that visible identity. The prayer asks God to remember the people He had claimed and to restore the honor of His name. God’s saving purpose ultimately extends to believing Gentiles through Christ, yet Romans 11 affirms that God has not rejected Israel and that His covenant purposes remain sure.
