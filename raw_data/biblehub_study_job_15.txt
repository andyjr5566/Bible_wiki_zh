Eliphaz: Job Does Not Fear God

Job 15:1

Then Eliphaz the Temanite replied:
Eliphaz was one of the three friends who came to mourn with Job and comfort him in his suffering. As the first friend to speak, he appears to hold a leading place among them. His name means “God is fine gold” or “God is my strength,” yet his counsel increasingly fails to reflect God’s compassion and wisdom.

Job 15:2

“Does a wise man answer with empty counsel
Eliphaz begins his second speech by denying that Job’s words reflect true wisdom. He assumes that wisdom produces calm submission to suffering and therefore judges Job’s lament as empty and unprofitable. Scripture teaches that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom, yet Job’s grief and questions do not prove that he lacks such fear. Eliphaz correctly values godly wisdom but wrongly applies his belief that severe suffering must expose personal wickedness.

or fill his belly with the hot east wind?
The scorching east wind was known for bringing dryness, destruction, and loss to the land. Eliphaz compares Job’s speech to such a wind, charging that his words are empty in substance and harmful in effect. The image intensifies Eliphaz’s rebuke, but it also reveals his failure to enter Job’s affliction with compassion. Human counsel becomes destructive when it speaks confidently about God’s purposes without the understanding God alone possesses.

Job 15:3

Should he argue with useless words
Eliphaz charges Job with defending himself through empty reasoning rather than submitting to the moral order Eliphaz believes governs suffering. In his second speech, Eliphaz assumes that severe affliction proves hidden wickedness, so Job’s insistence on his integrity appears to him as rebellion against God. Scripture does warn against careless and multiplying words (Proverbs 10:19), yet Job’s anguished questions are not meaningless. They preserve the reality that the righteous may suffer without knowing why, while still bringing their complaint before God.

or speeches that serve no purpose?
Eliphaz believes Job’s speeches neither explain his suffering nor lead him to repentance. His accusation reveals the limits of the friends’ wisdom: they seek a simple answer where God has not given one. Job’s words do serve a purpose, for they expose the inadequacy of human explanations and teach believers to bring grief, confusion, and faith honestly before the Lord. God ultimately rebukes Eliphaz and his friends for speaking wrongly about Him, while receiving Job’s humbled repentance and restored trust (Job 42:7–8).

Job 15:4

Eliphaz charges Job with eroding the reverence and submission that belong to the Lord. Fear of God stands at the heart of wisdom and covenant faithfulness (Deuteronomy 10:12; Proverbs 9:10). Yet the opening chapters have already identified Job as a man who feared God and turned from evil (Job 1:1, 8). Eliphaz wrongly treats Job’s anguished questions as proof of rebellion.

Eliphaz believes Job’s complaints discourage prayerful reflection and worship before God. Job’s suffering, however, has driven him repeatedly to bring his grief directly to the Lord rather than abandon Him. Honest lament is not the opposite of faith; it can be an act of faith when a sufferer continues to seek God amid darkness. The Lord later declares that Job spoke more rightly than his friends, exposing the failure of their harsh judgment (Job 42:7).

Job 15:5

For your iniquity instructs your mouth,
Eliphaz charges Job with letting hidden sin govern his defense. He assumes Job’s suffering proves guilt and interprets Job’s strong protests of innocence as further evidence of rebellion. Scripture often teaches that words reveal the heart (Matt. 12:34), yet Eliphaz wrongly applies that truth by treating Job’s grief and honest lament as proof of iniquity.

and you choose the language of the crafty.
Eliphaz portrays Job’s arguments as calculated deception rather than sincere appeals to God. The description recalls the serpent’s craftiness in Genesis 3:1 and casts Job’s speech in a morally dark light. Job does not deny God’s justice; he refuses to confess sins he has not committed. Eliphaz’s accusation exposes the limits of retributive theology: suffering may test the righteous and cannot always be explained as God’s punishment for personal sin.

Job 15:6

Your own mouth, not mine, condemns you;
Eliphaz charges that Job’s protests of innocence and questions about God’s justice expose rebellion in his heart. Words can indeed reveal a person’s character and carry moral weight (Prov. 18:21; Matt. 12:37). Yet Eliphaz wrongly treats Job’s anguished speech as proof of hidden wickedness. The Lord later rebukes Job’s friends because they had not spoken rightly about Him as Job had (Job 42:7). Job’s suffering was not punishment for secret sin, and his honest cries were the language of a faithful sufferer seeking God.

your own lips testify against you.
Eliphaz speaks as though Job’s testimony has supplied the evidence for his guilt. In legal settings, testimony could establish a matter, but Eliphaz assumes a verdict without knowing God’s purpose in Job’s trial. Scripture warns that the tongue can corrupt and destroy (James 3:6), and every person will give account to God (Romans 14:12). Yet God alone judges with complete knowledge and perfect righteousness. Job’s experience calls believers to speak truthfully, to bring grief honestly before God, and to avoid condemning those whose suffering cannot be explained by human wisdom.

Job 15:7

Were you the first man ever born?
Eliphaz challenges Job’s claim to understanding by asking whether he existed from humanity’s beginning. Adam, the first man, was created by God from the dust of the ground (Genesis 2:7), yet even Adam did not possess God’s limitless wisdom. Human knowledge remains bounded by creaturely life, history, and revelation. Eliphaz rightly affirms God’s superiority to man, though he wrongly assumes that Job’s suffering proves hidden wickedness.

The hills portray the ancient stability of the created world, formed long before any human generation. Proverbs 8:25 similarly speaks of wisdom as preceding the mountains, pointing to wisdom’s origin in God rather than in human experience. Job had not witnessed creation or entered God’s heavenly counsel; neither had Eliphaz. The question prepares for the truth later declared by the Lord: God alone knows and governs the purposes established from the foundation of the world.

Job 15:8

Do you listen in on the council of God
Eliphaz challenges Job’s claim to comprehend God’s purposes in suffering. God rules over the heavenly assembly of spiritual beings, as seen in 1 Kings 22:19–22 and Psalm 82, yet no creature stands as His equal or searches out His counsel. Job had not received a divine explanation for his trials, and Eliphaz insists that human wisdom cannot judge God’s hidden purposes.

or limit wisdom to yourself?
Eliphaz accuses Job of speaking as though his understanding surpassed that of the wise and godly. Wisdom belongs ultimately to the Lord, who gives knowledge and understanding (Prov. 2:6). Job rightly rejected the false comfort of his friends, but his grief had also led him to words that demanded an answer from God. The question calls every sufferer to humility before the God whose wisdom is fully revealed in Christ (1 Cor. 1:24).

Job 15:9

What do you know that we do not?
Eliphaz challenges Job as though the accumulated wisdom of the elders has already settled the meaning of suffering. His confidence rests on the belief that the righteous prosper and the wicked suffer, leaving no place for Job’s protest of innocence. Yet Job’s trial exposes the limits of traditional explanations. Human wisdom, even when rooted in experience and age, cannot fully interpret God’s hidden purposes. God alone knows the end from the beginning, and His ways rise above human judgments (Isaiah 55:8–9).

What do you understand that is not clear to us?
Eliphaz treats Job’s understanding as pride rather than recognizing that Job is wrestling honestly before God. The friends assume that their shared conclusion must be correct because it agrees with accepted wisdom. God later reveals that they have not spoken rightly about Him as Job has (Job 42:7). Job does not possess complete knowledge, but neither do his friends. The Lord’s speeches in Job 38–41 show that suffering cannot always be explained by visible circumstances, and faithful believers must submit their unanswered questions to God’s perfect wisdom.

Job 15:10

Both the gray-haired and the aged are on our side—
Eliphaz appeals to the presence of respected elders among those opposing Job’s claims. In the ancient world, advanced age commonly represented tested experience, familiarity with ancestral tradition, and standing within the community. Elders often exercised influence in family, civic, and legal matters, and their counsel was expected to be heard with honor. Eliphaz presents the accumulated wisdom of the older generation as support for the traditional conviction that suffering reveals divine judgment. Scripture does commend honor for the aged and recognizes gray hair as “a crown of glory” when joined to righteousness (Proverbs 16:31), yet Job’s ordeal demonstrates that human tradition must remain subject to the wisdom and justice of God.

men much older than your father.
By comparing these men with Job’s father, Eliphaz makes his appeal personal and forceful. Their greater age is meant to establish their superior authority over Job and to pressure him into accepting their interpretation of his suffering. Israel was later commanded to remember former generations and learn from the instruction of fathers and elders (Deuteronomy 32:7). Even so, age alone does not guarantee right judgment. Job will ultimately be corrected by God, but his friends’ confidence in inherited assumptions proves inadequate before the mystery of God’s providence.

Eliphaz rebukes Job as though he has rejected the comfort God provides through His promises, providence, and the counsel of friends. Yet Job’s anguish does not arise from contempt for God’s consolation, but from the bewildering loss of God’s felt favor while he remains faithful. The Lord’s comfort is sufficient, but human attempts to explain suffering can misrepresent Him.

Eliphaz charged Job with allowing inward turmoil to govern his conclusions about God. In Job’s suffering, his heart was indeed deeply grieved, yet the Lord had already declared him blameless and upright (Job 1:8). Eliphaz wrongly assumed that Job’s painful words proved rebellion rather than the anguish of a faithful man wrestling honestly before God. Scripture calls God’s people to guard the heart, for its direction shapes the whole life (Proverbs 4:23).

Eliphaz acknowledges humanity’s universal moral corruption before a holy God. No descendant of Adam possesses the purity required to stand righteous by personal merit; all share the fallen condition revealed after Genesis 3 and confessed in Psalm 51:5. Yet Eliphaz wrongly applies this truth to Job as proof that his suffering must result from exceptional hidden sin.

Psa 2:2 The kings of the earth take their stand…

and has vaunted himself against the Almighty,
Pride moves a sinner to treat God’s authority as a rival to be challenged rather than truth to be obeyed. The Almighty, a term emphasizing God’s supreme power and authority, is not merely stronger than human rulers; He is sovereign over heaven, earth, life, and death. Such self-exaltation recalls Babel, where mankind sought to make a name apart from God, and Saul, whose refusal to obey revealed a heart set against the Lord’s rule. Christ stands in direct contrast, humbling Himself in obedient service even unto death.

Job 15:26

rushing headlong at Him
Eliphaz portrays the wicked as openly defying God, charging against His authority with reckless confidence. Such rebellion rejects the fear of the Lord, which is the beginning of wisdom, and follows the sinful haste warned against in Proverbs 19:2. Pharaoh hardened his heart and pursued Israel despite repeated judgments, only to perish in the Red Sea (Exodus 14). Humanity similarly sought independence from God at Babel, uniting to make a name for itself rather than honoring the Creator (Genesis 11:1–9). No person can successfully contend with the Almighty, whose wisdom and power govern all creation.

Job 39:19-25 Do you give strength to the horse or adorn his neck…

with a thick, studded shield.
The warrior’s shield pictures determined self-protection and confidence in human strength. Ancient soldiers used heavy shields reinforced with metal studs to withstand an enemy’s weapons, but no physical defense can shelter the unrepentant from God’s judgment. Eliphaz’s image exposes the folly of treating wealth, power, military strength, or personal resolve as a defense against the Lord. God Himself is the true shield of those who trust Him (Psalm 18:2), and believers stand secure through the shield of faith rather than self-reliance (Ephesians 6:16).

Job 15:27

Though his face is covered with fat
Eliphaz portrays the wicked as outwardly prosperous, well-fed, and secure. In the ancient world, abundance of food often marked wealth and social success, yet Job’s suffering had already exposed the error of measuring God’s favor by visible prosperity. The ungodly may enjoy ease for a season while remaining hardened against the Lord.

Eliphaz portrays the wicked man as living amid the visible results of judgment and collapse. Ruined cities were reminders of conquest, famine, exile, and the removal of God’s protecting favor. The image anticipates the desolation that later came upon proud nations and rebellious cities, including Babylon and Nineveh.

Eliphaz portrays the wicked as unable to flee the darkness of divine judgment. Darkness recalls God’s plague upon Egypt and the spiritual blindness of those who reject the light of God (Ex. 10:21–23; John 3:19–20). Though Eliphaz wrongly applied such accusations to Job, his warning reflects the biblical certainty that rebellion against God ultimately brings judgment.

Job 15:32

It will be paid in full before his time,
Eliphaz warns that the wicked man will receive the full consequence of his rebellion before the expected course of his life is complete. A long life was commonly regarded as God’s blessing (Exodus 20:12), while an early death could reveal divine judgment. Eliphaz rightly affirms that God rules over life and its appointed times (Psalm 31:15), though the book of Job ultimately shows that suffering and premature death cannot always be explained as direct punishment for personal sin.
