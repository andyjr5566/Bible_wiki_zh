The Twelve Apostles

And calling His twelve disciples to Him,
The twelve correspond to the twelve tribes of Israel, showing that Jesus was gathering and renewing God’s covenant people under His messianic rule. Their summons came personally from Him, establishing them first as disciples who lived under His instruction before sending them as His representatives.

Jesus appointed twelve men as His authorized representatives, preparing them to carry His message beyond Galilee and Judea. Their number recalls the twelve tribes of Israel and points to the gathering and renewal of God’s covenant people under the reign of the Messiah. The apostles would witness Christ’s works, receive His teaching, proclaim the kingdom, and later bear testimony to His death and resurrection.

first Simon, called Peter,
Peter stands first in the apostolic lists and repeatedly serves as spokesman among the Twelve. Jesus gave Simon the name Peter, meaning “rock,” and later charged him with a central role in strengthening his brothers and confessing the truth that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God (Matthew 16:16–18; Luke 22:31–32). Though Peter’s failures were real, his restoration after denying Jesus displays Christ’s sustaining grace and prepared him for faithful leadership in the early church.

Andrew’s quiet faithfulness shows that bringing one person to Christ may have far-reaching consequences in God’s work.

James later became the first of the Twelve to die as a martyr when Herod Agrippa put him to death with the sword (Acts 12:2), revealing the cost of bearing witness to Christ.

Matthew 10:3

Philip and Bartholomew;
Philip, from Bethsaida like Andrew and Peter, was called directly by Jesus and promptly brought Nathanael to Him. Bartholomew is commonly identified with Nathanael, since Philip and Bartholomew are paired in the apostolic lists while Philip introduces Nathanael in John’s Gospel. Nathanael’s question about Nazareth gave way to confession when Jesus revealed His supernatural knowledge of him: “Rabbi, You are the Son of God; You are the King of Israel.” Christ met honest skepticism with personal revelation and called Nathanael to expect still greater displays of His glory.

Thomas would later struggle to believe the resurrection until he saw the wounds of the risen Lord, yet his encounter with Jesus led to the confession, “My Lord and my God.” Matthew had been a tax collector, a profession associated with Roman rule and public dishonesty. Jesus called him from that former life into the circle of the Twelve, displaying grace that reaches those regarded as socially and spiritually disreputable. Matthew’s Gospel would especially testify that Jesus is Israel’s promised Messiah and the fulfillment of the Scriptures.

James son of Alphaeus is distinguished from James the son of Zebedee and is likely the James called “the Less.” Scripture records little about his individual ministry, but his appointment shows that faithful service to Christ is not measured by public prominence. Thaddaeus is also called Judas son of James, not Judas Iscariot. The varied names among the apostles reflect the Jewish and Greek cultural setting of their day. Together, these lesser-known disciples testify that Christ chose ordinary men from differing backgrounds to bear authoritative witness to His kingdom.

Matthew 10:4

Simon the Zealot,
Simon’s former identification with Jewish zeal for Israel’s freedom distinguished him from Simon Peter. Whether he had belonged formally to the anti-Roman movement or was known for intense religious and national devotion, Simon had once been shaped by the political tensions of Roman-occupied Judea. Jesus called him into a kingdom not advanced by violence but by repentance, faith, and sacrificial love. Beside fishermen, a tax collector, and others from differing backgrounds, Simon testified that Christ unites those whom society and politics would divide.

Judas likely came from Kerioth in Judea, making him perhaps the only apostle from that region. He was entrusted with the disciples’ moneybag, yet John records that greed had taken root in his heart. Outward proximity to Jesus and participation in apostolic ministry did not produce genuine faith in Judas. His place among the Twelve reveals the searching knowledge of Christ and warns against the hidden corruption of a heart that refuses surrender.

Judas later felt remorse, but he did not turn to Christ in repentant faith. His tragic end stands in contrast to the mercy available to all who truly return to the Lord.

The Ministry of the Twelve

Jesus commissioned the twelve apostles as representatives of restored Israel, corresponding to the twelve tribes. Their mission announced the nearness of God’s kingdom and called Israel to recognize her Messiah. This limited sending prepared them for the wider work that would follow Christ’s death and resurrection.

The Messiah came to His own people in fulfillment of God’s faithfulness to Abraham, David, and the prophets. Gentile inclusion was always part of God’s purpose, as Isaiah foretold, but the risen Christ would later send His disciples to all nations.

After Pentecost, the gospel would spread into Samaria through the apostles’ ministry, showing that Christ’s salvation removes the barriers of ancestry, region, and longstanding enmity.

Sheep among Wolves

Matthew 10:16

Behold, I am sending you out like sheep among wolves;
Jesus sends the Twelve into a world that will often resist their message and oppose their Master. Sheep possess little natural defense and depend on the shepherd for guidance and protection; the disciples must therefore rely on Christ rather than strength, influence, or retaliation. Wolves portray those who would exploit, threaten, or persecute God’s servants. The warning anticipates the hostility Jesus later describes in John 15:18–20 and the suffering His witnesses would endure after His resurrection. Yet the danger does not cancel their mission. The gospel must be carried into a hostile world, and Christ remains the Shepherd who knows, leads, and preserves His flock.

Luk 10:3 Go! I am sending you out like lambs among wolves.

Jhn 10:12 The hired hand is not the shepherd, and the sheep…

Act 20:29 I know that after my departure, savage wolves will come…

therefore be as shrewd as snakes
Because they are sent among wolves, the disciples must exercise alertness, discernment, and practical wisdom. The serpent’s cunning in Genesis 3 shows its ability to recognize opportunity and danger, though Jesus does not command deceit or moral compromise. His servants are to speak truth faithfully while avoiding needless provocation, recognizing hostility, and acting wisely under pressure. Such wisdom would guide them in knowing when to remain, when to flee persecution, and how to answer opponents without abandoning the message entrusted to them.

and as innocent as doves.

This principle appears again in John 13:20 and calls Christ’s servants to faithfulness as His representatives.

Receiving Jesus brings a person face to face with the Father who sent Him. The Son’s mission reveals the Father’s will, so no one can honor God while rejecting His Messiah. The chain of reception establishes both the divine authority of Christ and the seriousness of responding rightly to those sent with His true message.

Matthew 10:41

Whoever receives a prophet because he is a prophet
God’s prophets bore His authoritative message, often at personal cost and in the face of opposition. To receive one of them was to welcome the God who sent him and to submit to His word. The widow of Zarephath received Elijah during famine and witnessed God’s provision and power (1 Kings 17:8–24). The Shunammite woman recognized Elisha as “a holy man of God,” gave him hospitality, and became the recipient of God’s mercy (2 Kings 4:8–37). Jesus had sent His disciples as His representatives; those who welcomed them for the sake of their divine mission received more than traveling teachers.
