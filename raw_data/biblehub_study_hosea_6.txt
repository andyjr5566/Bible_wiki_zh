The Unrepentance of Israel and Judah

Hosea 6:1

Come, let us return to the LORD.
Israel is summoned to seek again the covenant God whom it had abandoned through idolatry and political compromise. The call follows the Lord’s withdrawal in Hosea 5:15, where He waits for His people to acknowledge their guilt and seek His face in distress. Repentance is presented as a shared duty: the nation must not merely regret its suffering but turn back to the Lord in faith and obedience.

The same God who judged Israel for covenant unfaithfulness alone can restore it. Hosea had compared the Lord’s judgment to the attack of a lion against Ephraim and Judah (Hosea 5:14), fulfilling the warnings of judgment given in the Law. Divine discipline was not purposeless destruction; it exposed sin and called the people away from false gods and self-reliance.

The Lord’s judgment carries the hope of mercy for those who truly return to Him. He wounds in righteousness, yet He also binds, restores, and renews those humbled before Him. God’s healing of His people points beyond national restoration to the saving work of Christ, who bears the consequences of sin and brings forgiveness, reconciliation, and life to all who come to Him.

Israel acknowledges that the God who wounds in judgment is also able to heal and restore. The short period portrays the certainty and nearness of God’s mercy after His disciplining hand has accomplished its purpose. Revival includes renewed covenant life, repentance, and restoration from the ruin brought by sin.

The third day repeatedly marks God’s decisive intervention, and Hosea’s words find their fullest fulfillment in Jesus Christ, who rose from the dead on the third day. His resurrection secures the future resurrection and restoration of all who belong to Him.

Hosea 6:3

So let us know—let us press on to know the LORD.
Israel is called beyond outward repentance to covenant faithfulness marked by a growing, obedient relationship with the LORD. Knowing God involves trusting His character, receiving His word, and walking in the love and loyalty required by the covenant. The appeal recalls Israel’s calling to love the one true God with all the heart, soul, and strength (Deuteronomy 6:4–5), and it anticipates the New Covenant promise that God’s people would truly know Him (Jeremiah 31:34). Yet the Lord’s response in Hosea 6:4 shows that such words must be joined to steadfast love rather than temporary emotion.

His appearance brings both salvation for those who return to Him and accountability for those who persist in unfaithfulness. God had not abandoned His covenant purposes, even though Israel and Judah had repeatedly forsaken Him. The image also points forward to Christ, the true light who brings salvation and exposes darkness.

Hosea 6:4

What shall I do with you, O Ephraim?
Ephraim, the dominant tribe of the northern kingdom, stands for Israel as a whole. God’s question reveals grief as well as judgment: His people had repeatedly received His mercy yet returned to idolatry, injustice, and political dependence on foreign nations.

Judah shared Israel’s guilt despite retaining the temple in Jerusalem and the Davidic line of kings. Outward religious privileges had not produced enduring obedience. Both kingdoms needed repentance, for neither could claim safety merely through heritage, territory, or religious ceremony.

Hosea 6:5

Therefore I have hewn them by the prophets;
God sent His prophets to Israel and Judah with messages that exposed covenant unfaithfulness and called the people to return to Him. Their preaching cut through empty worship, political self-reliance, and divided loyalties. Like a stoneworker removing what is crooked and corrupt, the Lord used prophetic rebuke to confront sin and summon His people to repentance.

God’s announced judgments were certain because they proceeded from His own authority. The prophets did not speak merely human opinions; they declared the word of the Lord, which condemned rebellion and warned of the death and exile that persistent disobedience would bring. Israel’s refusal to heed that word brought the very judgment God had proclaimed.

His judgments expose what darkness conceals and establish that He remains faithful to His covenant, even when His people prove faithless.

Hosea 6:6

For I desire mercy, not sacrifice,
God condemned Israel’s empty worship because sacrifices were offered without covenant faithfulness, repentance, or love for neighbor. The sacrificial system was ordained by God, yet it was never meant to substitute for obedience. Samuel had similarly declared that obedience is better than sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22). Jesus cited Hosea 6:6 when confronting religious leaders who preserved outward forms while neglecting compassion (Matthew 9:13; 12:7).

and the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings.
God seeks a true, obedient relationship with His people, marked by knowing His character, trusting His word, and walking in His ways. Israel’s idolatry and injustice revealed that its worship was hollow. Burnt offerings symbolized wholehearted dedication, but they had no value when offered by hearts far from God. The call anticipates the New Covenant, in which Christ offered Himself once for all and brings His people into genuine fellowship with God (Hebrews 10:5–10).

Hosea 6:7

But they, like Adam, have transgressed the covenant;
Adam, the first man created by God, knowingly violated God’s command in the Garden of Eden and broke the fellowship for which he was created (Genesis 2:16–17; 3:1–19). Israel likewise violated the covenant God established at Mount Sinai, despite receiving His law, His promises, His priesthood, and His presence among them (Exodus 19–24). Their privileged position did not shield them from judgment when they chose rebellion. Adam’s fall revealed humanity’s bent toward sin; Israel’s history demonstrated that even a redeemed nation could not remain faithful apart from a transformed heart. The failure of Adam and Israel points to the need for the obedient last Adam, Jesus Christ, whose righteousness brings life to those who believe (Romans 5:12–19; 1 Corinthians 15:45).

Jer 31:32 It will not be like the covenant I made with their fathers…

there they were unfaithful to Me.
Israel’s betrayal was not merely the violation of religious rules but personal unfaithfulness toward the God who had loved, delivered, and provided for them. From the golden calf at Sinai to recurring devotion to Baal and other idols, they repeatedly exchanged worship of the living God for the practices of surrounding nations (Exodus 32; Judges 2:11–13). Hosea portrays such idolatry as marital treachery because Israel belonged to the Lord by covenant. Yet God’s charge of unfaithfulness is joined throughout Hosea with His call to return, for His mercy provides the way for restoration (Hosea 14:1–4).

Hosea 6:8

Gilead is a city of evildoers,
Gilead, the fertile highland east of the Jordan known for its healing balm, had become identified with rebellion against God. Its reputation for a medicinal ointment stood in bitter contrast to the moral sickness of its people. The land allotted to Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh had witnessed idolatry, injustice, and covenant unfaithfulness. No place of religious heritage or material blessing can shield a people who persist in sin.

Bloodstained tracks portray violence so open and frequent that guilt lay plainly visible upon the land. Murder, oppression, and the abuse of the innocent had defiled a people called to reflect God’s justice. God saw what human courts ignored, and He held Israel accountable for every act of bloodshed. The healing Gilead could not provide pointed ultimately to the cleansing God would accomplish through the blood of Christ, which alone removes the guilt of sin.

Hosea 6:10

The Lord’s judgment rests on His perfect knowledge of Israel’s covenant rebellion. The house of Israel, the Northern Kingdom, had received God’s law, priests, prophets, and repeated calls to return, yet it persisted in worshiping the calves at Bethel and Daniel and in adopting the corrupt practices of surrounding nations. What God sees is not hidden religious failure but a detestable betrayal of the relationship He established with His people.
