Divine favor rests on the person who deliberately depends on the LORD’s character, promises, and power. David grounds true security not in kings, armies, wealth, or personal ability, but in the covenant God who delivers His people. Abraham’s faith in God’s promise was counted to him as righteousness (Genesis 15:6), and believers are likewise called to trust the LORD wholeheartedly rather than their own understanding (Proverbs 3:5–6).

Psalm 40:6

Sacrifice and offering You did not desire,
God ordained Israel’s sacrificial system, but He never accepted ritual as a substitute for faithful obedience. Samuel rebuked Saul for disobeying God while preserving animals for sacrifice, declaring that obedience is better than sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22). David likewise knew that God receives a broken and contrite heart rather than empty offerings (Psalm 51:16–17). Sacrifices testified to sin, atonement, and dependence on God’s mercy, yet worship became offensive when offered by unrepentant hearts.

God had prepared the psalmist to hear His commands and yield himself willingly to His will. The image recalls the willing servant whose ear was pierced at the doorpost as a permanent sign of devoted service (Exodus 21:5–6). True worship begins when God’s servant listens to His voice and responds in obedience. Hebrews 10:5 applies Psalm 40 to Christ, whose obedient earthly life and self-offering fulfilled all that the sacrifices anticipated.

Burnt offerings and sin offerings You did not require.
Burnt offerings expressed complete consecration, and sin offerings addressed guilt before God, but neither could cleanse the conscience or permanently remove sin. God instituted these offerings as temporary provisions within the covenant with Israel and as shadows pointing to the final sacrifice. Jesus Christ fulfilled their purpose by offering Himself once for all, establishing the New Covenant in which God writes His law on the hearts of His people (Jeremiah 31:33; Hebrews 10:5–10).

Psalm 40:8

I delight to do Your will, O my God;
David portrays obedience to God as the glad response of a heart bound to Him in covenant love. Hebrews 10:5–10 applies Psalm 40:6–8 to Jesus Christ, whose perfect obedience fulfilled the Father’s redemptive purpose and surpassed the sacrifices that could never remove sin. Christ’s submission reached its fullest expression in His death on the cross: “Not as I will, but as You will” (Matthew 26:39). God’s will is not a burdensome alternative to joy but the path of faithful worship for those who know Him as “my God.”

Moses commanded Israel to keep God’s words upon their hearts (Deuteronomy 6:6), and Jeremiah foretold the new covenant in which God would write His law on the hearts of His people (Jeremiah 31:33). Jesus perfectly embodied that inward righteousness and calls His people to obedience flowing from a transformed heart.
