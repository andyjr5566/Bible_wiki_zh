Luke 20:10

At harvest time,
The concept of “harvest time” is significant in biblical literature, often symbolizing a time of judgment or fulfillment. In the context of a vineyard, it represents the expected time of fruitfulness and reward for labor. Biblically, harvests were times of celebration and thanksgiving, as seen in the Feast of Harvest (Exodus 23:16). This phrase sets the stage for the expectation of rightful returns from the vineyard, which is a metaphor for Israel (Isaiah 5:1-7).

The sending of a servant reflects the practice of landowners who would send representatives to collect their due. In the parable, the servant represents the prophets sent by God to Israel. Historically, prophets were often rejected or mistreated by the people they were sent to (Jeremiah 7:25-26). This action underscores God’s persistent attempts to reach His people despite their resistance.

The expectation of fruitfulness is a recurring theme in Scripture, where God desires justice and righteousness (Micah 6:8). This phrase highlights the rightful claim of God over His people and their spiritual output.

Luke 20:30

Then the second
In the context of Luke 20:30, this phrase is part of a parable Jesus tells to the Sadducees, who did not believe in the resurrection. The parable involves a woman who marries seven brothers in succession, each dying without leaving children. This scenario is based on the practice of levirate marriage, as outlined in Deuteronomy 25:5-10, where a brother is to marry the widow of his deceased brother to produce offspring in his name. This practice was culturally significant in ancient Israel to preserve family lineage and inheritance rights.

The “second” brother here represents the continuation of this law, highlighting the Sadducees’ attempt to challenge Jesus on the concept of resurrection by presenting a hypothetical situation. Theologically, this passage underscores the limitations of human understanding when it comes to divine matters such as life after death. It also serves to illustrate Jesus’ authority and wisdom in addressing complex theological questions.

In a broader biblical context, this passage connects to the theme of resurrection found throughout the New Testament, particularly in 1 Corinthians 15, where Paul elaborates on the resurrection of the dead. The parable ultimately points to the reality of the resurrection and the transformative power of God, which transcends earthly customs and laws.

Luke 20:47

They defraud widows of their houses,
Widows often lacked a husband’s income, legal advocate, and social protection. God commanded Israel to defend widows and warned of His judgment against those who afflicted them (Exodus 22:22–24; Deuteronomy 10:18). Scribes could exploit their knowledge of the law, their role in handling estates, or the trust extended to them as religious leaders in order to gain access to a widow’s property. Such conduct directly opposed the justice God required and revealed hearts willing to use spiritual influence for personal gain.

Outward religious activity cannot conceal injustice from the Lord, who sees both the hidden motives of leaders and the suffering of those they exploit.
