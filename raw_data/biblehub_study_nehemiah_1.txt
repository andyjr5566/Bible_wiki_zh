Nehemiah’s Prayer

Nehemiah 1:1

These are the words of Nehemiah son of Hacaliah:
Nehemiah records events in which he personally participated, giving the account the character of a faithful memoir. Hacaliah is otherwise unknown, yet Nehemiah’s family background did not prevent God from placing him in a position of great responsibility. His life displays how devoted prayer, courage, and obedience can serve God’s purposes in both foreign courts and covenant communities.

The twentieth year of King Artaxerxes I places these events in the mid-fifth century BC, during the Persian rule of Judah. The temple had already been rebuilt under Zerubbabel, and Ezra had called the returned exiles back to covenant faithfulness, but Jerusalem remained vulnerable and its walls lay in ruins. God’s restoration of His people was still unfinished.

Nehemiah served near the center of imperial power, where his access to Artaxerxes would become essential for Jerusalem’s rebuilding. Hanani was likely Nehemiah’s actual brother and later shared responsibility for Jerusalem’s security (Nehemiah 7:2). His arrival brought a deeply personal and trustworthy report. God often worked through family members in preserving and advancing His purposes, as with Moses and Aaron, and Nehemiah’s burden for Jerusalem was intensified by news carried by someone close to him.

Judah was the heartland of the restored Jewish community and the territory containing Jerusalem. The long journey from Judah to Susa, the Persian royal city where Nehemiah served, showed that the visitors carried news of great importance. Although many Jews had returned from Babylon, the work of restoring the land, the city, and covenant worship remained difficult.

Nehemiah did not regard the returned exiles as forgotten survivors but as the remnant preserved by God. The Lord had judged Judah for persistent sin, yet He had also kept His covenant promise to bring His people back to their land (Jeremiah 29:10–14). Their continued existence testified to God’s faithfulness and prepared the way for further restoration.

Jerusalem was the city chosen for the temple, the center of Israel’s worship, and the city associated with David’s royal line. Its ruined condition represented more than political weakness; it exposed the vulnerability and reproach of God’s covenant people among the nations. Nehemiah’s concern for Jerusalem revealed love for God’s name, God’s people, and the place where the Lord had set His worship.

Nehemiah received Hanani’s report that Jerusalem’s returned exiles were in great trouble and that the city’s walls and gates remained ruined. Though he served far away in the Persian capital of Susa, he regarded the distress of Jerusalem as his own burden. God’s people must not become indifferent to the spiritual weakness, suffering, or reproach borne by the covenant community.

I sat down and wept.
Nehemiah’s tears revealed grief over the dishonor brought upon the city where God had placed His name. Jerusalem’s broken defenses exposed its inhabitants to danger and testified to the continuing effects of sin, judgment, and neglect. His sorrow resembles Jeremiah’s lament for Jerusalem and shows that godly concern begins with a heart moved by the condition of God’s people.

Nehemiah did not treat the report as passing bad news. He continued in grief, humbling himself before the Lord and identifying with the sins and needs of his people. His mourning prepared him to seek God’s mercy and to accept the costly responsibility that would soon be placed before him.

Nehemiah brought his burden to God rather than acting in haste or relying on his influential position in the Persian court. The prayer that follows joins confession, Scripture, intercession, and a plea for divine favor.

Nehemiah worshiped the sovereign Lord who rules above Persia, Jerusalem, and every earthly power. The God of heaven had disciplined Judah through exile, yet He remained able and willing to restore His people according to His promises. Nehemiah’s confidence rested not in ruined walls or royal permission, but in the authority and mercy of God.

Nehemiah’s response to Jerusalem’s devastation began with mourning, fasting, and prayer. Before approaching King Artaxerxes or organizing the rebuilding effort, he brought the burden of God’s people before God Himself. Prayer governed his action because Nehemiah recognized that the restoration of Jerusalem required more than political favor or human resolve.

Nehemiah approached the Lord with reverence, acknowledging His incomparable majesty, holiness, and power. His prayer was rooted in the certainty that the God who had allowed Jerusalem’s judgment was also fully able to bring restoration. Fear of the Lord gave Nehemiah humility without despair.

Nehemiah 1:10

They are Your servants and Your people.
Israel belonged to the Lord by covenant, called to live under His rule and display His holiness among the nations. God had chosen Abraham’s descendants, formed them into a nation, and set them apart as a kingdom of priests (Genesis 12:2; Exodus 19:5–6). Nehemiah appeals to God’s own covenant commitment, recognizing that the returning exiles remained His people despite their sin, judgment, and scattered condition. Believers likewise belong to God through Christ and are called to declare His excellencies as His chosen people (1 Peter 2:9).

Their existence as a nation rested on God’s sovereign intervention rather than human strength. Nehemiah grounds his plea in that historic redemption, trusting that the God who had brought His people out of Egypt could restore them from exile. Israel’s deliverance foreshadowed the greater redemption accomplished by Jesus Christ, who delivers His people from sin and death through His sacrificial blood (Ephesians 1:7).
