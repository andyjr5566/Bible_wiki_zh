The pass became a notable battlefield in Israel’s history, including Joshua’s pursuit of the Amorite kings (Joshua 10:10–11). Its location made it vital for protecting access to Ephraim’s hill country.

Ephraim did not drive out the Canaanites living there (Joshua 16:10), and the city was later captured by Pharaoh and given to Solomon as a wedding gift (1 Kings 9:16). Its history illustrates both the incompleteness of Israel’s early conquest and God’s continuing work through later generations.

Although Manasseh was older, Jacob gave Ephraim the greater blessing, foretelling his future prominence in Israel.

The border of their inheritance went from Ataroth-addar in the east to Upper Beth-horon
Ephraim’s territory occupied an important central section of the land, adjoining Benjamin and Manasseh. Ataroth-addar, meaning “crowns of Addar,” marked a boundary near Benjamin, while Upper Beth-horon, meaning “house of hollows,” stood on a strategic ridge overlooking the route between the coastal plain and the hill country. The Beth-horon pass later became the setting of decisive battles, including the Lord’s victory over the Amorite coalition through Joshua, when hailstones struck Israel’s enemies as they fled down the ascent (Joshua 10:10–11). Ephraim’s central location contributed to its later influence among the northern tribes.

Joshua 16:10

But they did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer.
Gezer guarded an important route between the coastal plain and the hill country, making it a valuable military and commercial center within Ephraim’s allotted territory. Israel subdued the city’s inhabitants but did not carry out the Lord’s command to remove the Canaanite nations from the land (Deuteronomy 7:1–2). What appeared to be a practical compromise left an enduring source of pagan influence among God’s people. Israel’s incomplete obedience repeatedly brought the spiritual consequences warned of in Judges 2:1–3.

The continuing presence of Canaanites in Gezer showed that Ephraim had not fully possessed its inheritance. Living alongside peoples devoted to idolatry exposed Israel to their worship, customs, and alliances, pressures that later contributed to Israel’s unfaithfulness (Judges 3:5–6). God had called Israel to be distinct among the nations, trusting Him rather than accommodating influences that opposed His covenant.
