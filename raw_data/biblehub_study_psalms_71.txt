Be My Rock of Refuge

Psalm 71:1

In You, O LORD, I have taken refuge;
The psalmist rests his safety in the covenant God of Israel, whose faithfulness does not change with age, danger, or opposition. God was not merely a distant source of help but a secure dwelling for those who trusted Him. Cities of refuge provided legal protection for the accused in Israel (Numbers 35:9–15), yet they also pointed beyond themselves to the Lord’s greater protection of those who flee to Him. God remains “our refuge and strength” amid trouble (Psalm 46:1), and believers find final security in Christ.

let me never be put to shame.
The request seeks God’s public vindication against enemies who would regard the psalmist’s trust as foolish or defeated. In a culture where honor and disgrace shaped one’s standing within the community, deliverance from shame meant that God’s righteousness and faithfulness would be openly displayed. Those who wait for the Lord will not ultimately be ashamed (Psalm 25:2–3). Christ endured shame at the cross and now gives His people an enduring honor before God (Hebrews 12:2).

Psalm 71:2

In Your justice,
The psalmist appeals to God’s justice, a central theme in the Bible, reflecting God’s righteous nature. Justice in the biblical sense involves fairness, righteousness, and the upholding of God’s moral order. This plea is rooted in the understanding that God is the ultimate judge who will vindicate the righteous. The concept of divine justice is seen throughout the Old Testament, such as in Deuteronomy 32:4, which describes God as a “God of faithfulness and without iniquity, just and upright is he.”

incline Your ear
This phrase is a metaphorical request for God to listen attentively, indicating the psalmist’s desire for a personal and responsive relationship with God. It reflects the cultural context of ancient Near Eastern practices where a king or deity would be petitioned to hear and respond to a subject’s plea. The imagery of God inclining His ear is found in other Psalms, such as Psalm 17:6, emphasizing God’s willingness to listen to the cries of His people.

Psalm 71:6

The psalmist recalls God’s sustaining care as the foundation of an entire life, not merely help received in a recent crisis. God’s knowledge and purpose extend to the beginning of human life, as Jeremiah was known before birth and David was formed under God’s watchful care in the womb (Jeremiah 1:5; Psalm 139:13–16). Lifelong faith rests on the Lord’s faithfulness, which precedes human strength, understanding, and achievement.

A life received from God and preserved by God finds its proper response in grateful, enduring praise.

David’s afflictions made him an object of astonishment and warning to those around him. Pursued by Saul, opposed by enemies, and repeatedly brought near death, he bore public evidence of both human hostility and God’s preserving hand. His life demonstrated that the Lord’s chosen servant may suffer deeply without being abandoned. Like Isaiah and his children, who were signs in Israel (Isaiah 8:18), David’s trials testified to God’s righteous dealings and faithful deliverance.

God’s people find the same refuge in Him, not as an escape from all trouble, but as steadfast protection through it. Christ fulfills this hope as the sure refuge of all who flee to Him in faith (Hebrews 6:18).

Psalm 71:12

Be not far from me, O God.
God’s nearness is the refuge of those surrounded by trouble. Israel’s covenant Lord had promised His presence to His people, calling them to courage because He would not leave or forsake them (Deuteronomy 31:6). The psalmist appeals to that covenant faithfulness, knowing that God’s presence brings protection, guidance, and comfort even in the darkest danger (Psalm 23:4). Jesus later bore the full anguish of abandonment on the cross, crying words from Psalm 22: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Matthew 27:46). Yet His cry remained an address of faith to “My God,” and His suffering secured lasting help for all who trust in Him.

The request rests not in desperation alone but in confidence that God sees, hears, and acts at the right time. Christ is the final Helper and Deliverer, able to aid those who are tempted and afflicted because He Himself suffered and was tested (Hebrews 2:18).

Israel’s history testified to God’s mighty deeds in creation, the exodus, the preservation of His people, and His judgment of evil. The aged psalmist of Psalm 71 remembers those works as grounds for present confidence. God’s past deliverances reveal His unchanging character and assure His servants that He remains able to save.

Psa 86:10 For You are great and perform wonders; You alone are God.

Who, O God, is like You?
No ruler, idol, angel, or earthly power can compare with the Lord in holiness, authority, mercy, and strength. Moses asked the same question after the Lord delivered Israel through the Red Sea (Exodus 15:11). Worship belongs to God alone because He alone is the Creator, Redeemer, and righteous Judge of all the earth.

Psalm 71:20

Though You have shown me many troubles and misfortunes,
The psalmist recognizes God’s sovereign rule even through seasons of severe affliction. Like Job, he has learned that suffering does not place a servant of God outside His care. Israel’s history likewise revealed that discipline, hardship, and deliverance could serve God’s covenant purposes.

Confidence replaces despair because God has repeatedly restored His people. The immediate hope includes renewed strength and preservation from death, while the wider testimony of Scripture points to the resurrection secured through Christ. God brings life where human strength has failed.

Psalm 71:21

You will increase my honor
The psalmist rests in God’s power to restore the dignity that suffering and enemies had threatened to take away. In Israel’s world, honor involved reputation, security, and public vindication; God’s favor could reverse disgrace and establish His servant before those who opposed him. The confidence is not in personal strength or human influence, but in the Lord who humbles and exalts according to His righteousness. God’s exaltation of the humbled servant anticipates Christ, who suffered humiliation and was afterward exalted by the Father (Philippians 2:8–9).

and comfort me once again.
Past deliverances give the psalmist grounds to seek renewed consolation. God’s comfort includes His sustaining presence, reassurance, and deliverance amid affliction. Repeated trials do not signal the failure of God’s care; they deepen the believer’s dependence upon the Lord whose mercies remain faithful. The God who had comforted His servant before would again bring relief, as Christ invites the weary and burdened to find rest in Him (Matthew 11:28).

Psalm 71:22

So I will praise You with the harp for Your faithfulness, O my God;
The psalmist answers God’s deliverance with public and personal worship. Harps were used by Levite musicians in Israel’s worship, especially after David organized temple music. God’s faithfulness had sustained the psalmist through suffering, enemies, and advancing age, proving that the Lord remains true to His covenant promises.

I will sing praise to You with the lyre,
Music joined the voice of God’s people to thanksgiving, remembrance, and joyful confession. The psalmist offers every available gift in honor of the God who rescues and preserves His servants. Worship becomes the fitting response to a life marked by God’s mercy.

O Holy One of Israel.
Israel’s God is utterly pure, set apart from all false gods, and wholly faithful to His people. Isaiah frequently proclaimed the Holy One of Israel as the Redeemer and King of His covenant people. Jesus Christ, the Holy One, fulfills God’s saving purpose and brings His people near to the holy God.

Psalm 71:24

My tongue will indeed proclaim Your righteousness all day long,
The psalmist pledges continual public praise of God’s righteous character and saving acts. God’s righteousness includes His faithfulness to His promises, His just rule over the nations, and His deliverance of those who trust Him. Such testimony belongs not only in formal worship but throughout daily life, as Israel was commanded to speak of the Lord’s words at home, on the road, morning, and evening (Deuteronomy 6:7). God’s people bear witness to His righteousness in a world that often calls evil good and good evil.

Psa 35:28 Then my tongue will proclaim Your righteousness…

Psa 51:14 Deliver me from bloodguilt, O God, the God of my salvation…

Psa 40:10 I have declared Your faithfulness and salvation;…

for those who seek my harm are disgraced and confounded.
The enemies who plotted the psalmist’s ruin will not finally prevail, because God judges those who oppose His servants. Their shame comes through the exposure and failure of their evil purposes. David’s many deliverances from Saul, foreign foes, and internal rebels illustrate the Lord’s power to preserve His anointed. The pattern reaches its fullness in Christ, whose enemies appeared to triumph at the cross but were defeated through His resurrection. God will ultimately bring every hostile power under the righteous reign of His King.
