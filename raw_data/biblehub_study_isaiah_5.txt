The Song of the Vineyard

Isaiah 5:1

I will sing for my beloved a song of his vineyard:
Isaiah presents God’s case against Israel in the form of a carefully composed song. The beloved is the Lord, and the vineyard is identified later as the house of Israel and the men of Judah (Isaiah 5:7). The love-song setting exposes the tragedy of covenant unfaithfulness: the God who lovingly planted and protected His people must now judge the fruit they have produced. Psalm 80:8–16 likewise portrays Israel as a vine brought from Egypt and planted by God, while Jesus applies the vineyard image to Israel’s leaders in Matthew 21:33–41.

My beloved had a vineyard
God had chosen Israel from among the nations and had given her every covenant privilege needed to live in faith, justice, and holiness. His ownership of the vineyard establishes His rightful expectation of fruit. Israel was not self-made; her land, law, worship, protection, and prosperity came from the Lord’s gracious care. Jeremiah 2:21 recalls that God planted Israel as a choice vine, making her later corruption an act of deliberate rebellion rather than unavoidable failure.

God’s abundant provision heightened the seriousness of the nation’s injustice, idolatry, and refusal to bear the fruit of righteousness.

The Lord addresses the whole community because covenant unfaithfulness was not confined to one class or location.

Woes to the Wicked

Isaiah 5:8

Woe to you who add house to house
Isaiah pronounced God’s judgment on those who enlarged their wealth by acquiring the homes of others. Israel’s land belonged ultimately to the Lord, who assigned it to families as an inheritance and commanded that it not be permanently alienated (Leviticus 25:23–28). Wealthy men who exploited debt, poverty, or legal power to absorb family property defied God’s order for His covenant people.

and join field to field
The expansion of private estates deprived ordinary households of the land needed for food, livelihood, and inheritance. Ahab’s seizure of Naboth’s vineyard revealed the same contempt for God’s law and for a neighbor’s God-given inheritance (1 Kings 21). Greed turned land from a sacred trust into an instrument of oppression.

The powerful continued gathering property until poorer families had no place to live or cultivate. Such injustice destroyed the stability of the community and violated the purpose of the Sabbath and Jubilee provisions, which guarded families from permanent dispossession. God saw not only the outward transactions but also the covetousness that produced them.

Isaiah later foretold abandoned houses and ruined fields as God’s judgment on Judah’s pride and oppression (Isaiah 5:9–10). Possessions gained without righteousness cannot secure life; separation from God’s ways finally brings separation from the people and blessings He intended.
