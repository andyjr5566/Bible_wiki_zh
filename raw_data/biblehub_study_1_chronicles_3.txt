The Descendants of David

1 Chronicles 3:1

These were the sons of David who were born to him in Hebron:
David reigned over Judah from Hebron for seven and a half years after being anointed king there (2 Samuel 2:1–4; 5:4–5). Hebron stood in the hill country of Judah and was closely associated with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, whose burial place was nearby (Genesis 23:19; 49:29–32). The record of David’s sons establishes the royal line through which God would preserve His covenant promise. Though David’s household would suffer deeply because of sin, the Lord remained faithful to His purpose of bringing forth the promised King from David’s line (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Isaiah 11:1; Matthew 1:1).

The firstborn was Amnon by Ahinoam of Jezreel;
Amnon, David’s firstborn, possessed the natural expectation of succession, yet his life revealed the moral disorder that would trouble David’s family. Ahinoam came from Jezreel, likely the town in Judah’s hill country. Amnon’s violence against his half-sister Tamar and his later death at Absalom’s command brought grief and division into David’s house (2 Samuel 13). David’s sins did not nullify God’s covenant, but the consequences foretold by Nathan began to unfold within his own family (2 Samuel 12:10–12).

the second was Daniel by Abigail of Carmel;
Daniel, meaning “God is my judge,” is called Chileab in the parallel genealogy of 2 Samuel 3:3. His mother Abigail had been the discerning wife of Nabal of Carmel, whose wise intervention restrained David from shedding innocent blood (1 Samuel 25:32–35). After Nabal’s death, David took Abigail as his wife. Daniel receives no further narrative in Scripture, and his absence from later events may indicate that he died before any question of succession arose. His place in the genealogy nevertheless bears witness to the historical reality of David’s household and the unfolding line of the kingdom God established.

1 Chronicles 3:2

the third was Absalom the son of Maacah daughter of King Talmai of Geshur;
Absalom was born to David through a marriage that likely strengthened relations with Geshur, an Aramean kingdom northeast of the Sea of Galilee. After murdering his half-brother Amnon for violating Tamar, Absalom fled to Talmai in Geshur for three years (2 Samuel 13:37–38). His later return to Jerusalem led to a calculated rebellion against David, fulfilling Nathan’s warning that violence and conflict would arise within David’s own house because of his sin (2 Samuel 12:10–12). Absalom’s death ended his revolt, yet it also displayed David’s enduring grief over a son who rejected both his father and the Lord’s appointed king.

the fourth was Adonijah the son of Haggith;
Adonijah was David’s eldest surviving son near the end of David’s life and sought to seize the throne for himself. Like Absalom, he gathered chariots, horsemen, and influential supporters while exalting himself as king (1 Kings 1:5–7). His attempt failed because the Lord had designated Solomon to succeed David, a choice David publicly confirmed through Solomon’s anointing. Adonijah’s ambition revealed the continuing disorder within David’s family, while Solomon’s accession upheld God’s covenant purpose for the Davidic line. The promised eternal King would come not through self-assertion or political intrigue, but through God’s sovereign appointment in Jesus Christ.

1 Chronicles 3:3

Shephatiah, meaning “Yahweh has judged” or “Yahweh is a judge,” was born during David’s reign in Hebron, when David’s household was growing alongside his kingdom (2 Samuel 3:4). Abital, whose name means “my father is dew,” appears nowhere else in Scripture, yet her inclusion preserves the historical record of David’s family. David’s many marriages reflected royal customs of the age, but they also contributed to the later tensions and divisions within his house. Shephatiah did not stand in the line through which the throne and messianic promise continued, yet he remained part of the covenant king’s family.

Ithream was the last of the six sons born to David at Hebron before David established his rule in Jerusalem (2 Samuel 3:5). 1 Chronicles 3:4

These six sons were born to David in Hebron,
David’s time in Hebron marks the beginning of his reign as king over Judah. Hebron, a significant city in the tribal territory of Judah, was a city of refuge and a Levitical city. It was also the burial place of the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, which added to its importance. The six sons born to David in Hebron were Amnon, Daniel, Absalom, Adonijah, Shephatiah, and Ithream. Each of these sons played a role in the unfolding narrative of David’s family and kingdom, with some, like Absalom and Adonijah, later challenging David’s rule. This period in Hebron was foundational for David’s consolidation of power and the establishment of his dynasty.

And David reigned in Jerusalem thirty-three years,
After his reign in Hebron, David moved his capital to Jerusalem, where he reigned for thirty-three years. Jerusalem, a Jebusite city, was strategically located and became the political and spiritual center of Israel. David’s capture of Jerusalem and establishment of it as the capital is a pivotal moment in biblical history, as it became the City of David and later the site of Solomon’s Temple. The number thirty-three is often seen as significant in biblical numerology, symbolizing a complete and divinely appointed period. David’s reign in Jerusalem solidified his legacy and set the stage for the future of Israel, with Jerusalem remaining central in biblical prophecy and typology, often seen as a type of the eternal kingdom of Christ.

Amaziah his son,
Amaziah succeeded Joash as king of Judah and initially followed the Lord, though not with an undivided heart. He obeyed the law by sparing the children of his father’s assassins, and the Lord granted him victory over Edom after he dismissed Israelite mercenaries in obedience to God’s word. Yet Amaziah later worshiped Edom’s defeated gods and proudly challenged Jehoash of Israel. Judah was defeated, Jerusalem was plundered, and Amaziah’s reign ended in conspiracy and death at Lachish. His life shows that outward obedience cannot replace steadfast devotion to the Lord.

Azariah his son,
Azariah, also called Uzziah, became king after Amaziah and reigned for fifty-two years. As long as he sought the Lord, God gave him success against surrounding enemies and enabled Judah to grow in military strength, agriculture, and commerce. His prosperity became a snare when he entered the temple to burn incense, a duty reserved for Aaron’s priestly descendants. The Lord struck him with leprosy, and he lived separated from public life while Jotham governed in his place. Uzziah’s judgment warns that no position or success permits a person to disregard God’s appointed order.

Though the people of Judah continued in corrupt practices, Jotham’s faithful reign preserved a testimony that personal obedience to God remains possible even amid national spiritual decline.

The Royal Line After the Exile

1 Chronicles 3:17

The descendants of Jeconiah the captive:
Jeconiah, also called Jehoiachin, was carried to Babylon by Nebuchadnezzar in 597 B.C. after reigning in Jerusalem for only three months. His captivity fulfilled Jeremiah’s warnings against Judah’s persistent rebellion (Jeremiah 22:24–30). Though no descendant of Jeconiah would again rule over Judah from David’s throne, the royal line was not extinguished. God preserved the house of David through exile, keeping His covenant purposes alive when the kingdom itself had fallen.

1 Chronicles 3:19

The sons of Pedaiah: Zerubbabel and Shimei.
Zerubbabel emerged as the leading Davidic prince of the restoration period, governing Judah under Persian authority after the Babylonian exile. Ezra records his leadership in returning exiles and rebuilding the temple, while Haggai and Zechariah portray him as God’s chosen servant in renewing worship at Jerusalem. Although other passages identify Zerubbabel as the son of Shealtiel, the genealogical relationship may reflect a legal or family connection within the Davidic house. His place in David’s line preserved the royal hope that God’s promised King would yet come.
