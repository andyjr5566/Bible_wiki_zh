Psalm 115:1

Not to us, O LORD, not to us,
Israel’s deliverance, preservation, and prosperity belonged to the Lord rather than to human strength, kings, armies, or wisdom. The doubled plea rejects self-exaltation and acknowledges that God alone rescues His people. Such humility stands against pride, which God opposes, and reflects dependence upon His grace (James 4:6).

Psalm 115:2

Why should the nations say,
Israel’s distress gave surrounding peoples occasion to ridicule the God of the covenant. In the ancient world, military defeat was often treated as proof that a nation’s god had been overcome or had abandoned his people. Yet the Lord’s faithfulness was never measured by Israel’s immediate circumstances. His dealings with his people included discipline for sin, preservation in judgment, and eventual deliverance for the sake of his name.

“Where is their God?”
The nations demanded visible evidence because their gods were represented by images and shrines. Yahweh had forbidden Israel to make an image of him, for he is the living Creator, not a work of human hands. Though unseen, he acts in history, hears his people, and rules from heaven. Israel’s hope rested in the Lord’s covenant promises, ultimately revealed in Jesus Christ, the image of the invisible God, through whom God made his saving presence known.

Heaven is His throne and earth His footstool (Isaiah 66:1), declaring His supreme authority, holiness, and independence from all created things.

Psalm 115:4

Their idols are silver and gold,
The nations fashioned images from precious metals that displayed wealth, artistry, and royal prestige, yet no material value could give them life or deity. Scripture repeatedly exposes the folly of assigning divine honor to objects drawn from God’s own creation and shaped for human use (Isaiah 44:9–20). Silver and gold could adorn an idol, but they could not enable it to see, hear, speak, or save.

made by the hands of men.
Idols depend entirely upon the craftsmen who design, carry, protect, and repair them. In contrast, the Lord made heaven and earth and is not dependent on anything made by human hands. The commandment against images and their worship guards God’s people from exchanging the Creator for created things (Exodus 20:3–4; Jeremiah 10:3–5). Jesus Christ is not a human invention or a lifeless representation of God; He is the eternal Word who became flesh and reveals the Father perfectly (John 1:14; Colossians 1:15).

In contrast, the Lord spoke creation into being and made Himself known to Israel through His commands, prophets, and covenant promises. The prophets likewise exposed the absurdity of worshiping objects made by human hands (Isaiah 44:13–20; Jeremiah 10:3–5). God’s final and fullest revelation is found in His Son, the living Word (John 1:1, 14).

Psalm 115:8

Those who make them become like them,
The idols described in Psalm 115:4–7 have mouths, eyes, ears, noses, hands, and feet, yet cannot speak, see, hear, smell, touch, walk, or make a sound. Those who fashion such images devote themselves to what is powerless and become spiritually insensible in return. Israel repeatedly encountered this danger amid the idolatry of surrounding nations, exchanging the glory of the living God for objects made by human hands. Worship molds the worshiper: devotion to lifeless gods produces futile thinking, moral blindness, and hearts unable to respond rightly to God.

as do all who trust in them.
Trust in an idol transfers hope, security, and allegiance from the Creator to created things. Isaiah exposed the folly of a man using part of a tree for warmth and food while bowing before another part as his god (Isaiah 44:9–20). Such trust leaves people without help in the day of need, for idols cannot save, speak, or act. The psalm contrasts them with the Lord, who remembers His people, blesses them, and reigns over heaven and earth. Believers are therefore called to flee every form of idolatry and place their full confidence in the living God.

Israel was called to rely on the covenant God rather than idols, military power, or foreign alliances. The Lord had redeemed them from Egypt, sustained them in the wilderness, and given them the land according to His promises. In contrast to lifeless idols that cannot speak, see, hear, or act, the God of Israel alone is living and able to save. Such trust required exclusive allegiance to Him, as commanded in the Law.

Sheol is portrayed as a realm of silence and separation from earthly service, where the dead no longer join the congregation in declaring the LORD’s covenant faithfulness. Psalm 6:5 and Isaiah 38:18 similarly stress the urgency of honoring God during earthly life. The living are entrusted with the privilege of worship, obedience, and witness.
