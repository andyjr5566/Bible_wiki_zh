Isaiah 38:6

And I will deliver you and this city from the hand of the king of Assyria.
God joined Hezekiah’s personal preservation to Jerusalem’s rescue during Sennacherib’s invasion of Judah. Assyria had already destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and subdued much of Judah, making Jerusalem’s survival humanly improbable. Yet the Lord alone would break the power of the empire that exalted itself against Him. The deliverance fulfilled God’s covenant commitment to David’s house and preserved the city where His temple stood. In answer to Hezekiah’s prayer, the Lord later struck the Assyrian army and compelled Sennacherib to return home in defeat (Isaiah 37:36–37). Israel’s earlier rescue from Egypt stood behind this promise: the God who redeemed His people from Pharaoh remained able to save them from every proud ruler. God’s merciful intervention also points forward to the greater deliverance accomplished by Christ, who rescues His people from sin, death, and the dominion of darkness.

God’s defense upheld His promise that David’s line would not fail, a promise ultimately fulfilled in Jesus Christ, David’s greater Son and eternal King. Hezekiah could rest in the Lord’s protection amid a threat no earthly power could overcome. The stairway of Ahaz was likely a stepped structure used to observe the sun’s shadow and mark the passing of the day. Associated with Hezekiah’s father, it provided a public and unmistakable setting for the sign God promised through Isaiah. The shadow’s reversal was not an illusion or a private experience but a visible alteration of the normal course of nature, confirming that the Lord would heal Hezekiah and add fifteen years to his life (Isaiah 38:5–7). God did not reveal the physical means by which He accomplished the wonder; He revealed His sovereign power over the created order. As He had divided the sea for Israel and later caused the sun to stand still in Joshua’s day, the Lord demonstrated that all creation serves His purposes.

Hezekiah portrays his earthly life as a tent suddenly dismantled and carried away. Facing a deadly illness, Hezekiah recognized the frailty of human existence and the certainty that life does not rest in human control. Paul later used the image of an earthly tent to describe the mortal body, contrasting it with the eternal dwelling God provides for His people (2 Corinthians 5:1).
