Solomon led Israel’s leaders and representatives to Gibeon in a public act of worship, joining the nation’s political life to its covenant obligations before the LORD. Gibeon, a Benjaminite city once inhabited by the Gibeonites, had become Israel’s principal worship center because the tabernacle and bronze altar were there. Although high places later became associated with idolatry, worship at Gibeon was proper at this time because God’s appointed sanctuary remained there.

Solomon’s Riches

2 Chronicles 1:14

Solomon accumulated 1,400 chariots and 12,000 horses,
Solomon’s resources displayed the peace, prosperity, and international influence granted to him by God. Chariots represented the most formidable military equipment of the ancient world, while large numbers of horses required extensive wealth, trade networks, trained personnel, and provisions. Yet the king’s growing military establishment also stood in tension with Deuteronomy 17:16, which warned Israel’s kings against multiplying horses and returning to Egypt for them. God had given Solomon security, but military strength could never replace covenant faithfulness.

Solomon established strategic centers for housing, maintaining, and deploying his forces. Cities such as Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer guarded important routes through the land and helped secure Israel’s borders and trade. The organization of these cities reveals an orderly administration capable of supporting a powerful kingdom. Such strength served Israel well in Solomon’s day, though later generations would learn that fortifications and weapons could not preserve a nation that abandoned the Lord.

2 Chronicles 1:15

Solomon’s God-given wisdom brought wealth beyond what he had requested, fulfilling the Lord’s promise to grant him riches and honor as well as wisdom (1 Kings 3:10–13). Jerusalem became the center of an international kingdom whose trade, tribute, and administration supplied extraordinary resources. Yet such abundance also carried responsibility, since Israel’s kings were warned not to multiply wealth in a way that displaced trust in the Lord (Deuteronomy 17:17). The splendor of Solomon’s reign anticipates the greater glory of God’s coming kingdom, where the New Jerusalem displays divine holiness and abundance without corruption or idolatry (Revelation 21:18–21).

A chariot could be imported from Egypt for six hundred shekels of silver,
Egypt was widely known for its horses and chariot forces, making its military equipment highly prized throughout the ancient Near East. The considerable price of a chariot reveals both its advanced construction and its value as an instrument of royal power. Solomon’s kingdom possessed sufficient wealth and commercial influence to obtain such resources on a large scale.

Horses supplied the mobility required for chariots and strengthened a king’s military capacity. Yet Israel’s kings had been warned not to multiply horses or return the people to Egypt for them (Deuteronomy 17:16). Solomon’s prosperity displayed God’s promised blessing, but his growing dependence on military resources and Egyptian trade also foreshadowed spiritual compromises that would mark his later reign.

Solomon’s merchants became intermediaries between Egypt and the rulers of the northern kingdoms. Trade in chariots and horses extended Israel’s influence, enriched the kingdom, and established diplomatic connections across the region. The record demonstrates the remarkable peace and prosperity granted during Solomon’s early reign, while also showing how wealth, alliances, and military strength could become sources of misplaced confidence.
