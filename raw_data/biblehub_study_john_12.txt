Belief and Unbelief

John 12:37

Although Jesus had performed so many signs in their presence,
Jesus’ works were public demonstrations of His identity and authority. John records signs such as the healing of the official’s son, the feeding of the five thousand, the giving of sight to a man born blind, and the raising of Lazarus. Many in Jerusalem had heard of or witnessed Lazarus’s resurrection, yet the religious leaders responded by plotting against both Lazarus and Jesus (John 11:45–53; 12:9–11). These miracles fulfilled Old Testament expectations that the Messiah would open blind eyes, heal the lame, and bring God’s saving power near (Isaiah 35:5–6). The evidence was sufficient, but miracles alone could not overcome hearts set against God.

they still did not believe in Him.
Israel’s rejection fulfilled Isaiah’s lament: “Lord, who has believed our report?” (Isaiah 53:1; John 12:38). Their unbelief resembled the generation that saw God’s deliverance from Egypt and yet rebelled in the wilderness (Numbers 14:11), as well as Pharaoh’s resistance before the signs of Moses. Jesus had revealed the Father’s glory, but many preferred human approval and the security of established religious power to confession of Him as the Christ (John 12:42–43). The following verses show that such unbelief also involved divine judgment, as God gave hardened hearts over to the blindness they had chosen. Saving faith requires more than seeing God’s works; it requires the gracious opening of the heart to receive the Son.

John 12:42

Nevertheless, many of the leaders believed in Him.
Jesus’ public signs and teaching persuaded some members of Israel’s ruling class that He was the promised Messiah. These leaders likely included members of the Sanhedrin, where Pharisees, chief priests, and other influential men governed religious and civic matters. Nicodemus came to Jesus acknowledging that His works testified to God’s presence (John 3:1–2), and Joseph of Arimathea later identified himself openly by caring for Jesus’ body after the crucifixion (John 19:38–39). Their faith stood against the prevailing rejection described earlier in the chapter and demonstrated that God was revealing His Son even within Israel’s highest councils.

Public allegiance to Jesus threatened the authority of those who opposed Him. The Pharisees had substantial influence in the synagogues and among the people, and they regarded Jesus’ teaching as a challenge to their traditions and leadership. Private conviction without public confession revealed the conflict between faith and fear. Jesus had taught that those who acknowledge Him before men will be acknowledged before the Father, while those who deny Him will face judgment (Matthew 10:32–33).

Exclusion from the synagogue meant more than losing access to a place of worship. It brought separation from the religious and social life of the Jewish community, often carrying disgrace and practical hardship. The parents of the man born blind had already feared this penalty because the Jewish authorities had agreed to expel anyone who confessed Jesus as the Christ (John 9:22). Such fear exposed the cost of following Christ in a hostile setting and anticipated the opposition His disciples would face. Faith in Him calls for allegiance that values God’s approval above acceptance, status, or security among men.
