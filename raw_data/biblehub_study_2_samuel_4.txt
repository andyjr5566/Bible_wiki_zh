Abner had established Ish-bosheth’s rule over the northern tribes after Saul’s death and had supplied the military strength and political influence that sustained his throne. Hebron, where Abner died, was David’s royal city in Judah and a city of refuge appointed by God. Abner’s death there removed the strongest human support behind Saul’s remaining dynasty while David’s kingdom continued to stand according to God’s purpose.

The Execution of Rechab and Baanah

2 Samuel 4:9

But David answered Rechab and his brother Baanah, the sons of Rimmon the Beerothite,
Rechab and Baanah had served as captains under Ish-bosheth, Saul’s surviving son and David’s rival king. Their murder of Ish-bosheth was an attempt to secure advantage with David, but David refused to build his kingdom through treachery. Beeroth belonged to the Gibeonite cities and was associated with Benjamin, Saul’s tribe, placing these men within the tangled political divisions that followed Saul’s death. David held them accountable not merely as political enemies but as murderers who had killed a defenseless man in his own home.

2 Samuel 4:12

So David commanded his young men, and they killed Rechab and Baanah.
David refused to establish his kingdom through murder, even when the victims were political rivals. Rechab and Baanah expected favor for removing Ish-bosheth, but David treated their deed as the wicked killing of an innocent man in his own house and bed. As with the Amalekite who claimed to have killed Saul, David upheld justice rather than exploiting another man’s death for personal advantage. The judgment also vindicated Ish-bosheth as Saul’s son and rejected the treachery that had weakened Saul’s house.

The public punishment showed that murder and betrayal would not be tolerated under David’s rule. The pool at Hebron was a prominent gathering place, so the display served as a visible warning to all who might seek power through violence. David’s reign began in Hebron, and justice publicly administered there showed that his authority was accountable to God rather than driven by political expediency.
