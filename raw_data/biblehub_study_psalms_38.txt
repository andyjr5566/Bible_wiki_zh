Do Not Rebuke Me in Your Anger

Psalm 38:1

A Psalm of David, for remembrance.
David brings his suffering and sin before God with the confidence of one who knows the Lord’s covenant faithfulness. The psalm preserves a testimony of repentance, affliction, and dependence upon divine mercy. Its use for remembrance calls God’s people to recall both the seriousness of sin and the Lord’s past deliverance of those who humble themselves before Him.

O LORD, do not rebuke me in Your anger
David appeals to Yahweh, the covenant God of Israel, rather than withdrawing in fear. He does not deny God’s right to correct him, but pleads that correction would be governed by mercy rather than the full weight of righteous anger. David’s request accords with the confidence later expressed in Psalm 103:8–10: the Lord is compassionate and does not deal with His repentant people according to their sins.

God’s discipline is necessary for His children, yet David recognizes that sin deserves judgment beyond what he could bear. His plea anticipates the mercy revealed fully in Christ, who bore the wrath deserved by sinners so that those who repent and trust Him may receive correction as loving fatherly discipline rather than condemnation.

Psalm 38:2

For Your arrows have pierced me deeply,
David portrays God’s rebuke as arrows that penetrate beyond outward circumstances into the conscience. Job similarly described the arrows of the Almighty as producing deep anguish (Job 6:4). The wound exposes sin and brings the psalmist to humble repentance rather than self-defense. God’s discipline is grievous, yet it belongs to His fatherly care for those He loves (Hebrews 12:6).

God’s powerful hand has made David keenly aware of the burden and consequences of his sin. David used similar language when he confessed that God’s hand was heavy upon him until he acknowledged his transgression (Psalm 32:4–5). The weight of deserved judgment also directs attention to Christ, who bore sin’s penalty on the cross so that repentant believers might receive forgiveness and peace with God.

Psalm 38:3

The psalmist recognizes that his affliction stands under God’s righteous displeasure against sin. God’s anger is not arbitrary but reflects His holiness and His opposition to evil. Physical suffering is not always the direct result of personal sin, yet David understands that his present distress is bound to his guilt before God and cannot be relieved apart from repentance.

Like the unconfessed guilt described in Psalm 32:3–4, his sin has become a heavy burden that drains strength and peace. The psalm leads toward honest confession and dependence on God’s mercy, fulfilled ultimately in Christ, who bore the judgment sin deserves and gives rest to those who come to Him.

Psalm 38:4

For my iniquities have overwhelmed me;
David confesses that his own sins have risen over him like floodwaters, leaving him unable to escape their guilt or consequences. Psalm 38 joins physical suffering, divine discipline, and personal repentance, refusing to treat sin as a minor fault. Israel’s repeated sacrifices testified that sin required atonement, yet they could not finally remove the conscience’s burden. All people share this need, for all have sinned and fall short of God’s glory (Romans 3:23).

Sin becomes an unbearable load when God awakens the conscience to its offense against Him. David’s helplessness points beyond human effort to the mercy God alone can provide. Jesus Christ bore sins in His body on the cross (1 Peter 2:24), fulfilling what the sacrificial system anticipated. He calls burdened sinners to come to Him for rest and forgiveness (Matthew 11:28–30).

Psalm 38:5

My wounds are foul and festering
David portrays his suffering as open, decaying wounds, outward evidence of the heavy discipline described earlier in the psalm. Sin corrupts what God made whole and brings misery into every part of life. The image recalls Isaiah’s depiction of a people spiritually diseased from head to foot (Isaiah 1:5–6). David does not hide his ruin from the Lord but brings it into the light of confession.

because of my sinful folly.
David accepts responsibility rather than blaming his enemies, circumstances, or bodily weakness. His folly was moral rebellion against God’s wisdom, and its consequences had become grievous. Such confession prepares the way for mercy, for the Lord does not despise the repentant heart (Psalm 51:17). David’s wounds also point forward to Christ, who was without sin yet bore the judgment and grief produced by human sin, being wounded for the transgressions of His people (Isaiah 53:5).

Psalm 38:6

I am bent and brought low;
David portrays himself as crushed beneath the weight of God’s chastening and the consequences of his sin. Earlier he describes God’s arrows piercing him and his iniquities rising over his head (Psalm 38:2–4). His bowed posture reveals inward humiliation as well as bodily affliction. Job likewise knew the misery of physical suffering and public humiliation (Job 30:16–17). God receives the contrite rather than the self-exalting, and a broken spirit remains the fitting posture of one seeking mercy (Psalm 51:17).

all day long I go about mourning.
Sorrow governs David’s daily life, not merely a moment of regret. In Israel, mourning commonly involved visible signs of grief, including torn garments, sackcloth, ashes, and public lamentation (Genesis 37:34; 2 Samuel 13:19). David’s grief arises from sin, suffering, and estrangement from others, yet he continues to bring his condition before the Lord. God draws near to the brokenhearted and does not despise those crushed in spirit (Psalm 34:18). Jesus later promised comfort to those who mourn before God (Matthew 5:4).

Psalm 38:7

For my loins are full of burning pain,
David describes suffering that has reached the center of bodily strength and vitality. The burning pain accords with his earlier confession that God’s arrows have pierced him and that his sins have gone over his head (Psalm 38:2, 4). His affliction is not treated as meaningless misery; it drives him to acknowledge the heavy burden of sin and to seek the Lord’s mercy. Bodily suffering can expose human frailty and awaken the conscience to the seriousness of rebellion against God.

David portrays himself as weakened throughout, without health, relief, or inner peace. His condition recalls Isaiah’s picture of a people wounded from head to foot because of sin (Isaiah 1:5–6). Yet Psalm 38 does not end in despair. David waits for the Lord, confident that God hears the cry of the repentant and does not forsake those who confess their need (Psalm 38:15, 21–22).

Earlier he acknowledges that his iniquities have gone over his head and become too heavy to bear. Bodily weakness, mental anguish, and spiritual conviction converge in a suffering that he cannot overcome by his own strength. His condition anticipates the greater suffering of Christ, who was crushed for the iniquities of His people and bore the judgment sinners deserved.

David brings his deepest longings openly before the covenant God, knowing that the Lord searches the heart and sees what human eyes cannot discern. Neither unspoken need nor righteous hope escapes God’s knowledge. Such openness rests on God’s faithful care for those who seek Him.

David’s lament also points forward to Christ, the Man of Sorrows, whose suffering was fully known to the Father and whose intercession brings needy sinners before God.

Psalm 38:11

My beloved and friends shun my disease,
Severe affliction has driven even the psalmist’s closest companions away. Illness could bring fear of contagion, ritual impurity, or the suspicion that God’s judgment rested upon the sufferer. Leviticus 13–14 required separation in cases of certain skin diseases, illustrating how physical suffering could result in social exclusion. Job likewise mourned that relatives, friends, and servants withdrew from him in his misery (Job 19:13–19). David’s lament anticipates the rejection endured by Christ, who was despised and forsaken by men as He bore the griefs and sorrows of His people (Isaiah 53:3–4).

and my kinsmen stand at a distance.
Those bound to David by family loyalty remain aloof when he most needs comfort. In Israel, kinsmen ordinarily bore responsibilities of protection, support, and redemption; their distance therefore deepens the sorrow of his condition. David knew betrayal and division within his own household, and Scripture records similar wounds in Joseph’s rejection by his brothers. The abandonment of human support directs the suffering believer to the Lord, who does not withdraw from His afflicted servants. Jesus also experienced desertion when His disciples fled at His arrest (Mark 14:50), yet He remained faithful to accomplish salvation.

David confesses his weakness before God and the danger surrounding him. Affliction, opposition, and the burden of sin have brought him near collapse. Psalm 73:2 records a similar awareness of nearly slipping from faithful trust. Such honesty is not despair but humility, for the one who knows his frailty is driven to seek the Lord’s sustaining mercy. Jesus, though without sin, entered genuine human weakness and sorrow, praying in Gethsemane with complete dependence upon the Father (Matthew 26:38–39).

Psalm 38:18

David refuses to conceal his guilt before the holy God. Confession brings sin into the light and agrees with God’s judgment against it. Under the law, confessed sin required atonement through the sacrifices God appointed; those sacrifices pointed forward to Christ, whose one offering provides the cleansing they could only foreshadow. Genuine repentance does not excuse wrongdoing but acknowledges personal responsibility and seeks mercy.

David’s distress is not mere fear of suffering, but grief over the guilt that has disrupted fellowship with the Lord. Such godly sorrow leads the sinner to seek the forgiveness and restoration that God gives through His grace.
