The Parable of the Ten Virgins

Matthew 25:1

“At that time
Jesus directs attention to the period surrounding His return, following His warnings about watchfulness and faithful service in Matthew 24. The parable belongs to His teaching about the end of the age, when outward profession will be tested by genuine readiness for the coming King.

The virgins represent a bridal party awaiting the wedding procession. Their shared appearance and common purpose show that people may be closely associated with the visible community of faith while differing profoundly in inward spiritual preparedness.

The bridegroom portrays Christ, whose return will be personal, visible, and decisive. Like attendants waiting for the arrival of the bridegroom in a Jewish wedding, His people are called to live with alert expectation, prepared to welcome Him at any hour.

Matthew 25:2

Five of them were foolish,
The foolish virgins anticipated the bridegroom’s coming but failed to make adequate provision for delay. Their condition pictures those who profess expectation of Christ’s return while neglecting the enduring faith, obedience, and watchfulness that readiness requires. Scripture consistently joins wisdom with reverence for God and submission to His instruction (Prov. 1:7; 9:10).

Pro 17:21 A man fathers a fool to his own grief;…

and five were wise.
The wise virgins prepared beforehand and remained ready when the bridegroom arrived. Their wisdom reflects the disciple who hears Christ’s words and acts on them (Matt. 7:24–27). Christ’s return will be certain, though its timing is not disclosed; therefore, faithful believers are called to persevere in readiness rather than presume upon future opportunity.

Matthew 25:3

The foolish ones
Five of the virgins lacked the wisdom that fears God and lives in readiness for the Bridegroom. Though included among the wedding party, they failed to prepare for the delayed arrival that Jesus foretold.

Mat 7:26 But everyone who hears these words of Mine…

took their lamps
Each virgin carried a lamp, showing an outward expectation of the bridegroom’s coming. The lamps provided light for joining the nighttime procession into the wedding feast, portraying a professed readiness to receive Christ when He returns.

Luk 12:35 Be dressed for service and keep your lamps burning.

Mat 5:15 Neither do people light a lamp and put it under a basket.…

Luk 8:16 No one lights a lamp and covers it with a jar…

but did not take along any extra oil.
Their failure was not a lack of initial participation but a lack of enduring preparation. Oil sustained the lamp through the night; without a reserve, their lamps could not remain useful when the bridegroom arrived. The parable warns that outward profession cannot replace persevering faith and watchful obedience.

Matthew 25:4

But the wise ones
The wise virgins represent those who are spiritually prepared for the bridegroom’s arrival. Their wisdom is shown not by knowledge alone but by prudent action, reflecting the biblical pattern of fearing God, obeying His word, and living in expectation of His purposes.

Eph 5:15-16 Pay careful attention, then, to how you walk…

Pro 14:8 The wisdom of the prudent is to discern his way…

Pro 27:12 The prudent see danger and take cover…

took oil in flasks
They anticipated delay and made provision beyond immediate need. Oil enabled their lamps to continue burning through the night, illustrating the enduring readiness required of those who await Christ. Oil is often associated in Scripture with anointing and the work of the Holy Spirit, though the central emphasis remains personal preparedness that cannot be borrowed from others at the last moment.

Lamps were necessary for participation in a nighttime wedding procession, when the bridegroom’s arrival could come at an unexpected hour. The wise virgins possessed both the visible lamps and the needed supply of oil, portraying a faith that is not merely outward but sustained and ready for the Lord’s return.

Matthew 25:5

When the bridegroom was delayed,
The bridegroom’s arrival at the bride’s home could not be fixed by the attendants, and the waiting period tested whether they had made proper provision for the night. Jesus portrays His return as certain, though its timing remains undisclosed. The period between His ascension and return is not a failure of promise but an appointed season of mercy, in which God calls sinners to repentance and His people are to remain ready (2 Pet. 3:9).

2Pe 3:9 The Lord is not slow in keeping His promise…

they all became drowsy
Both the wise and foolish virgins experienced the weariness of prolonged waiting. Ordinary human weakness is not the central issue; the distinction lies in whether they possessed the oil needed when the cry announced the bridegroom’s approach. Long expectation can expose complacency, making continual faith, obedience, and dependence upon God essential (Romans 13:11).

Pro 24:33-34 A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding…

Rom 13:11-12 And do this, understanding the occasion. The hour has come…

and fell asleep.
The virgins’ sleep heightens the suddenness of the midnight summons. Christ’s return will interrupt the normal course of life and reveal the reality of each person’s preparation. Believers may rest from daily labor, but they must not live unprepared for the Lord’s appearing, remaining spiritually sober and watchful until He comes (1 Thess. 5:6).

Matthew 25:6

The bridegroom arrived at an hour when the attendants did not expect him, underscoring Jesus’ warning that His return will come at an unknown time. Midnight heightens the urgency of readiness amid spiritual darkness and delay.

Jesus is the Bridegroom who comes for His people. The image recalls His teaching that the bridegroom would one day be taken away and points forward to the marriage supper of the Lamb.

The attendants were expected to join the bridegroom’s procession with lamps prepared and burning. Christ’s people must be ready to receive Him with persevering faith, for His coming will separate those who are prepared from those who are not.

Matthew 25:7

Then all the virgins woke up
The midnight cry brings every attendant to immediate attention as the bridegroom arrives. Both the wise and foolish virgins had expected his coming, yet the delay revealed the difference between outward association with the wedding party and genuine readiness. Christ’s return will awaken every false security and expose the true condition of each person. Believers are therefore commanded to remain spiritually alert and sober-minded while awaiting the day of the Lord (1 Thessalonians 5:6).

Eph 5:14 So it is said: “Wake up, O sleeper, rise up from the dead…

Rom 13:11 And do this, understanding the occasion. The hour has come…

1Th 5:6 So then, let us not sleep as the others do,…

and trimmed their lamps.
Preparing a lamp involved removing the charred portion of the wick so that it would burn clearly and brightly. The action shows that readiness includes active, continuing care rather than a past profession alone. God’s Word directs the believer’s path as a lamp gives light (Psalm 119:105), and a faithful life must be sustained by the inward reality symbolized by the oil. When the bridegroom comes, preparation cannot be borrowed from others; each person must be ready to meet Christ.

Matthew 25:8

The foolish ones said to the wise,
The five foolish virgins had taken lamps but had not brought the oil needed to sustain them through the delay. Their failure was not ignorance of the bridegroom’s coming, but neglect in preparing for it. Jesus contrasts them with the wise virgins, who expected delay and made provision. The distinction recalls His earlier warning that those who hear His words must also act on them, like the wise builder who built on the rock (Matthew 7:24–27).

Pro 13:20 He who walks with the wise will become wise,…

‘Give us some of your oil;
The request came when the bridegroom’s arrival exposed their lack. Oil sustained the lamps used in wedding processions and fittingly represents the inward spiritual readiness required for entrance into the kingdom. The wise virgins could not transfer their supply, because no one can borrow another person’s faith, obedience, repentance, or relationship with the Lord. Each person must be ready to meet Christ.

our lamps are going out.’
Their lamps had burned for a time, but without a reserve of oil they could not continue through the night. The fading light reveals the danger of outward profession without enduring spiritual life. God’s Word is a lamp for His people’s path (Psalm 119:105), and believers are called to remain watchful because the Son of Man will come at an hour not expected (Matthew 24:42–44).

Matthew 25:9

The wise virgins recognize that readiness for the bridegroom cannot be transferred from one person to another. The parable distinguishes outward association with the wedding party from the inward preparedness required to meet Christ. Faithful endurance, genuine faith, and a life prepared for the Lord cannot be borrowed at the moment of His appearing.

The limited oil exposes the folly of delayed preparation. Each virgin is responsible to be ready, for no believer can supply another with saving faith or substitute obedience. Jesus elsewhere warned that many who profess His name will be excluded because they never truly knew Him or did the Father’s will (Matthew 7:21–23).

Matthew 25:10

This phrase highlights the urgency and unpreparedness of the foolish virgins. In the cultural context of first-century Jewish weddings, the bridegroom’s arrival was often unpredictable, requiring the bridal party to be ready at any moment. The foolish virgins’ decision to leave and buy oil signifies a lack of foresight and spiritual readiness. This can be seen as a metaphor for spiritual preparedness, emphasizing the importance of being ready for Christ’s return, as echoed in other scriptures like Matthew 24:44.

The Parable of the Talents

Matthew 25:14

For it is just like a man going on a journey,
Jesus portrays His departure after the resurrection and ascension, when He would be received into heaven until His return in glory. The journey belongs to the larger teaching about the kingdom of heaven and the need for readiness during the period between Christ’s first and second comings.

who called his servants
The servants represent those who belong to Christ and live under His authority. His call establishes both privilege and obligation, for disciples are summoned not merely to await their Master’s return but to carry out His will faithfully.

Luk 19:13 Beforehand, he called ten of his servants…

Act 10:7 When the angel who spoke to him had gone,…

and entrusted them with his possessions.
The possessions represent all that Christ places in the care of His people, including abilities, opportunities, material resources, spiritual gifts, and the truth of the gospel. Believers are stewards rather than owners, accountable to the Lord for how they employ what He has entrusted to them in His service and for the good of others.

To one he gave five talents,
A talent represented an enormous amount of wealth, roughly equivalent to 6,000 denarii, or many years of a laborer’s wages. The master entrusted this servant with resources of great value, revealing both his wealth and his confidence in the servant’s ability to manage them faithfully.

to another two talents,
The smaller amount did not indicate lesser worth in the master’s eyes. Two talents remained a substantial trust, and the servant was accountable to use what he received for the master’s benefit. God’s servants are not measured against one another but called to faithfulness with the opportunities, gifts, and responsibilities entrusted to them.

and to another one talent—
Even one talent was a valuable stewardship. The servant who received the least was not neglected or given an insignificant task; he was given enough to serve his master meaningfully. Every believer bears responsibility to use what God has provided rather than allowing fear, comparison, or neglect to prevent faithful service.

The master knew his servants and assigned their responsibilities wisely. God gives differing gifts and measures of opportunity, yet each person remains accountable for obedient stewardship. The master’s departure created a period in which the servants could demonstrate their loyalty without his visible presence. Jesus points to the time between His ascension and return, when His followers are to labor faithfully with all He has entrusted to them. His absence is not abandonment, for He will return and require an account from every servant.

The servant who had received the five talents
The master entrusted this servant with an exceptionally large sum, reflecting his knowledge of the servant’s proven ability. A talent represented roughly 20 years of wages for a laborer, so five talents placed immense resources and responsibility in his care. God distributes abilities, opportunities, material resources, and responsibilities according to His wise purposes, and greater trust brings greater accountability.

Prompt action revealed faithfulness, confidence in the master’s purpose, and willingness to serve without delay. Like Abraham rising early to obey God’s command (Genesis 22:3), the servant treated obedience as urgent rather than optional.

The servant actively employed what had been entrusted to him rather than preserving it in fear or neglect. His labor reflects the mandate to be fruitful and to exercise wise stewardship over all that God places in human hands (Genesis 1:28; 1 Corinthians 4:2).

His faithful labor resulted in a full increase, demonstrating that the master’s trust was well placed. The gain came through diligent stewardship, and it anticipates the eternal reward promised to those who serve Christ faithfully until His return.

The second servant receives a substantial trust, for a talent represented an immense sum of money, often equal to many years of wages. The master gives according to each servant’s ability, and faithful stewardship is measured by obedience, not by comparison with others.

Matthew 25:18

But the servant who had received the one talent
This phrase introduces the third servant in the Parable of the Talents. In biblical times, a talent was a significant sum of money, often equivalent to about 20 years of a laborer’s wages. The servant’s receipt of one talent indicates a substantial responsibility, despite being less than the others received. This reflects the principle that God entrusts individuals with resources according to their abilities (Matthew 25:15). The servant’s actions are a focal point for understanding stewardship and accountability in the Kingdom of Heaven.

Luk 19:13 Beforehand, he called ten of his servants…

The phrase “went off” suggests a deliberate action taken by the servant. It implies a choice to separate himself from the immediate presence of his master and the other servants. This decision can be seen as a metaphor for spiritual distancing or neglect of one’s divine calling. The servant’s departure from the master’s presence symbolizes a lack of engagement with the responsibilities given by God.

Digging a hole in the ground was a common method in ancient times for safeguarding valuables. This action reflects a mindset of fear and self-preservation rather than faith and initiative. The servant’s choice to bury the talent contrasts with the proactive investments made by the other servants. Biblically, this act can be seen as a failure to utilize God-given gifts for His glory, reminiscent of the warning in James 2:17 about faith without works being dead.

The money remained the master’s property, and the servant was accountable for its use. His failure was not theft or loss, but deliberate inaction. Believers are called to use every God-given gift, opportunity, and resource in service to Christ and others, knowing that the Lord will require an account from His servants.

Matthew 25:35

For I was hungry and you gave Me something to eat,
Jesus commends practical mercy toward those lacking daily bread. Hunger was a recurring reality in Israel because of poverty, debt, crop failure, and famine. God’s law required His people to provide for the needy through generosity and gleaning provisions (Leviticus 19:9–10; Isaiah 58:7). The manna given in the wilderness revealed God’s faithful care for His people’s physical needs (Exodus 16). Feeding the hungry does not replace the need for spiritual nourishment from God’s word (Matthew 4:4), but love for God is demonstrated in concern for the whole person. Such works do not earn salvation; they reveal the faith and allegiance of those who belong to the King.

I was thirsty and you gave Me something to drink,
Water was precious in the dry lands of Israel, where wells, springs, and stored water often determined the safety of travelers and communities.
