Jehoiachin Reigns in Judah

2 Kings 24:6

And Jehoiakim rested with his fathers,
Jehoiakim died after an eleven-year reign marked by evil before the Lord, oppression, idolatry, and rebellion against Babylon (2 Kings 23:36-37; 24:1). The customary royal notice of death does not cancel Jeremiah’s prophecy that Jehoiakim would receive no proper lament and would be cast out with the burial of a donkey (Jeremiah 22:18-19). Scripture does not record the circumstances of his burial. His death came as Babylon’s power tightened around Judah, confirming that covenant judgment had begun because of Judah’s persistent sin.

and his son Jehoiachin reigned in his place.
Jehoiachin, also called Jeconiah and Coniah, inherited a kingdom already threatened by Nebuchadnezzar. His reign lasted only three months before Jerusalem surrendered to Babylon (2 Kings 24:8-16). Nebuchadnezzar carried Jehoiachin, the royal family, leading officials, warriors, craftsmen, and treasures from the temple into exile. Jehoiachin’s captivity demonstrated the collapse of Judah’s earthly strength, yet his preservation and later release from prison (2 Kings 25:27-30) kept the Davidic line before the world. He appears in the genealogy of Jesus Christ, through whom God fulfills His promise of an everlasting King from David’s house (Matthew 1:11-12).

2 Kings 24:19

And Zedekiah did evil in the sight of the LORD,
Zedekiah, originally named Mattaniah, was installed by Nebuchadnezzar after the deportation of Jehoiachin and ruled Judah during its final years before Jerusalem fell. His evil included the covenant unfaithfulness condemned by Jeremiah and Ezekiel: idolatry, injustice, refusal to humble himself before God, and rejection of prophetic warning. Although Jeremiah urged submission to Babylon as God’s appointed judgment, Zedekiah broke his oath to Nebuchadnezzar and sought help from Egypt. His rebellion brought the Babylonian siege that ended in Jerusalem’s destruction, the burning of the temple, and the exile of Judah.

Jehoiakim, Zedekiah’s brother and an earlier king of Judah, had established the same pattern of defiance. The final kings of Judah did not merely inherit a political crisis; they continued the spiritual rebellion that had provoked God’s long-announced judgment. Their repeated refusal to hear the Lord showed that national collapse flowed from covenant unfaithfulness as well as Babylonian power.
