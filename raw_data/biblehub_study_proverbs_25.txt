More Proverbs of Solomon

Proverbs 25:1

These are additional proverbs of Solomon,
Solomon, David’s son and king over Israel, received extraordinary wisdom from God and spoke thousands of proverbs (1 Kings 4:29–34). These sayings belong to a further collection of his inspired instruction, preserving practical wisdom for righteous living, just leadership, disciplined speech, and reverent fear of the Lord.

Pro 10:1 The proverbs of Solomon: A wise son brings joy…

Pro 1:1 These are the proverbs of Solomon son of David,…

1Ki 4:32 Solomon composed three thousand proverbs,…

which were copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah:
Hezekiah’s scribes preserved and arranged Solomon’s proverbs about two centuries after Solomon’s reign. Their work occurred during a king’s efforts to remove idolatry and restore faithful worship of the Lord (2 Kings 18:3–6). The careful transmission of these proverbs shows God’s providence in preserving His wisdom for later generations, calling His people to live faithfully amid a corrupt world.

Proverbs 25:2

It is the glory of God to conceal a matter
God’s hidden counsels display His infinite wisdom and sovereign freedom. His purposes in creation, providence, judgment, and redemption are never beyond His knowledge, though they often remain beyond human discovery until He chooses to reveal them. “The secret things belong unto the LORD our God” (Deuteronomy 29:29), yet He has made known all that is necessary for faith and obedience. The wisdom once hidden in God was revealed in Christ and in the gospel (1 Corinthians 2:7–10). Jesus’ parables both disclosed truth to believing disciples and exposed the hardness of those who refused Him (Matthew 13:10–17). God’s concealment calls His people to humility, trust, prayer, and diligent attention to His written Word.

Deu 29:29 The secret things belong to the LORD our God,…

Dan 2:22 He reveals the deep and hidden things; He knows…

Rom 11:33 O, the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge…

and the glory of kings to search it out.
Earthly rulers bear responsibility to investigate matters carefully, discern truth, administer justice, and govern for the good of those under their care. Solomon asked God for an understanding heart so that he could judge Israel rightly (1 Kings 3:9), and God granted him wisdom exceeding that of surrounding kings (1 Kings 4:29–34). The search for truth is therefore an honor rather than a burden for those entrusted with authority. Proverbs 25 begins with sayings copied by the men of Hezekiah, reminding later rulers that wise government requires careful preservation, study, and application of God-given wisdom. Every human king remains limited, but Jesus Christ is the perfect King, in whom “are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Colossians 2:3).

Proverbs 25:3

As the heavens are high and the earth is deep,
The immeasurable height of the heavens and the inaccessible depths of the earth portray realities beyond human reach. Scripture repeatedly uses the heavens to declare God’s glory and exalted wisdom, while the depths evoke what lies hidden from human sight, including the realm of the dead. God’s ways exceed human understanding as the heavens exceed the earth.

Isa 55:9 “For as the heavens are higher than the earth,…

Psa 103:11 For as high as the heavens are above the earth,…

Job 11:8-9 They are higher than the heavens—what can you do?…

so the hearts of kings cannot be searched.
A king’s inner purposes, counsels, and decisions were not fully known by those he governed. Though rulers exercise real authority, their hearts remain under God’s sovereign rule, for He searches every heart and directs kings according to His purposes. The proverb warns against presumptuous judgment of those in authority and reminds rulers themselves that their hidden motives are known to God. Christ, the King of kings, possesses wisdom and purposes no human can exhaust, yet He reveals His saving will through His Word.

Proverbs 25:4

Silver was purified by intense heat, which brought its impurities to the surface so they could be removed. God uses testing, correction, and His Word to expose sin and cleanse His people from attitudes and practices that corrupt faithful living (Isaiah 1:25; Malachi 3:3). Holiness requires more than outward reform; the Lord works to purify the heart.

Refined silver could be formed into a useful and valuable vessel in the hands of a skilled craftsman. Cleansed believers are prepared for honorable service under the hand of Christ, who conforms them to His image and employs them for His purposes (2 Timothy 2:21; Romans 8:29).

Proverbs 25:5

Remove the wicked from the king’s presence,
A ruler’s judgment is shaped by the counselors, officials, and companions permitted near the throne. Wicked advisers foster bribery, false testimony, oppression, and policies that serve private gain rather than the good of the people. Solomon compares their removal to taking dross from silver so that a skilled craftsman may produce a useful vessel (Proverbs 25:4). David’s court was endangered by Ahithophel’s betrayal during Absalom’s rebellion (2 Samuel 15:12), while Rehoboam’s rejection of wise counsel brought division to Israel (1 Kings 12:6–19). Kings were accountable to God to resist corrupt influences and uphold justice (Proverbs 16:12; 20:8).

and his throne will be established in righteousness.
Righteous rule produces stability because it reflects God’s own justice, protects the innocent, restrains evil, and refuses partiality. A throne cannot endure through corruption, violence, or deceit, even when such practices appear to strengthen a kingdom for a time. Hezekiah and Josiah demonstrated the blessing of seeking the Lord and restoring obedience to His law (2 Kings 18:3–7; 22:1–2). The fullest fulfillment belongs to Jesus Christ, the Son of David, whose eternal kingdom rests on perfect righteousness and justice (2 Samuel 7:16; Hebrews 1:8; Isaiah 9:6–7).

Proverbs 25:6

Do not exalt yourself in the presence of the king,
Earthly kings possessed authority to reward, remove, or disgrace those within their courts. Self-promotion before a ruler displayed pride and could bring swift humiliation. Joseph stood before Pharaoh with humility, giving God credit for the interpretation of dreams, and God raised him to authority in His appointed time (Genesis 41:16, 39–41). Believers are likewise called to humble themselves before Christ, the King of kings, trusting Him rather than seeking honor for themselves.

and do not stand in the place of great men;
Places of honor at royal gatherings and banquets were assigned according to rank and favor. Taking such a place without invitation risked public shame when a more honored guest arrived. David refused to seize Saul’s throne, though he had been anointed as Israel’s future king, and waited for God to establish him (1 Samuel 24:4–7). Jesus applied the same wisdom to banquet seating, teaching that the humble will be exalted while the self-exalting will be humbled (Luke 14:8–11).

do not bring hastily to court.
Civil cases were commonly heard at the city gate before elders and witnesses. Wisdom forbids turning a private offense into a public dispute before the facts are known and reconciliation has been sought. A rushed accusation can deepen hostility, distort justice, and expose one’s own wrongdoing. Jesus likewise commands His followers to pursue reconciliation promptly with an adversary (Matthew 5:25).

Litigation may begin with confidence but end with regret when evidence, witnesses, or hidden circumstances reveal a different account. Proverbs repeatedly warns that haste produces error (Proverbs 19:2). God’s people must consider the lasting cost of conflict rather than acting from anger, wounded pride, or a desire for vindication.

Proverbs 25:9

Argue your case with your neighbor
Disputes should be addressed honestly and directly with the person involved rather than spread through gossip or public accusation. Israel’s covenant life depended on truth, justice, and peace among neighbors. Jesus affirmed this pattern by commanding a believer to go privately to a brother who has sinned, seeking restoration before involving others (Matthew 18:15–17).

A personal grievance never permits the exposure of secrets entrusted by others. Revealing confidential matters to gain advantage in an argument damages reputations, multiplies conflict, and proves unfaithfulness. Proverbs repeatedly condemns the talebearer who betrays trust (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19). God’s people are called to speak truth while guarding what has been entrusted to them, reflecting the faithfulness of Christ toward those who belong to Him.

Proverbs 25:10

Private disputes should be handled with restraint, not exposed through careless speech. In a culture where honor shaped family standing, public disclosure could damage both the individual and the community connected to him. Revealing another person’s confidential matter may invite a justified rebuke from those who hear, since the speaker has shown himself untrustworthy. Proverbs repeatedly warns that gossip separates close friends and stirs conflict, while faithful people conceal matters entrusted to them (Proverbs 11:13; 16:28). The tongue can spread harm far beyond the original dispute, as James 3:5–6 warns.

Ecclesiastes 10:1 similarly teaches that a small act of folly can spoil what was otherwise honorable. God calls His people to pursue peace, guard their words, and live blamelessly before others, so that their conduct does not bring reproach upon the name of Christ (Philippians 2:15).

Rebekah received a gold ring and bracelets as signs of honor and covenantal purpose (Genesis 24:22), while gold also adorned the tabernacle and temple in service to the holy God (Exodus 25; 1 Kings 6). A precious ornament enhanced rather than diminished its wearer; so true correction, though sometimes painful to receive, brings spiritual beauty and honor to a humble life.

Proverbs 25:13

Harvest in Israel came during the heat and dryness of summer, when labor in the fields was demanding and water cooled by snow from the mountains brought welcome relief. Snow on Mount Hermon and other high elevations could refresh those working below. The image conveys timely strength and restoration in the midst of weary labor. God likewise sends His provision at the proper time, and His word accomplishes His nourishing purpose as rain and snow water the earth (Isaiah 55:10–11).

Jude similarly describes false teachers as “clouds without water,” offering spiritual promise while bringing no life-giving truth. Empty display cannot nourish the soul or sustain those who depend upon it.

Such boasting exposes a heart concerned with appearing generous rather than actually serving others. Christ stands in complete contrast, for every promise of God finds its fulfillment in Him, and His gifts of grace, forgiveness, and eternal life are never empty words.

Proverbs 25:21

Personal hostility does not cancel the duty to show mercy. In a culture where shared meals often marked peace and fellowship, feeding an adversary rejected the ordinary cycle of retaliation. Elisha modeled such mercy when Israel fed the captured Aramean army and sent them home in peace (2 Kings 6:21–23). Jesus later commanded His disciples to love their enemies and pray for those who persecute them (Matthew 5:44).

God gives daily provision even to those who disregard Him, displaying patience and kindness that call sinners to repentance (Matthew 5:45; Romans 2:4). Such mercy may expose the hardness of an enemy’s heart, open a path toward reconciliation, and guard the righteous person from becoming overcome by evil.

Proverbs 25:24

Better to live on a corner of the roof
Ancient Israelite homes commonly had flat roofs, reached by an outside stairway and used for work, rest, storage, and sleeping during warm weather. A roof corner offered little shelter, comfort, or companionship. Yet solitude in such a place is portrayed as preferable to the continual distress of conflict within the home. Peace is a greater treasure than comfort, possessions, or the appearance of a well-ordered household. Proverbs 21:9 repeats the same lesson, underscoring its importance.

Proverbs 25:25

Water was precious in the dry lands of Israel, and a traveler exhausted by heat and thirst knew the restoring power of cool water. Physical refreshment pictures the relief God provides for those burdened in heart and spirit. The psalmist described his longing for God as a deer panting for streams of water (Psalm 42:1–2), and Jesus promised living water to all who come to Him in faith (John 4:14).

Proverbs 25:26

Like a muddied spring or a polluted well
Springs and wells sustained households, herds, and travelers in Israel’s dry land. When water became fouled, it endangered those who depended on it and ceased to provide the refreshment expected from it. Scripture often uses clean water to portray life, cleansing, and God’s blessing (Psalm 1:3; John 4:14). Corrupted water pictures the public harm caused when what should nourish and protect becomes compromised.

Proverbs 25:27

It is not good to eat too much honey
Honey represented God’s abundance and the sweetness of His provision in the land flowing with milk and honey. Yet even a good gift becomes harmful when governed by appetite rather than self-control. Excess brings sickness and dishonor, illustrating the wisdom of restraint in food, pleasure, possessions, and every earthly blessing. Proverbs repeatedly joins wisdom with disciplined desire, warning that gluttony and drunkenness lead to poverty and ruin.

Proverbs 25:28

Like a city whose walls are broken down
Ancient city walls provided defense, order, and refuge for those within. When Jerusalem’s walls lay in ruins, the city stood exposed to enemies and disgrace until God used Nehemiah to lead their rebuilding (Nehemiah 1:3; 2:17). Jericho’s fallen walls likewise marked the city’s defeat before Israel (Joshua 6:20). Broken walls left a community vulnerable; they pictured the loss of protection and stability.

is a man who does not control his temper.
Uncontrolled anger leaves a person exposed to sin, damaged relationships, rash words, and destructive actions. Saul’s jealousy and rage against David repeatedly drove him toward murder and rebellion against God’s purposes (1 Samuel 18:10–12; 19:9–10). God calls His people to be slow to anger, because human anger does not produce the righteousness He desires (James 1:19–20).
