Zechariah 7:1

In the fourth year of King Darius,
Darius I reigned over Persia from 522 to 486 BC, placing this revelation in 518 BC. The Jewish exiles had returned from Babylon under Cyrus’s decree, and the temple had been completed two years earlier in 516 BC. Persian rule brought sufficient stability for the restored community to rebuild its worship, order its civic life, and confront the deeper spiritual issues that remained after exile.

Bethel, meaning “House of God,” lay north of Jerusalem and carried a troubled spiritual history. Abraham and Jacob had worshiped the Lord there, but Jeroboam later made it a center of rival worship for the northern kingdom. The delegation from Bethel came during the restoration after exile, seeking the Lord’s favor through the worship established at Jerusalem.

The Lord commanded Zechariah to address both the general population and the priesthood. Every part of the restored community bore responsibility for its worship, repentance, and obedience. Priests were to guide the people in the law of God, yet neither religious office nor public custom could substitute for sincere devotion.

The fifth-month fast remembered the destruction of Jerusalem and Solomon’s temple by Babylon in 586 BC. The seventh-month fast commemorated the murder of Gedaliah, an event that scattered the remaining Jewish community and deepened the nation’s ruin (2 Kings 25:25–26; Jeremiah 41). These observances preserved the memory of judgment, loss, and exile.

Zechariah 7:6

And when you were eating and drinking,
Israel’s fasts commemorated Jerusalem’s destruction and the loss of the temple, yet their ordinary meals and religious feasts could be equally self-focused. Eating and drinking were not condemned; God had appointed feasts such as Passover and the Feast of Tabernacles for grateful remembrance and fellowship. The Lord exposed the heart behind both mourning and celebration, because outward observance without obedience does not honor Him.

The people’s fasting had not been governed by sorrow over sin or a desire to seek God, and their feasting had not been marked by thankful devotion to Him. Their practices served personal grief, tradition, appetite, and appearance rather than God’s revealed will. Like the worship rebuked in Isaiah 58 and Amos 5, religious activity became empty when separated from justice, mercy, and faithful obedience. God calls His people to worship Him from the heart and to let devotion to Him shape their treatment of others.

The returning exiles were not receiving a new standard but were being confronted with the same message their fathers had refused.

God again addressed Zechariah with authoritative instruction for the returned exiles. Jerusalem and the temple were being rebuilt, yet the people also needed renewed obedience, justice, and covenant faithfulness. Zechariah stood in the line of prophets such as Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel, who proclaimed God’s word to correct sin, call for repentance, and sustain hope. Zechariah 7:10

Do not oppress the widow or the fatherless,
Widows and orphans often lacked the economic security and legal protection supplied by a husband or father in ancient Israel. The Mosaic Law placed them under God’s special care and warned that He hears their cry against those who exploit them (Exodus 22:22–24). Israel’s worship could not be separated from justice toward the defenseless. The Lord identifies Himself as “a father of the fatherless and a judge for the widows” (Psalm 68:5), and the prophets exposed national unfaithfulness when rulers and citizens neglected them (Isaiah 1:17; Jeremiah 7:6).

the foreigner or the poor.
Foreign residents and impoverished Israelites were also vulnerable to abuse, exclusion, and debt. Israel was commanded to remember its own slavery and sojourning in Egypt by showing mercy to those living without land, family protection, or material provision (Leviticus 19:33–34; Deuteronomy 10:18–19). Gleaning laws and other provisions prevented the poor from being abandoned to hunger (Leviticus 19:9–10). God’s people were to reflect His righteous rule by giving practical care and impartial justice to those easily overlooked.

God’s demand reaches beyond outward conduct to the intentions from which oppression, fraud, and violence arise. The heart directs the life, and concealed resentment or calculated harm violates the covenant even before it becomes visible in action (Proverbs 4:23; 6:16–19). Jesus likewise taught that evil deeds proceed from within (Matthew 15:19–20). Genuine obedience requires hearts shaped by love, truth, and peace rather than personal gain at another’s expense.
