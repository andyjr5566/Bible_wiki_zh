Come Quickly to Me

Psalm 141:1

A Psalm of David.
David wrote as a man acquainted with danger, sin, forgiveness, and the sustaining presence of God. His prayers arose from real conflicts during the years he fled Saul, faced foreign enemies, endured family sorrow, and ruled Israel. David’s confidence did not rest in his position as king but in the covenant faithfulness of the God who had chosen and preserved him.

I call upon You, O LORD;
David turns directly to YHWH (LORD), Israel’s covenant God, rather than seeking refuge in human strength or political alliances. Prayer expresses faith that the Lord knows His servant’s need and remains able to deliver. David’s appeal models the dependence required of all who belong to God.

Psa 28:1 Of David. To You, O LORD, I call; be not deaf to me, O my Rock.…

Psa 18:6 In my distress I called upon the LORD; I cried…

come quickly to me.
Immediate danger or temptation can make the need for God’s help feel urgent. David asks the Lord to act without delay, trusting that God is neither distant nor indifferent to the cries of His people. Israel’s deliverance from Egypt and God’s repeated rescues throughout David’s life testified that the Lord responds in His appointed time.

David seeks more than relief from circumstances; he seeks the attentive presence of God. The Lord who hears His people invites them to bring their fears, needs, and petitions before Him. Believers approach God with similar confidence through Christ, who intercedes for His people before the Father.

Psalm 141:2

May my prayer be set before You like incense;
Incense rose continually before the Lord from the altar of incense in the tabernacle and temple, burned each morning and evening according to God’s command (Exodus 30:7–8). Its costly, holy composition and its placement before the veil portrayed worship offered with reverence and devotion. David asks that his prayer would be received as an acceptable offering before God. Revelation portrays the prayers of the saints as incense before God’s throne (Revelation 5:8; 8:3–4), affirming that faithful prayer is precious to Him.

Rev 8:3-4 Then another angel, who had a golden censer,…

Rev 5:8 When He had taken the scroll, the four living creatures…

Exo 30:7-8 And Aaron is to burn fragrant incense on it…

my uplifted hands, like the evening offering.
Raised hands expressed dependence, surrender, and earnest appeal to God. The evening offering included the daily sacrifice of a lamb, together with grain and drink offerings, and reminded Israel that access to God required atonement (Exodus 29:38–41). David joins his personal prayer to Israel’s appointed worship, desiring a life of continual devotion rather than occasional religious activity. The sacrifices anticipated the once-for-all offering of Christ, whose death secured forgiveness and opened the way for believers to draw near to God (Hebrews 10:10–12).

Psalm 141:3

Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth;
David asks the Lord to restrain words that could dishonor God, injure others, or reveal a heart drawn toward evil. Because the tongue can bless or curse, human resolve alone is insufficient. Proverbs 18:21 teaches that death and life are in the power of the tongue, and James warns that no man can fully tame it. God’s guarding work reaches beyond outward speech to the desires and thoughts from which words arise.

Psa 39:1 For the choirmaster. For Jeduthun. A Psalm of David.…

Pro 13:3 He who guards his mouth protects his life…

Pro 21:23 He who guards his mouth and tongue keeps his soul…

keep watch at the door of my lips.
The image recalls a guarded city gate, where access was carefully controlled. David seeks continual divine vigilance over every word permitted to leave his mouth. Such prayer calls for truthfulness, restraint, gentleness, and discernment, especially amid temptation, conflict, and the influence of ungodly people. Jesus, the Word made flesh, spoke only what pleased the Father and provides the perfect pattern for speech governed by holiness and truth.

Psalm 141:4

Do not let my heart be drawn to any evil thing
David asks God to restrain the inner life from turning toward sin. The heart governs thought, desire, purpose, and choice; therefore corruption begins before evil is outwardly committed. Proverbs 4:23 calls for vigilance over the heart, and Jesus taught that evil deeds proceed from within (Matthew 15:19). God’s preserving grace is needed because temptation often gains entrance through disordered desire.

or take part in works of wickedness
The prayer seeks deliverance not only from evil desires but also from evil actions. Holiness requires refusing participation in deeds that dishonor God, even when such deeds offer advantage, pleasure, or acceptance. Believers are called to separate from darkness and walk as children of light (Ephesians 5:8–11).

with men who do iniquity;
David recognizes that companionship can shape conduct. Psalm 1 begins by warning against adopting the counsel, path, and settled fellowship of the wicked. Association with those committed to sin can normalize rebellion against God and draw the righteous into shared guilt. Wise fellowship strengthens obedience, while corrupt company damages godly character (1 Corinthians 15:33).

let me not feast on their delicacies.
The delicacies picture the attractive rewards offered by sinful companionship, including comfort, status, pleasure, and gain obtained without righteousness. Shared meals commonly expressed fellowship and loyalty, so David refuses the table of those whose lives oppose God. Sin may appear desirable for a moment, but its pleasures are deceptive and its end brings death. David seeks satisfaction in the Lord rather than in the temporary enticements of wickedness.

Godly correction may wound pride, yet it serves the healing of the soul. David prefers the painful honesty of a faithful man to the flattering speech that leads toward sin. Proverbs 27:6 likewise teaches that the wounds of a friend are faithful.

Rebuke given by one who fears God is not cruelty but covenant kindness. The Lord Himself disciplines those He loves, using correction to turn His people from destructive paths and restore them to obedience.

David’s readiness to be corrected does not mean tolerance of evil. He seeks God in prayer against wicked deeds, desiring both personal holiness and the Lord’s righteous judgment upon persistent rebellion.

Psalm 141:7

As when one plows and breaks up the soil,
Farmers cut and turned the hardened ground before sowing, leaving clods and stones exposed across the field. The violent image portrays devastation so extensive that the slain appear scattered like earth overturned by a plow.

Unburied bones testified to defeat, humiliation, and the cruelty of enemies. David identifies himself with the faithful who have suffered violence for refusing the ways of the wicked, yet he continues to look to the Lord rather than seek rescue through sin.

Psalm 141:8

But my eyes are fixed on You, O GOD the Lord.
David turns from the threats of evildoers to the covenant God who rules over all. His attention is deliberately directed toward the LORD, whose faithfulness is joined to sovereign authority. Such steadfast looking anticipates the believer’s call to fix the eyes on Jesus, who perfects faith and sustains His people (Hebrews 12:2).

God Himself is David’s shelter, not merely the giver of shelter. The psalmist rests in the protection of the Lord amid surrounding danger, as God’s people repeatedly did in times of war, persecution, and distress. The refuge promised in the Psalms finds its fullest security in Christ, who receives all who come to Him.

Psalm 141:9

Keep me from the snares they have laid for me,
David asks the Lord to preserve him from concealed plots designed to bring him down. Hunters hid snares along an animal’s path, and hostile people likewise worked through secrecy, slander, betrayal, and violence. God sees dangers that remain hidden from His servants and is able to direct their steps safely. Believers are called to put on the whole armor of God, standing alert against the devil’s schemes rather than trusting their own perception or strength (Ephesians 6:11).

Psa 119:110 The wicked have set a snare for me, but I have not strayed…

Psa 31:4 You free me from the net laid out for me…

and from the lures of evildoers.
Wicked people often make sin appear advantageous, harmless, or necessary, as the serpent did in Eden. Their enticements seek not merely to harm outwardly but to draw the righteous into compromise and ruin. The fear of man becomes a snare, but trust in the Lord provides security (Proverbs 29:25). God’s people must seek His wisdom and obey His word, knowing that temptation gives birth to sin when it is welcomed (James 1:14–15).

Psalm 141:10

Let the wicked fall into their own nets,
David entrusts judgment to the Lord rather than taking revenge against those who devise his ruin. Nets, pits, and snares portray deliberate schemes of violence and deceit, yet God often turns evil designs back upon their makers (Psalm 7:15–16; Psalm 9:15–16; Proverbs 26:27). Haman’s death on the gallows prepared for Mordecai vividly displays this righteous reversal (Esther 7:9–10). God’s justice exposes the guilt of those who oppose Him and harm His servants.

while I pass by in safety.
David looks to God for preservation amid danger. The Lord does not always remove the threat immediately, but He brings His people through it according to His purpose. As Israel passed safely through the Red Sea while Pharaoh’s army was overthrown, God is able to make a way for His people where enemies intend destruction. The righteous find refuge in the Lord, who guards them until their appointed path is complete.
