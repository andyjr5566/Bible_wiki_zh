I Will Sing of His Love Forever

Psalm 89:1

A Maskil of Ethan the Ezrahite.
Ethan’s name recalls the wisdom tradition of Israel, for Solomon’s wisdom surpassed that of Ethan the Ezrahite (1 Kings 4:31). The psalm’s instruction centers on the Lord’s covenant with David and the apparent crisis facing David’s royal line. Ethan approaches suffering not by denying God’s promises, but by bringing the tension between God’s covenant and Israel’s circumstances before Him in worship.

I will sing of the loving devotion of the LORD forever;
The psalm begins with praise for the Lord’s steadfast covenant love before turning to the distress of God’s people. God’s mercy toward David’s house rests on His own character and promise, not on human strength or faithfulness. The commitment to sing “forever” anticipates the everlasting fulfillment of the Davidic covenant in Jesus Christ, the Son of David whose kingdom will never end.

Isaiah likewise asks, “To whom then will you liken God?” (Isaiah 40:18), calling God’s people to reject every rival and trust Him without reservation.

Mount Tabor rises prominently from the Jezreel Valley, while Mount Hermon towers in Israel’s far north, its snowy heights visible across a wide region. Their inclusion portrays the whole land, from central Israel to its northern boundary, as rejoicing before its Maker. The question does not deny God’s faithfulness; it appeals to it amid the painful delay between His promises and their visible fulfillment.

Psalm 89:48

What man can live and never see death?
Death entered human experience through Adam’s sin, and every generation bears the sentence pronounced in Eden: “You are dust, and to dust you shall return” (Genesis 3:19). Kings, warriors, priests, and the poor alike pass away. Ecclesiastes recognizes that God has appointed “a time to be born, and a time to die” (Ecclesiastes 3:2), while Hebrews declares that men die once and afterward face judgment (Hebrews 9:27). Human mortality stands in sharp contrast to the Lord, who is everlasting from before the mountains were brought forth (Psalm 90:2). Yet death does not have the final word for those united to Christ. As all die in Adam, so all who belong to Christ will be made alive through His resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:22).

Can he deliver his soul from the power of Sheol?
No human strength, wealth, wisdom, or righteousness can prevent the descent into the grave. Sheol portrays the realm of the dead and the helplessness of those under death’s power. Job wrestled with the same reality, asking whether a man who dies can live again (Job 14:14). Sin earns death, and mankind cannot pay its penalty or break its grip (Romans 6:23). The hope denied to human effort is accomplished by the Lord Jesus, who truly died, rose bodily from the grave, and holds the keys of Death and Hades (Revelation 1:18). He delivers His people from condemnation and promises resurrection life to all who trust in Him.
