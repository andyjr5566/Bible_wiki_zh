The Invasion of Locusts

Joel 1:1

This is the word of the LORD
Joel’s message came by divine revelation, not human insight. The Lord spoke with authority to address Judah’s sin, summon His people to repentance, and reveal both coming judgment and future restoration. Like Jeremiah and Ezekiel, Joel served as a messenger entrusted with God’s own words.

that came to Joel
Joel received and faithfully delivered the Lord’s message to Judah. His name, meaning “Yahweh is God,” accords with the burden of his prophecy: the people must recognize the Lord alone as God and return to Him with wholehearted repentance. Though the book gives few historical details, its warning concerning judgment and its promise of the outpoured Spirit remain enduring testimony to God’s holiness, mercy, and faithfulness.

Grain offerings expressed thanksgiving and consecration to God, while drink offerings commonly accompanied sacrifices as an offering poured out before Him (Leviticus 2; Numbers 15:1–10). The loss of these provisions brought agricultural ruin into the center of Israel’s worship and displayed the covenant judgment warned of in Deuteronomy 28:38–42.

Joel 1:16

The devastation caused by the locust plague lay openly before Judah, leaving no harvest for daily bread and no reserve for the future. Grain, wine, and oil—the basic produce of the land—had been consumed, exposing the nation’s helplessness before God’s judgment. The disaster was not distant or theoretical; every household could see its effects in empty fields and depleted stores. Like the famines recorded in Genesis, this calamity revealed humanity’s dependence upon the Lord, who alone gives rain, harvest, and life.

Cattle supplied labor, milk, meat, and wealth, so their distress exposed the depth of Judah’s agricultural and economic collapse. The disaster has exposed Israel’s helplessness and calls for more than human recovery; it requires the mercy of the LORD. The prophet’s appeal models the response God desires from His people when His discipline reveals their sin and dependence upon Him.

The land promised as a place of abundance had become desolate under God’s judgment. Fire elsewhere in the prophets often accompanies the Lord’s consuming judgment, and the ruined pastures testified that every part of the nation’s life was affected.

Fruit trees, shade trees, and woodland growth had been stripped and burned by the severe drought, removing both present sustenance and future harvests. The destruction of the trees underscores the completeness of the calamity: field, pasture, vineyard, flock, and herd all suffered together. Yet Joel’s cry to the LORD anticipates that judgment need not be the final word, for the God who disciplines His covenant people also calls them to return to Him.

The drought reaches beyond Judah’s cities and fields into the wilderness, where wild animals suffer from thirst and the loss of vegetation. Their distress displays creation’s dependence on its Creator and reflects the wider groaning of a world damaged by sin and judgment (Romans 8:22). Even the animals’ need points Judah toward the God from whom rain, food, and life must come.

The loss of streams brought severe consequences for drinking water, crops, herds, and every part of the land’s economy. Drought had been warned as a covenant judgment for persistent disobedience (Deuteronomy 28:23–24). Judah could not escape its need for the Lord through human effort or stored resources. Scorching heat and drought had stripped the grazing lands of their grass and consumed what remained. The devastation of vineyards, grain fields, trees, and pastures showed that the judgment was comprehensive. Fire throughout Scripture can signify God’s holy judgment, yet Joel’s message also presses toward repentance and restoration.
