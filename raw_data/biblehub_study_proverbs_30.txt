The Words of Agur

Proverbs 30:1

These are the words of Agur son of Jakeh—
Agur, whose name means “collector” or “gatherer,” appears nowhere else in Scripture, and Jakeh is otherwise unknown. His inclusion reminds readers that God’s wisdom is not confined to Israel’s most prominent kings, prophets, or priests. Proverbs preserves the instruction of a man whose authority rests not on public status but on the truth he received and faithfully conveyed.

Num 24:3-4 “This is the prophecy of Balaam son of Beor,…

Pro 1:1 These are the proverbs of Solomon son of David,…

the burden that this man declared to Ithiel:
Agur delivered his teaching as a weighty message, not as casual reflection. Ithiel was likely a hearer or disciple receiving instruction meant to form a life of humility, truthfulness, and reverence for God. Wisdom in Israel was often taught personally, passed from teacher to learner so that knowledge would become obedient character.

“I am weary, O God, and worn out.
Agur begins by confessing human insufficiency before God. His weariness does not end in despair but prepares the way for dependence upon the Lord, whose understanding is perfect. Like Elijah in his exhaustion and David in his affliction, Agur recognizes that strength and wisdom cannot be produced by human effort alone.

Proverbs 30:2

Surely I am the most ignorant of men,
Agur begins with a deliberate confession of human insufficiency before God. Though wisdom was prized in Israel and throughout the ancient world, Agur refuses self-confidence and recognizes that no human mind can master the works, ways, and purposes of the Lord. His humility resembles Job’s repentance after God revealed His greatness and Job acknowledged that he had spoken of matters beyond his understanding (Job 42:3). Such honesty is the proper beginning of wisdom, for the fear of the Lord humbles man before the God who alone knows all things.

Psa 73:22 I was senseless and ignorant; I was a brute beast…

Psa 49:20 A man who has riches without understanding…

Psa 94:8 Take notice, O senseless among the people!…

and I lack the understanding of a man.
Agur does not claim that reason and learning are worthless; he admits that human discernment is inadequate when separated from God’s revelation. Proverbs repeatedly warns against trusting one’s own understanding and directs the heart to seek wisdom from the Lord (Proverbs 3:5–6; 2:6). The confession prepares for Agur’s later questions concerning God’s majesty and His Son (Proverbs 30:4). God gives wisdom generously to those who ask in faith, teaching His people to depend upon His Word rather than human pride (James 1:5).

Jas 1:5 Now if any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God,…

Proverbs 30:3

I have not learned wisdom,
Agur, the son of Jakeh, begins his oracle by confessing his inadequacy before God. Human observation, education, and experience cannot produce the wisdom that begins with the fear of the Lord. Solomon likewise confessed his inability to govern Israel apart from God’s gift of an understanding heart (1 Kings 3:7–9). Agur’s humility prepares the reader to receive God’s instruction rather than trust human insight.

God’s holiness places Him beyond the reach of fallen human reason. Job asks whether anyone can discover the depths of God or fathom the limits of the Almighty (Job 11:7). Knowledge of God must be revealed by God, ultimately through His Son, who makes the Father known (John 1:18). Such knowledge is not merely information about God but reverent fellowship with Him.

The God whose words are perfect also personally guards His people. David repeatedly called the Lord his shield in danger, acknowledging that true security rests in God rather than human strength, weapons, or plans (Psalm 18:30).

Proverbs 30:6

Do not add to His words,
God’s words are pure and trustworthy, as affirmed in the preceding verse, and they require neither human improvement nor human alteration. Agur’s warning stands with Deuteronomy 4:2 and Revelation 22:18–19, where God forbids adding to or taking away from His revealed commands. Israel was called to preserve God’s covenant instruction amid nations whose religions were shaped by human tradition and changing mythology. Scripture remains the final authority for faith, obedience, and the knowledge of God.

Deu 12:32 See that you do everything I command you;…

Rev 22:18-19 I testify to everyone who hears the words of prophecy…

lest He rebuke you
God corrects those who presume to speak beyond what He has revealed or who reshape His commands to suit themselves. His rebuke is righteous and purposeful, exposing error and calling His people back to truth. Hebrews 12:5–6 presents divine discipline as the loving work of a Father who refuses to leave His children in deception.

Adding human teaching as though it carried God’s authority misrepresents Him. False prophets, religious leaders, and teachers throughout Scripture were judged for claiming divine approval for words God had not spoken. Such distortion aligns with the father of lies rather than with Christ, who is the truth (John 14:6). Reverence for God’s Word requires humble submission to all He has spoken.

Proverbs 30:7

Two things I ask of You—
Agur approaches God with humble dependence, bringing a complete and carefully ordered petition. The two requests that follow concern integrity and contentment, revealing that a godly life requires both truthful character and freedom from the corrupting pressures of wealth or poverty. Solomon likewise asked God for discernment to lead His people rightly (1 Kings 3:9). Jesus taught His disciples to ask their Father with confidence in His care and provision (Matthew 7:7).

Psa 27:4 One thing I have asked of the LORD; this is what I desire:…

Mat 7:7 Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find;…

do not refuse me before I die:
Agur pleads with urgency, recognizing that life is brief and that faithful living cannot be postponed. Human mortality should produce wisdom and a desire to use remaining days in obedience to God (Psalm 90:12). The prayer looks beyond temporary comfort and seeks God’s favor for a life marked by truth, humility, and dependence on Him.

Proverbs 30:8

Keep falsehood and deceitful words far from me.
Agur begins with a prayer for personal integrity and for protection from the lies that corrupt human relationships and oppose God’s character. The Lord delights in truthful speech, while lying lips are an abomination to Him (Proverbs 12:22). Such a request recognizes that deceit can arise from one’s own sinful heart as well as from the influence of wicked people. God’s people are called to put away falsehood and speak truth with one another (Ephesians 4:25).

Eph 4:25 Therefore each of you must put off falsehood…

Give me neither poverty nor riches;
Material circumstances can become spiritual tests. Wealth may tempt a person toward pride, self-sufficiency, and forgetfulness of God, as Israel was warned before entering the Promised Land (Deuteronomy 8:11–14). Severe poverty may bring distress and temptation toward dishonest gain. Agur seeks freedom from both dangers, desiring a life governed by dependence upon God rather than by abundance or deprivation. Godliness joined with contentment remains a great gain (1 Timothy 6:6–10).

God faithfully provides what His servants truly need. Israel received manna day by day in the wilderness, learning that life depends on the word and provision of the Lord (Exodus 16; Deuteronomy 8:3). Agur asks for sufficient daily provision, trusting God to assign the proper portion rather than pursuing excess. Jesus taught His disciples to pray, “Give us this day our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11), directing believers toward the same humble reliance on the Father.

Proverbs 30:9

Otherwise, I may have too much and deny You, saying, ‘Who is the LORD?’
Material abundance can foster the illusion that life is sustained by personal strength rather than by God’s providence. Israel received this warning before entering the promised land, where full storehouses and prosperous fields could lead them to forget the Lord who delivered and sustained them (Deuteronomy 8:11–14). Pharaoh’s defiant question, “Who is the LORD?” revealed a proud refusal to acknowledge God’s authority (Exodus 5:2). Wealth becomes spiritually dangerous when it displaces gratitude, dependence, and obedience. God alone gives lasting security, and riches must never become a substitute for trust in Him (1 Timothy 6:17).

Exo 5:2 But Pharaoh replied, “Who is the LORD that I should…

Or I may become poor and steal, profaning the name of my God.
Severe need can tempt a person to violate God’s commands in pursuit of survival. Theft wrongs a neighbor and rejects the Lord’s righteous standard (Exodus 20:15). One who bears God’s name is called to honor Him even under pressure, since sinful conduct can bring reproach upon the God professed (Leviticus 19:12). The prayer seeks neither luxury nor desperate want, but a condition in which faithful obedience may be maintained. God provides strength for contentment in every circumstance and calls His people to trust Him without surrendering integrity (Philippians 4:12–13).

Proverbs 30:10

A servant belonged to a household under the master’s authority and could be especially vulnerable to accusation. A malicious report could cost him trust, livelihood, protection, or even safety. God forbids false witness and requires His people to protect the innocent rather than exploit those with less social power. Truthful speech reflects love for one’s neighbor and recognizes that every person stands before the Lord as accountable and valuable.

A wrongly accused servant may cry out to God for justice when no earthly recourse is available. Scripture repeatedly shows the Lord’s concern for the oppressed, the poor, the foreigner, the widow, and those subject to another’s power. The curse does not present personal vengeance as righteous; it warns that unjust harm may bring the offender under God’s judgment.

Proverbs 30:11

There is a generation of those who curse their fathers
Agur identifies a morally corrupt people marked by contempt for parental authority. God commanded children to honor father and mother (Exodus 20:12), and Israel’s law treated cursing either parent as a grave rebellion against the order God established in the home (Leviticus 20:9; Exodus 21:17). Such defiance rejects not only parents but also the God whose authority they are called to represent. Paul later includes disobedience to parents among the sins characteristic of the last days (2 Timothy 3:1–2).

Exo 21:17 Anyone who curses his father or mother…

Lev 20:9 If anyone curses his father or mother, he must be put to…

Deu 27:16 Cursed is he who dishonors his father or mother…

and do not bless their mothers.
Refusing to bless one’s mother reveals ingratitude for her care, instruction, and sacrifice. Wisdom calls children to receive the teaching of both father and mother (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20). In a culture where family honor was central, withholding honor from a mother displayed deep moral decay. The failure to give thanks for parental care also mirrors the wider human tendency to receive God’s gifts without gratitude or worship (Romans 1:21).

Proverbs 30:12

There is a generation of those who are pure in their own eyes
This phrase highlights a self-righteous attitude prevalent in certain groups or generations. Biblically, this reflects the human tendency to justify oneself without acknowledging sin, reminiscent of the Pharisees in the New Testament (Matthew 23:25-28). The idea of being “pure in their own eyes” suggests a lack of true self-awareness and humility, contrasting with the biblical call to repentance and recognition of one’s sinful nature (Romans 3:23). This self-deception is warned against throughout Scripture, as seen in Isaiah 5:21, which cautions against being wise in one’s own eyes.

Pro 16:2 All a man’s ways are pure in his own eyes,…

Pro 21:2 All a man’s ways seem right to him, but the LORD…

and yet unwashed of their filth.
God’s judgment exposes the impurity hidden beneath self-righteous appearances. Israel’s purification rites taught that uncleanness barred fellowship with a holy God and required cleansing He provided (Leviticus 16:30). No person can remove the defilement of sin by moral effort or religious display. God cleanses the repentant heart, ultimately through the blood of Christ, and gives new life by His Spirit (Ezekiel 36:25–27; 1 John 1:7).

The blood-feeding leech vividly portrays appetite that takes without gratitude or restraint. Its two daughters, both named Give, personify covetous desire that continually demands more. Greed is never satisfied by what it receives; it multiplies its demands and enslaves the heart. Ecclesiastes 5:10 likewise teaches that the lover of money will not be satisfied with money. God calls His people to contentment, generosity, and trust in His provision rather than the restless pursuit of more.

Hebrew wisdom often uses a numbered list to draw attention to observations drawn from God’s created order. The pattern prepares the reader to consider several familiar realities that illustrate an appetite no earthly supply can fill.

Proverbs 30:16

Sheol,
Sheol is often understood as the realm of the dead in Hebrew thought, a place of darkness and silence. It is depicted as insatiable, always consuming but never full. This concept is echoed in other scriptures such as Isaiah 5:14 and Habakkuk 2:5, where Sheol is portrayed as having an unending appetite. Theologically, it represents the inevitability of death and the grave, a reminder of human mortality and the need for redemption.

Pro 27:20 Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied; so the eyes of man…

Isa 5:14 Therefore Sheol enlarges its throat and opens wide…

Hab 2:5 and wealth indeed betrays him. He is an arrogant man…

the barren womb,
In ancient cultures, including Israel, barrenness was often seen as a source of shame and distress for women, as children were considered a blessing from God (Psalm 127:3). The barren womb symbolizes unfulfilled desire and longing. This theme is seen in the stories of Sarah, Rachel, and Hannah, who experienced barrenness before God intervened. It highlights the deep yearning for life and continuation, reflecting humanity’s desire for legacy and purpose.

1Sa 1:5-6 But to Hannah he would give a double portion,…

Jdg 13:2-3 Now there was a man from Zorah named Manoah,…

land never satisfied with water,
This phrase refers to the arid and dry regions common in the Near East, where water is a precious and often scarce resource. The land’s thirst for water symbolizes the constant need for sustenance and the unending cycle of nature. Biblically, water is also a symbol of life and spiritual renewal, as seen in passages like John 4:14, where Jesus speaks of living water. The insatiable land parallels the human soul’s need for spiritual nourishment.

Fire is a powerful biblical symbol of both destruction and purification. It consumes everything in its path, representing judgment and the refining process. In the context of Proverbs, fire’s insatiability underscores the destructive nature of unchecked desires and passions. This imagery is consistent with other biblical references, such as James 3:6, where the tongue is likened to a fire, capable of great harm. Fire’s unending appetite serves as a warning against the dangers of greed and lust.

Proverbs 30:18

Agur introduces a numerical proverb, a familiar Hebrew poetic pattern that builds expectation and gives weight to the matters named. The wonders that follow are not puzzles to be solved by human cleverness but realities in creation that display wisdom beyond ordinary observation. Job likewise confessed that God accomplishes things “too wonderful” for human understanding (Job 42:3). Such recognition humbles the reader before the Creator, whose works and purposes exceed human knowledge.

Sudden power can expose pride and turn former oppression into new oppression. Leadership requires humility, justice, and the fear of the Lord.

Leah’s marriage to Jacob shows the sorrow of being joined to a husband whose heart was set on another woman (Genesis 29:30–31). Such a household could become marked by rivalry, favoritism, and conflict. God’s design calls husbands to faithful, sacrificial love and wives to covenant faithfulness, bringing peace where human sin would otherwise breed bitterness (Ephesians 5:25–33).
