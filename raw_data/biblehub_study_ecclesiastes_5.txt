Approaching God with Fear

Ecclesiastes 5:1

Guard your steps when you go to the house of God.
The temple in Jerusalem stood as the appointed center of Israel’s worship and the visible sign of God’s dwelling among His covenant people. Entering its courts required more than physical movement; worshipers were to come with reverence, self-examination, and humility before the holy God. Moses at the burning bush and Joshua near Jericho removed their sandals at God’s command because they stood on holy ground (Exodus 3:5; Joshua 5:15). The same awareness belongs to all who seek God’s presence. Psalm 24:3–4 asks who may ascend the hill of the Lord and answers: the one with clean hands and a pure heart.

Psa 24:3-4 Who may ascend the hill of the LORD? Who may stand…

Psa 15:1-2 A Psalm of David. O LORD, who may abide in Your tent?…

Heb 12:28-29 Therefore, since we are receiving an unshakable kingdom,…

Draw near to listen rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools,
God desires receptive obedience above religious activity performed without faith or submission. Samuel told Saul, “To obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Samuel 15:22), exposing the emptiness of offerings that attempt to cover a disobedient heart. Israel’s sacrifices were ordained by God, yet the prophets repeatedly condemned worship that continued alongside injustice, idolatry, and rebellion (Isaiah 1:11–17). Drawing near to listen requires yielding to God’s word and allowing it to correct conduct and direct life. Jesus likewise commended those who hear His words and put them into practice (Matthew 7:24).

Hos 6:6 For I desire mercy, not sacrifice, and the knowledge of God…

Jas 1:19 My beloved brothers, understand this: Be quick to listen…

who do not know that they do wrong.
Foolish worship can persist where people presume that outward devotion satisfies God while refusing to examine their hearts and actions. Israel often maintained religious ceremonies while ignoring the knowledge of God and His covenant commands, bringing the warnings of the prophets upon the nation (Hosea 4:6). Such blindness does not make sin innocent; it reveals the need for repentance and godly wisdom. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge (Proverbs 1:7), and Christ is God’s wisdom for those who believe (1 Corinthians 1:24).

Ecclesiastes 5:2

Do not be quick to speak,
Worship begins with listening. Ecclesiastes 5:1 warns those who enter God’s house to draw near to hear rather than offer the sacrifice of fools. Careless speech reveals a heart that has not weighed the holiness of God. Proverbs 10:19 teaches that many words invite sin, while James 1:19 calls believers to be quick to listen and slow to speak.

Jas 1:19 My beloved brothers, understand this: Be quick to listen…

Pro 29:20 Do you see a man who speaks in haste?…

and do not be hasty in your heart to utter a word before God.
Rash promises, prayers, and vows are not harmless before the Lord. Numbers 30:2 and Deuteronomy 23:21–23 require a person to fulfill what he has vowed to God. The heart must be guarded because God sees motives as well as words. Reverent prayer is sincere, truthful, and submitted to God’s will rather than driven by emotion or pressure.

Psa 19:14 May the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart…

Psa 141:3 Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch…

For God is in heaven and you are on earth.
God is exalted above His creation, while man remains dependent, finite, and accountable before Him. Heaven and earth distinguish the Creator from the creature and call worshipers to humility. God’s ways are higher than human ways, as Isaiah 55:8–9 declares, yet He graciously hears those who come before Him in faith.

Deu 4:39 Know therefore this day and take to heart…

Isa 55:8-9 For My thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways…

So let your words be few.
Few words do not mean prayer should be cold or infrequent; they mean that prayer should be thoughtful and true. Jesus warned against empty repetitions offered as though God could be persuaded by many words, for the Father knows the needs of His children before they ask Him. Reverence before God produces speech marked by honesty, restraint, and trust.

Ecclesiastes 5:3

As a dream comes through many cares,
Dreams may arise from the burdens, plans, fears, and labors that fill the mind rather than from a message from God. The Lord did reveal His will through dreams to Joseph, Daniel, and others, but Scripture also warns against accepting every dream as divine revelation. In the setting of worship, Ecclesiastes calls God’s people to approach Him with reverence, refusing the anxious and careless thoughts that can produce empty promises.

Job 7:13-14 When I think my bed will comfort me, and my couch…

Ecc 2:23 Indeed, all his days are filled with grief,…

Pro 3:24 When you lie down, you will not be afraid;…

so the speech of a fool comes with many words.
A fool’s abundance of speech reveals a heart that has not carefully considered God, truth, or the weight of one’s commitments. Many words do not make a prayer, vow, or counsel more faithful; they may instead expose presumption and lack of self-control. Solomon’s warning accords with Proverbs and with James’s command to be quick to hear and slow to speak. Reverent worship requires honest words, restrained speech, and obedience to what has been promised before God.

Pro 10:19 When words are many, sin is unavoidable,…

Pro 18:2 A fool does not delight in understanding,…

Pro 17:28 Even a fool is considered wise if he keeps silent…

Ecclesiastes 5:4

When you make a vow to God,
A vow was a voluntary but binding promise offered before the Lord, often connected with worship, thanksgiving, or a plea for His help. Israel was commanded to keep every vow made to God (Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21–23). The Lord does not require His people to make vows, but once a promise is spoken in His name, it becomes a matter of covenant faithfulness.

Deu 23:21 If you make a vow to the LORD your God, do not be slow…

Psa 76:11 Make and fulfill your vows to the LORD your God;…

do not delay in fulfilling it,
Prompt obedience reveals that the vow arose from a sincere heart. To postpone fulfillment while enjoying God’s mercy treats lightly the holy God to whom the promise was made. Deuteronomy warned that delayed vows could become sin, for God holds His people accountable for what their lips have pledged.

because He takes no pleasure in fools.
Foolishness in Ecclesiastes is not mere lack of intelligence but a life lived without the fear of God. A person who speaks carelessly before the Lord and then fails to act shows contempt for God’s holiness. God sees beyond outward religious words and judges the integrity of the worshiper.

Psa 5:4-5 You are not a God who delights in wickedness;…

Fulfill your vow.
Keeping one’s word to God is an act of reverence and worship. Solomon’s instruction prepares for the warning that it is better not to vow than to vow and fail to pay (Ecclesiastes 5:5). Jesus likewise called His followers to truthful speech marked by simple faithfulness, so that their yes would be yes and their no would be no (Matthew 5:33–37).

Ecclesiastes 5:5

It is better not to vow
God does not require His people to make voluntary promises beyond His commands. A vow spoken before Him brought a person under solemn obligation, and careless words could not be treated as empty religious speech. Israel was commanded to fulfill whatever was voluntarily promised to the Lord (Deuteronomy 23:21–23; Numbers 30:2).

Deu 23:22 But if you refrain from making a vow, you will not be guilty…

Pro 20:25 It is a trap for a man to dedicate something rashly…

than to make a vow
Vows were often made in times of need, when worshipers sought God’s help or blessing. Hannah vowed that any son given to her would be dedicated to the Lord, and she faithfully brought Samuel to serve at Shiloh after God answered her prayer (1 Samuel 1:11, 27–28). Such commitments required sober reflection, not emotional bargaining or impulsive devotion.

Deu 23:21 If you make a vow to the LORD your God, do not be slow…

Num 30:2 If a man makes a vow to the LORD or swears an oath…

and not fulfill it.
An unfulfilled vow broke faith with God and revealed a heart that spoke more readily than it obeyed. God is wholly faithful to His word, and He calls His people to reflect His truthfulness in every commitment. Jesus therefore taught His disciples to speak with such integrity that a simple yes or no would be dependable (Matthew 5:33–37).

Psa 50:14 Sacrifice a thank offering to God, and fulfill your vows…

Ecclesiastes 5:6

Do not let your mouth cause your flesh to sin,
Careless words can bind a person to obligations before God that he later refuses to fulfill. Vows were voluntary, but once made they were sacred and binding (Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21–23). Speech reveals the condition of the heart and can lead the whole person into disobedience (Matthew 12:34; James 3:5–6).

Jas 3:6 The tongue also is a fire, a world of wickedness…

Jas 1:26 If anyone considers himself religious…

Pro 21:23 He who guards his mouth and tongue keeps his soul…

and do not tell the messenger that your vow was a mistake.
A messenger connected with the sanctuary may have come to receive or record what had been pledged. Claiming that a vow had been uttered in error was no excuse for withholding what had been promised to God. Israel was to approach the Lord with reverence, honesty, and deliberate obedience.

Psa 15:4 who despises the vile but honors those who fear the LORD…

Why should God be angry with your words
God hears every vow made in His name and holds His people accountable for empty promises. He delights in truthful speech and rejects lips that speak deceitfully or presumptuously (Proverbs 12:22). Jesus likewise warned against multiplying oaths and commanded His disciples to speak with simple integrity (Matthew 5:33–37).

Psa 12:3-4 May the LORD cut off all flattering lips…

and destroy the work of your hands?
God’s discipline could remove the fruit of labor, possessions, and plans that appeared secure. Such loss teaches that human effort has no lasting blessing apart from the fear of God. Faithful obedience, including honesty in what is promised, places all labor under His favor.

Ecclesiastes 5:7

For as many dreams bring futility,
Dreams may reflect restless human thoughts and desires, yet they cannot establish truth or secure lasting meaning. Though God at times gave revelatory dreams to Joseph, Daniel, and others, Ecclesiastes warns against trusting subjective impressions that foster empty confidence. In the setting of worship and vows, human imagination must yield to God’s revealed will.

Zec 10:2 For idols speak deceit and diviners see illusions…

Jer 23:25-32 I have heard the sayings of the prophets…

Deu 13:1-3 If a prophet or dreamer of dreams arises among you…

so do many words.
A flood of speech often produces empty promises, careless vows, and excuses before God. Ecclesiastes has already urged worshipers not to be hasty with their mouths, for God is in heaven and man is on earth. Wisdom listens carefully, speaks truthfully, and fulfills what it promises.

Jas 1:19 My beloved brothers, understand this: Be quick to listen…

Therefore, fear God.
Reverent submission to God rescues life from the emptiness of self-directed dreams and speech. God sees every vow, judges every word, and alone gives enduring purpose to human labor. The fear of the LORD (Yahweh, “I AM”) leads the worshiper to humility, obedience, and trust in His sovereign rule.

The Futility of Wealth

Ecclesiastes 5:8

If you see the oppression of the poor and the denial of justice and righteousness in the province,
Israel’s law required rulers and judges to protect the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the resident alien rather than exploiting their weakness (Exodus 23:6; Deuteronomy 24:17). Yet officials often used their authority to seize property, accept bribes, and deny justice, as the prophets repeatedly charged (Isaiah 1:23; Micah 3:9–11). The province represents the ordinary places of public life where rulers were meant to administer justice but frequently served their own interests.

Ecc 4:1 I looked, and I considered all the oppression under the sun.…

Isa 10:1-2 Woe to those who enact unjust statutes…

Jer 22:3 This is what the LORD says: Administer justice…

do not be astonished at the matter;
Such evil should grieve God’s people, but it should not overturn their faith in God’s rule. Fallen human hearts corrupt even structures established for order and protection. Ecclesiastes faces the painful reality that injustice may persist for a time, while directing the reader to remember that God sees what earthly courts ignore and will finally judge every deed (Ecclesiastes 12:14).

Ecc 1:9-10 What has been will be again, and what has been done…

for one official is watched by a superior,
Government in the ancient world operated through layers of administrators who answered to rulers above them. A lower official could oppress the people while seeking favor or protection from a superior, and appeals for justice could be delayed or silenced within the system. Human authority, though necessary for civil order, cannot remove the sin that resides in those who wield it.

Mat 8:9 For I myself am a man under authority, with soldiers…

Luk 7:8 For I myself am a man under authority, with soldiers…

Rom 13:1-2 Everyone must submit himself to the governing authorities…

and others higher still are over them.
The chain of authority reaches upward through increasingly powerful rulers, often leaving the poor with little recourse. No earthly hierarchy, however, stands beyond God’s authority. The Lord is higher than every king and official, and His throne is founded on righteousness and justice (Psalm 89:14). Christ will ultimately establish the just kingdom that human rulers have failed to provide (Isaiah 9:6–7).

Ecclesiastes 5:9

The produce of the earth is taken by all;
God’s provision through the land sustains every level of society. From laborers and servants to merchants and landowners, all depend upon seedtime, rain, harvest, and the ordinary bounty of creation. Israel’s gleaning laws required landowners to leave portions for the poor and the foreigner, showing that the earth’s abundance was never meant to be treated as the possession of the powerful alone (Leviticus 19:9–10). Human wealth ultimately rests upon resources God gives and continually upholds.

the king himself profits from the fields.
Even a king depends upon the productivity of the land and the labor of those who cultivate it. Royal taxes, tribute, food supplies, and national stability were bound to the harvest, as Joseph’s administration in Egypt demonstrated during years of abundance and famine (Genesis 41:34–36). Earthly rulers may possess authority, but they remain dependent upon God’s created order. The king’s need for the field humbles human power and reminds all people that God alone is the true source of provision.

Ecclesiastes 5:10

He who loves money is never satisfied by money,
Wealth promises security and freedom, yet the heart devoted to it continually demands more. Israel’s agrarian economy measured prosperity in land, flocks, harvests, and silver, but abundance could not protect anyone from death, injustice, drought, or the judgment of God. Paul likewise warns that the love of money leads people into many griefs and away from faith (1 Timothy 6:10).

Luk 12:15 And he said to them, “Watch out! Guard yourselves…

and he who loves wealth is never satisfied by income.
Increased earnings do not cure covetousness; they often enlarge desire and anxiety. Jesus warned that life does not consist in the abundance of possessions (Luke 12:15), and He called His disciples to seek treasure in heaven rather than earthly gain (Matthew 6:19–21). Contentment rests not in what a person receives, but in trustful dependence on God, who gives every good gift.

Pro 28:22 A stingy man hastens after wealth and does not know…

This too is futile.
Money is temporary, cannot redeem the soul, and cannot provide lasting joy. Ecclesiastes exposes the emptiness of pursuing created things as though they were the source of life. Material resources are good when received with gratitude and used faithfully, but they become a cruel master when loved. Only the fear of God and fellowship with Him give earthly labor and possessions their proper place.

Ecclesiastes 5:11

When good things increase,
Material abundance often brings a larger household, greater obligations, and more people who expect to share in its benefits. In Israel and the wider ancient world, prosperous landowners supported servants, laborers, relatives, guests, and dependents. Wealth could be received as God’s provision, yet it also exposed the heart to pride and misplaced trust (Proverbs 11:28).

Increased possessions attract those who benefit from them, whether through legitimate service, family need, or selfish advantage. The owner discovers that abundance does not remove burdens; it often multiplies them. James likewise warns that riches gained or guarded without righteousness bring accountability before God (James 5:1–6).

The Preacher asks whether the accumulation itself gives lasting gain to the person who possesses it. A man may control great resources while lacking peace, contentment, or freedom from anxiety. Earthly wealth cannot secure the soul or preserve life, as Christ taught when He asked what profit there is in gaining the world and forfeiting one’s soul (Matthew 16:26).

Honest labor brings bodily weariness and the quiet satisfaction of duties faithfully performed. God gave mankind work in the garden before the fall, and Scripture honors diligent labor as a means of provision and service (Genesis 2:15; Proverbs 12:11).

Ecclesiastes 5:13

Solomon identifies a painful reality in fallen human life: possessions meant to serve people can become instruments of sorrow. Life in the world often exposes the limits of earthly security and the misery produced by trusting in what cannot endure.

Wealth guarded selfishly can enslave the one who possesses it through fear, anxiety, isolation, and false confidence. The owner may lose the ability to enjoy God’s gifts, neglect those in need, and become spiritually impoverished while materially rich. Jesus warned against laying up treasures on earth, where they perish or can be taken away (Matthew 6:19–21), and Paul taught that the love of money brings many sorrows (1 Timothy 6:9–10). Wealth is a gift to be received with gratitude and used responsibly, not a master to be served.

Ecclesiastes 5:14

or wealth lost in a failed venture,
Material prosperity can disappear through failed business, disaster, theft, war, or other circumstances beyond human control. Ancient trade and agriculture involved substantial risk from drought, crop failure, unsafe roads, changing rulers, and dishonest partners. Proverbs 23:4-5 warns that riches can suddenly fly away, and 1 Timothy 6:17 forbids confidence in uncertain wealth. Jesus’ parable of the rich fool likewise exposes the danger of treating accumulated goods as lasting security while neglecting one’s standing before God (Luke 12:16-21).

A son ordinarily represented the continuation of a family’s name, land, labor, and inheritance. Israel’s inheritance laws sought to preserve a family’s place among God’s people, making the loss of a father’s resources especially grievous (Deuteronomy 21:15-17). The father’s failed venture affects not only his own comfort but also the earthly prospects of his child. Parents bear real responsibility to provide wisely, yet they cannot guarantee material security for the next generation.

Wealth cannot secure a permanent legacy, because death and loss ultimately remove human control over possessions. Ecclesiastes repeatedly confronts the sorrow of laboring for gain that may be lost or left to someone else. God’s people are therefore called to seek an inheritance that cannot perish, spoil, or fade: the living hope given through Christ (1 Peter 1:3-4). Earthly provision remains a good gift to receive gratefully and use faithfully, but lasting treasure is found in belonging to God and investing one’s life in His kingdom (Matthew 6:19-21; Hebrews 12:28).

Every person enters the world helpless, possessing nothing earned or owned. Human life begins by God’s gift and remains wholly dependent on Him, recalling that mankind is God’s creature, formed by His hand and sustained by His breath.

Labor, wealth, and achievement have value within God’s purposes, yet none can secure life beyond the grave or be carried into eternity. Paul repeats the same truth: “We brought nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it” (1 Timothy 6:7).

Ecclesiastes 5:16

This too is a grievous affliction:
The certainty that wealth, achievement, and labor cannot be carried beyond death weighs heavily on the Preacher’s heart. Even the greatest earthly success cannot remove the curse of mortality introduced through sin. The phrase highlights the deep sorrow and frustration that the Preacher, traditionally understood as Solomon, whose name means “peaceful,” feels about the futility of human endeavors. The term “grievous affliction” suggests a profound burden or misfortune, echoing the themes of vanity and meaninglessness found throughout Ecclesiastes. This sentiment is consistent with the book's exploration of life's transient nature and the limitations of human wisdom and effort.

Every person enters the world with empty hands and leaves it the same way. Job confessed, “Naked I came from my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return” (Job 1:21), and Paul likewise taught that “we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world” (1 Timothy 6:7). Kings and laborers alike stand equal before the grave.

Labor devoted only to temporary possessions and personal advancement cannot secure lasting profit. Work itself is God’s good gift, but work pursued apart from God becomes a futile attempt to grasp what cannot be held. Christ directs His people to store up treasure in heaven, where death and decay cannot destroy it (Matthew 6:19–21).

Ecclesiastes 5:17

Moreover, all his days
Life apart from the fear of God is portrayed as a continuing burden rather than a season of hardship. Human labor has been marked by sweat, frustration, and mortality since the curse following Adam’s sin (Genesis 3:17–19). Wealth cannot remove the deeper misery of a heart that lives without gratitude, contentment, or hope beyond what is gained under the sun.

Ecclesiastes 5:18

Here is what I have seen to be good and fitting:
After exposing the emptiness of wealth, oppression, and restless striving, the Teacher identifies a way of life that accords with God’s design. Human wisdom cannot master every mystery of providence, yet it can recognize the goodness of receiving life as God’s gift.

In Israel’s life, eating and drinking often belonged to fellowship, worship, celebration, and the enjoyment of God’s provision. Such enjoyment is not self-indulgence but thankful recognition that daily bread comes from the Lord.

Ecclesiastes 5:19

Furthermore, God has given riches and wealth to every man,
Material possessions ultimately belong to God, who gives according to His wise purposes. Israel was warned not to credit personal strength or skill for prosperity, but to remember that God gives power to produce wealth (Deuteronomy 8:18). Wealth may reveal God’s kindness, yet it is never a measure of personal righteousness or a guarantee of His approval. What God entrusts must be received with humility and managed as stewardship before Him.

and He has enabled him to enjoy them,
Possessing abundance does not ensure satisfaction. Ecclesiastes repeatedly observes that riches can bring anxiety, isolation, and an endless desire for more. God must grant the inward capacity to receive earthly blessings with gratitude rather than fear, greed, or idolatry. Believers are called to hope in God, “who richly provides us with all things for our enjoyment” (1 Timothy 6:17).

Contentment recognizes that God assigns the boundaries, opportunities, and seasons of life. Such acceptance is not passive resignation but trusting submission to God’s providence. Paul learned to be content in poverty and abundance because his strength was found in Christ, not in changing circumstances (Philippians 4:11–13).

Though sin brought painful toil into human labor, work remains a meaningful calling through which people serve others and reflect God’s order. Joy in labor comes when work is received as service to the Lord rather than merely a pursuit of gain (Colossians 3:23).

Ecclesiastes 5:20

For a man seldom considers the days of his life,
The person who receives God’s gifts with gratitude is not consumed by regret over the past or anxiety about the future. Life remains brief and uncertain, yet God frees the heart from carrying its passing days as a crushing burden. Psalm 90:12 calls believers to number their days wisely, while Ecclesiastes shows that such wisdom includes receiving each day from God rather than trying to control it.

Joy in Ecclesiastes is God’s answer to the emptiness of wealth, achievement, and pleasure pursued apart from Him.
