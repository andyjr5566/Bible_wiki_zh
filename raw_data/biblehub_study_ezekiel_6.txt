Judgment against Idolatry

Ezekiel 6:1

And the word of the LORD came to me, saying,
Ezekiel received God’s message during the Babylonian exile, after he had been taken from Judah in 597 BC. Though Jerusalem still stood at this point, the nation’s persistent idolatry and rebellion had brought it under coming judgment. God continued to speak through His prophet among the exiles, revealing that distance from the land and the temple did not limit His authority or presence. The message that follows announces judgment against Israel’s mountains and high places, where idols and unlawful altars had been established.

Israel’s high places, altars, and idols could neither protect their worshipers nor avert the sword God sent against them. The dead placed before their idols would expose the utter helplessness of false worship and demonstrate that the gods Israel served had no power to save. The judgment fulfilled covenant warnings that rebellion against the Lord would bring devastation, defeat, and death (Deuteronomy 28:25–26). Sin’s wages are death, and idolatry is especially grievous because it gives to created things the honor belonging to the Creator alone.

Burial was a matter of great dignity in Israel, so unburied and scattered remains signified severe divine judgment. The very places where Israel sought blessing from false gods would become witnesses to God’s wrath against their unfaithfulness. Yet the destruction of corrupt worship pointed toward the restoration God would later bring: He would cleanse His people from idols and establish worship marked by truth, repentance, and wholehearted devotion to Him (Ezekiel 36:25; John 4:23–24).

A Remnant to Be Blessed

Ezekiel 6:8

Yet I will leave a remnant,
God would not permit Israel’s rebellion to erase His covenant purposes. The survivors would not be spared because they were innocent, for Ezekiel 6:9 shows that they would later remember the Lord and grieve over their idolatry. God’s preservation of a remnant displays both His judgment against sin and His faithfulness to the promises made to Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and David. Isaiah also foretold that a remnant would return, and Paul identified a remnant preserved by grace among Israel.

The sword points to the violence of Babylon’s invasion, which would bring death upon Jerusalem and the cities of Judah. Some would survive the slaughter, often through capture and deportation rather than deliverance from suffering. Their survival ensured that witnesses to God’s judgment would remain and that repentance could arise among those who had known His covenant and rejected it.

Israel’s exile fulfilled the covenant warnings of Leviticus and Deuteronomy, where God warned that persistent idolatry would result in dispersion among foreign peoples. Babylon carried many Judeans away in successive deportations, beginning before Jerusalem’s final fall in 586 BC. Even in exile, the Lord remained present with His people, becoming “a sanctuary” to them and promising future restoration.

Jer 9:16 I will scatter them among the nations…

The dispersion would extend beyond Judah’s borders into many regions under Babylonian control and, in later generations, farther among the nations. Israel’s scattering would not be its end. God promised to gather His people, cleanse them from idolatry, restore them to their land, and unite them under His appointed King. The preserved remnant therefore carried both the memory of judgment and the hope of restoration.
