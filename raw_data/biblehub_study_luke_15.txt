Tax collectors served Rome’s financial system and were widely regarded as compromised by their association with Gentile rule and by frequent abuses in collection. “Sinners” included people known for immoral conduct as well as those excluded by religious leaders for failing to observe their traditions. Many had been treated as beyond the reach of God’s mercy.

Luke 15:2

So the Pharisees and scribes began to grumble:
The Pharisees and scribes were influential teachers and interpreters of the Law, committed to preserving Israel’s covenant identity through obedience to Scripture and inherited traditions. Their complaint recalls Israel’s grumbling against the Lord in the wilderness, exposing resistance to God’s gracious work. Jesus’ ministry confronted their assumption that holiness required separation from those publicly known for sin. Luke places their grumbling at the opening of the parables of the lost sheep, lost coin, and lost son, revealing why Jesus told them: heaven rejoices when sinners repent.

“This man welcomes sinners
Jesus did not approve of sin or minimize its seriousness; He received sinners in order to call them to repentance and restore them to God. “Sinners” included people whose lives openly violated God’s law, as well as those treated as morally or socially unclean by the religious establishment. The Messiah came to seek and save the lost, fulfilling the servant’s mission foretold in Isaiah, who would bear the sin of many and be numbered with transgressors. Jesus’ welcome displayed the mercy of God toward people who could not make themselves righteous.

Shared meals expressed fellowship, peace, and acceptance in first-century Jewish life. Jesus’ table fellowship showed that God’s kingdom was opening to repentant sinners rather than remaining the possession of the self-righteous. His meals anticipated the future banquet of God’s salvation, when the redeemed will share joy in His presence. The grace Jesus offered at the table would be secured through His death, establishing the new covenant in which forgiveness and reconciliation are available to all who turn to Him.

This phrase indicates a continuation of a narrative. Jesus, the central figure of the New Testament, is often depicted as a teacher and storyteller. His use of parables is a hallmark of His teaching method, designed to convey deep spiritual truths in a relatable manner. In the context of Luke 15, Jesus is addressing a mixed audience of tax collectors, sinners, Pharisees, and scribes, highlighting His role as a teacher to all people, regardless of social or religious standing.

Each account reveals God’s active concern for those separated from Him and the joy of heaven when a sinner repents. The parables also expose the spirit of the Pharisees, who could not rejoice when grace was extended to those they despised.

The act of finding the sheep highlights the initiative and effort of the shepherd, reflecting God’s active pursuit of the lost. The shepherd’s search signifies the divine mission of Jesus, who came “to seek and to save the lost” (Luke 19:10).

The Parable of the Lost Coin

Luke 15:8

The ten silver coins were drachmas, each roughly a day’s wage. Such coins could represent a woman’s personal savings and may have been connected with a married woman’s dowry or coin headdress. Losing one meant losing a meaningful part of her limited resources.

Jesus continued His response to the Pharisees and scribes who criticized Him for receiving sinners and eating with them. The parable of the two sons climaxes the preceding accounts of the lost sheep and lost coin by revealing the Father’s joy over a repentant sinner and exposing the self-righteous resentment of those who refuse to share that joy.

The younger son will represent those who openly wander into sin and later repent, while the older son reflects those who remain outwardly near God yet resist His grace toward the undeserving. Both sons need reconciliation with their father, showing that rebellion may appear through open wrongdoing or through proud refusal to forgive.

This phrase highlights the prodigal son’s complete lack of foresight and responsibility. In biblical times, inheritance was a significant part of one’s identity and security. By squandering his inheritance, the son not only loses his wealth but also his social standing and future security. This reflects the spiritual state of humanity when it turns away from God, squandering the spiritual inheritance given by the Father. The act of spending all he had can be seen as a metaphor for living a life apart from God’s wisdom and guidance, leading to spiritual bankruptcy.

The famine in this parable serves as a catalyst for the son’s realization of his dire situation. It symbolizes the spiritual emptiness and desperation that result from a life lived apart from God.
