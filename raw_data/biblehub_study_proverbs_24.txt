Do Not Envy

Proverbs 24:1

Do not envy wicked men
The apparent success of those who disregard God can tempt the righteous to measure life by wealth, power, ease, or influence. Israel often faced surrounding peoples who gained advantage through violence, idolatry, and injustice. Yet their prosperity was neither secure nor blessed. Psalm 73 records Asaph’s struggle with this same temptation until he entered God’s sanctuary and understood their final end. Lasting good belongs to those who fear the Lord, walk in His ways, and await His righteous judgment.

Psa 37:1 Of David. Do not fret over those who do evil;…

Pro 3:31 Do not envy a violent man or choose any of his ways;

or desire their company;
Fellowship with those committed to evil can gradually reshape convictions, conduct, and desires. Israel’s history repeatedly shows the danger: alliances and social ties with idolatrous nations drew many into false worship and disobedience. Wisdom calls God’s people to seek companions who strengthen faithfulness rather than normalize sin. “Bad company corrupts good character” (1 Corinthians 15:33), while the blessed man of Psalm 1 refuses the counsel and path of the wicked.

The wicked nurture plans of harm within the heart, the inner seat of thought, desire, and moral purpose. Violence begins before it is committed; it is conceived in a will set against God and neighbor. Scripture repeatedly warns that outward sin grows from inward corruption (Matthew 15:19). Wisdom therefore forbids envy of evildoers or fellowship with them, since their apparent success rests on destructive purposes.

Their speech reveals and advances the evil they have planned. Words may spread deceit, provoke conflict, ruin reputations, or recruit others into sinful acts. Jesus taught that the mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart (Luke 6:45), and James warns that an uncontrolled tongue can cause great damage (James 3:5–6). God’s people must recognize that destructive speech is not harmless; it exposes a heart opposed to truth and peace.

Proverbs 24:3

A household, family, and even a kingdom require more than wealth, strength, or human ambition. God-given wisdom directs priorities, relationships, discipline, work, and worship according to the fear of the Lord, which is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10). Solomon’s reign displayed how wisdom can bring order and blessing, though his later compromise also warns that wisdom must remain joined to obedience. Jesus likewise portrayed the wise man as one who hears His words and acts on them, building upon a foundation that endures every storm (Matthew 7:24–25).

Saying 22

Proverbs 24:5

A wise man is strong,
Wisdom gives strength for righteous living, sound judgment, and endurance under pressure. Proverbs places wisdom within the fear of the Lord, so strength is not self-reliance but the ability to live according to God’s truth. Ecclesiastes 7:19 likewise declares that wisdom strengthens a man more than ten rulers in a city. In the surrounding proverbs, wisdom builds a household and provides counsel for conflict, showing that godly discernment equips a person for both daily responsibilities and serious trials.

Pro 8:14 Counsel and sound judgment are mine; I have insight…

and a man of knowledge enhances his strength.
Knowledge of God’s ways increases a person’s ability to act faithfully and effectively. It guards against deception, rash decisions, and the destructive confidence of the wicked. Proverbs teaches that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge, making reverence for God the foundation for true understanding. Christ perfectly displayed such strength, resisting Satan’s temptations through obedience to God’s Word and carrying out the Father’s will with unwavering faithfulness.

Proverbs 24:6

Only with sound guidance should you wage war,
Israel’s kings faced real military threats and were not to act presumptuously. David repeatedly sought the Lord before battle, including during conflict with the Philistines (2 Samuel 5:19, 23). God’s people were to recognize that success did not rest merely on numbers, weapons, or courage, but on obedience to the Lord’s direction. The same dependence is necessary in spiritual conflict, where believers stand firm through God’s strength, prayer, truth, righteousness, faith, and His Word (Ephesians 6:10–18).

and victory lies in a multitude of counselors.
Wise leadership receives godly counsel rather than trusting isolated judgment. Kings were often surrounded by elders, commanders, priests, and advisors whose counsel could expose danger, confirm a course of action, or restrain reckless decisions. Rehoboam’s rejection of the older counselors’ wisdom brought division to the kingdom (1 Kings 12:6–19). Counsel is safest when it is shaped by the fear of the Lord and tested against His revealed Word. Within the church, mature believers help one another pursue faithfulness, discern error, and persevere in obedience.

Days of adversity reveal whether faith has been grounded in the Lord or merely sustained by favorable circumstances. Israel’s history repeatedly displayed this testing, from slavery in Egypt to wilderness hardships, enemy invasions, and exile. God called His people to remember His works and obey Him when fear, loss, and pressure tempted them to despair. Believers are likewise commanded to stand in the evil day, clothed with God’s armor and strengthened by His power (Ephesians 6:13).

A father’s instruction to his son continues the personal pattern of Proverbs, where godly wisdom is entrusted to the next generation. Honey is good because it is a fitting gift from God, received with gratitude and enjoyed within His design.

God’s Word is sweeter than honey from the comb (Psalm 19:10), bringing true understanding, correction, and hope. Christ, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, satisfies those who receive and obey His teaching.

Saying 28

Proverbs 24:17

Do not gloat when your enemy falls,
God forbids delight in the ruin of a personal adversary. Ancient nations commonly celebrated an enemy’s defeat, but Israel was called to display the Lord’s justice without adopting the cruelty of surrounding peoples. Personal vengeance belongs to God, who judges righteously and knows every heart. David’s grief at Saul’s death and his lament for Absalom show that even a foe’s downfall should awaken sorrow rather than triumph. Jesus deepened this standard by commanding His disciples to love their enemies and pray for those who persecute them (Matthew 5:44).

Mic 7:8 Do not gloat over me, my enemy! Though I have fallen…

and do not let your heart rejoice when he stumbles,
God addresses not only outward celebration but also the hidden pleasure of the heart. Another person’s failure may expose sin and bring needed consequences, yet no believer should enjoy the misery of one made in God’s image. Joseph refused to repay his brothers for their evil against him, recognizing that God had worked through their sin to preserve life (Genesis 50:20). Such restraint leaves room for repentance, restoration, and God’s mercy. Romans 12:19 likewise calls believers to leave vengeance to the Lord, whose judgment is perfect and whose mercy remains available to the fallen.

To show partiality in judgment
Judges, elders, and other leaders were forbidden to favor the rich, powerful, poor, or personally connected. Leviticus 19:15 and Deuteronomy 16:19 require justice without bias, because the Lord Himself shows no partiality. Jehoshaphat reminded Judah’s judges that they served in the Lord’s judgment, not merely in human courts (2 Chronicles 19:6–7). The same standard governs relationships within the church, where favoritism dishonors Christ and oppresses others (James 2:1–9).

Proverbs 24:24

Whoever tells the guilty, “You are innocent”—
A judge, ruler, witness, or influential person who clears the wicked corrupts justice and treats evil as though it were good. God commanded Israel’s judges to acquit the innocent and condemn the guilty (Deuteronomy 25:1), and He calls both condemning the righteous and justifying the wicked an abomination (Proverbs 17:15). Mercy never requires denial of guilt; true mercy confronts sin and provides a righteous way of forgiveness. At the cross, God does not ignore the guilt of those He saves, but Christ bears its judgment so that God may be just while justifying those who trust in Him (Romans 3:25–26).

Proverbs 24:28

Do not testify against your neighbor without cause,
God forbids accusations that are unfounded, spiteful, or offered without sufficient knowledge. Israel’s courts depended on reliable witnesses, and a false charge could bring severe punishment, loss of property, or death. The ninth commandment protected both justice and the neighbor’s good name (Exodus 20:16). Truthful speech remains an essential expression of love for others, since believers are called to deal honestly and seek their neighbor’s welfare (Matthew 22:39; Ephesians 4:25).

Proverbs 24:30

I went past the field of a slacker
The observer deliberately considers the visible results of a life marked by neglect. In Israel’s agrarian society, a field supplied food, income, and security for a household; its condition revealed whether its owner had faithfully carried out his responsibilities. The slacker wastes resources entrusted to him and allows opportunities for fruitful labor to pass away. Proverbs repeatedly warns that laziness brings want, while diligent labor ordinarily leads to provision (Proverbs 6:6–11; 10:4; 13:4).

A vineyard required patient cultivation, pruning, protection, and timely harvest. Its neglect exposed not merely a lack of effort but a failure of wisdom, since the owner did not recognize the consequences of inaction. Scripture often portrays vineyards as blessings placed under human stewardship, and their fruitfulness depends upon faithful care. The ruined vineyard prepares the warning that follows: folly left unchecked produces spiritual, moral, and material decay. Believers are likewise called to watchfulness and fruitful obedience, abiding in Christ rather than permitting sin and neglect to overtake the heart (John 15:1–8).
