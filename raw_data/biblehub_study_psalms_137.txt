By the Rivers of Babylon

Psalm 137:1

By the rivers of Babylon
Babylon’s waterways and canals became gathering places for Judean exiles after Jerusalem fell to Nebuchadnezzar in 586 BC. Far from the land God had given them, they lived amid the prosperity of an empire that had destroyed their city and Temple. The setting emphasizes both their displacement and their longing for the place where the Lord had set His name.

Ezk 1:1-3 In the thirtieth year, on the fifth day of the fourth month,…

Dan 1:1-2 In the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim king of Judah,…

we sat and wept
Their posture reflected public mourning and helpless grief. The exiles mourned Jerusalem’s destruction, the loss of worship at the Temple, and the consequences of Judah’s covenant unfaithfulness. Yet their tears also showed that captivity had not erased their love for God’s promises or their identity as His people.

Lam 1:2 She weeps aloud in the night, with tears upon her cheeks.…

Jer 9:1 Oh, that my head were a spring of water,…

when we remembered Zion.
Zion represented Jerusalem, the Temple, David’s throne, and the Lord’s dwelling among His people. Remembering Zion stirred sorrow because of what had been lost, but it also preserved hope. God had judged Judah, yet He had not abandoned His covenant, and He would ultimately bring a remnant back to Jerusalem.

Psalm 137:2

There on the willows
The willow-lined banks of Babylon’s rivers and canals became a place of communal mourning for Judah’s exiles. Far from Jerusalem and the temple, they remembered Zion as the center of worship, covenant life, and God’s promised dwelling among His people.

The harps used for praise and celebration were set aside because grief had silenced their songs. Babylonian captivity had not destroyed God’s covenant, but it had brought the judgment warned of by the prophets. Their suspended instruments testified to loss, repentance, and longing for the day when the Lord would restore His people to Zion.

Babylonian conquerors held the exiles far from Jerusalem after the city and temple were destroyed in 586 BC. Israel’s sacred songs, once sung in worship before the Lord, were demanded as entertainment by those who had devastated their nation.

Zion, Jerusalem, represented God’s dwelling place, David’s throne, and the center of Israel’s covenant life. The captors’ mockery sharpened the exiles’ longing for restoration, while also preserving their hope that the Lord would again bring joy to Zion.

Psalm 137:4

How can we sing
Israel’s captives struggled to offer songs of worship while living under the grief, shame, and oppression of exile. The destruction of Jerusalem and the temple had made celebration seem impossible, yet their sorrow revealed how deeply worship belonged to their identity as God’s covenant people.

a song of the LORD
The sacred songs of Israel proclaimed the LORD’s holiness, His mighty acts of deliverance, and His faithfulness to His promises. Babylonian mockery sought entertainment from what Israel regarded as worship. Songs once sung in joyful procession before the LORD could not be treated as amusement for those who dishonored Zion and its God.

Babylon was the land of captivity after Jerusalem fell in 586 BC. Far from the temple and surrounded by idolatry, the exiles mourned both their sin and the loss of their homeland. Their lament did not mean that the LORD had abandoned them; He remained God in Babylon as well as Jerusalem, preserving His people and promising restoration through the prophets.

Psalm 137:5

If I forget you, O Jerusalem,
Jerusalem stood as the covenant city where God placed His name and where the temple testified to His dwelling among His people. Though the captives lived far away in Babylon, they refused to treat exile as a reason to abandon the worship, promises, and identity God had given Israel. Their longing anticipated the return from captivity and ultimately points to the future peace and reign of God associated with Jerusalem (Isaiah 2:2–3; Psalm 122:6).

Jer 51:50 You who have escaped the sword, depart and do not linger!…

Isa 62:6-7 On your walls, O Jerusalem, I have posted watchmen;…

may my right hand cease to function.
The psalmist calls down judgment upon his own strength and skill if he allows Jerusalem to fade from his heart. The right hand commonly represented power, work, and ability, so its loss would silence both labor and the music of worship. Remembering Jerusalem meant remaining faithful to the Lord who had chosen the city, established His covenant, and promised restoration to His repentant people.

Psalm 137:6

May my tongue cling to the roof of my mouth
The psalmist invokes a severe self-imposed curse: may he lose the power of speech if he becomes indifferent to Jerusalem. Silence would mean the loss of song, prayer, praise, and public testimony to the Lord’s works. In exile, when Babylon sought to turn Israel’s sacred songs into entertainment, the ability to speak and sing for God remained inseparable from covenant faithfulness.

Ezk 3:26 I will make your tongue stick to the roof of your mouth…

Psa 22:15 My strength is dried up like a potsherd…

Lam 4:4 The nursing infant’s tongue clings in thirst…

if I do not remember you,
Remembering Jerusalem requires more than retaining memories of its streets or former glory. It calls for loyal devotion to the city God chose for His name, the temple where sacrifices were offered, and the covenant promises associated with David’s throne. Israel was repeatedly commanded to remember the Lord through obedient faith; forgetting Jerusalem in Babylon would reveal a deeper forgetfulness of the God who had made Zion the center of Israel’s worship.

Isa 49:14-16 But Zion said, “The LORD has forsaken me;…

Psa 45:17 I will commemorate your name through all generations;…

if I do not exalt Jerusalem as my greatest joy!
Jerusalem stood as the earthly sign of God’s dwelling among His people and of His promised restoration after judgment. The psalmist refuses to let personal comfort, safety, or prosperity in Babylon surpass joy in God’s redeemed people and appointed worship. The longing anticipates the prophets’ vision of restored Zion and ultimately points to the New Jerusalem, where God will dwell with His people forever.

Edom descended from Esau, Jacob’s brother, yet Edom’s long hostility toward Israel culminated in cruel betrayal during Judah’s calamity. The plea asks God to bring their deeds before His righteous judgment and to uphold His covenant care for His afflicted people.

Jerusalem was destroyed by Babylon in 586 BC, bringing the kingdom of Judah to an end and sending many survivors into exile. Edom did not mourn with their kinsmen but rejoiced over the city’s ruin, as Obadiah records.

“Destroy it,” they said,
Edomite voices joined the enemies of Jerusalem, urging its devastation rather than showing mercy to a people bound to them by family history. Their delight in Judah’s suffering exposed contempt for the Lord’s people and for the city where He had placed His name.

“tear it down to its foundations!”
Edom desired not merely Jerusalem’s defeat but its complete erasure. God later condemned Edom for standing against Judah in its hour of need and promised judgment, while also preserving hope that Jerusalem would be rebuilt according to His purpose.

Psalm 137:8

O Daughter of Babylon,
Babylon, the proud capital that destroyed Jerusalem and carried Judah into exile in 586 BC, is addressed as a personified city. Her wealth, military strength, and idolatrous splendor could not shield her from the God of Israel.

Isa 47:1 Go down and sit in the dust, O Virgin Daughter of Babylon.…

Jer 50:42 They grasp the bow and spear; they are cruel and merciless.…

Rev 18:2 And he cried out in a mighty voice: “Fallen, fallen…

doomed to destruction,
God had appointed judgment for Babylon because of her cruelty, arrogance, and violence against His people. Babylon fell to the Medes and Persians in 539 BC, fulfilling the judgments proclaimed through Isaiah and Jeremiah. Her downfall foreshadows the final overthrow of every kingdom and world system that exalts itself against God.

Jer 51:8 Suddenly Babylon has fallen and been shattered.…

Rev 18:21 Then a mighty angel picked up a stone…

Isa 13:19 And Babylon, the jewel of the kingdoms,…

blessed is he who repays you
The blessing rests upon the one who carries out God’s righteous judgment against Babylon. The psalmist does not celebrate private revenge but appeals for justice according to God’s rule that evil will answer for what it has done. God remains the final Judge, and no oppression escapes His notice.

as you have done to us.
Babylon had burned the temple, ruined Jerusalem, killed many of Judah’s people, and driven survivors from their land. The requested repayment follows the principle declared by the prophets: deeds done in violence and pride return upon the guilty nation. God’s justice vindicates His people and displays His sovereignty over the nations.

Psalm 137:9

The beatitude declares that Babylon’s downfall will be regarded as righteous recompense for the violence Babylon inflicted on Jerusalem. Israel’s appeal rests on God’s covenant justice and on His promise to judge nations that exalt themselves against Him.

Babylon had shown no mercy when Jerusalem fell, and the exiles remembered the slaughter of their own children. Isaiah had foretold that Babylon would receive the same devastation it had inflicted on others (Isaiah 13:16).

and dashes them against the rocks.
The horrifying image reflects the brutal realities of ancient conquest and the depth of the exiles’ grief. The psalmist entrusts vengeance to God, who promised to repay Babylon for its evil against Zion (Jeremiah 51:24). God’s judgment against Babylon was fulfilled through its overthrow by the Medes and Persians, demonstrating that cruelty, pride, and opposition to His people do not escape His justice.
