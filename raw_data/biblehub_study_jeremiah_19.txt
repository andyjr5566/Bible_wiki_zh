Jeremiah 19:2

The Valley of Ben-hinnom lay south and southwest of Jerusalem and had become notorious for the worship of foreign gods and the sacrifice of children at Topheth (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31). God sends Jeremiah to the site of Judah’s deepest covenant unfaithfulness, where past abominations would become the setting for announced judgment. The valley later gave rise to the name Gehenna, a biblical image of final punishment, because it testified to the terrible consequences of rejecting the Lord.

The Potsherd Gate was likely a southern gate near the valley, associated with pottery production or the disposal of broken vessels. Its setting anticipates Jeremiah’s later action of breaking a clay jar before the elders and priests (Jeremiah 19:10–11). Jerusalem, hardened by persistent sin, would be shattered beyond human repair unless the people turned back to God.

Jeremiah 19:5

High places were elevated cultic sites where Israel adopted Canaanite patterns of worship rather than honoring the Lord at the place He chose. Baal, revered as a storm and fertility god, represented a direct rival to Yahweh in the life of the covenant people. Building such shrines revealed settled, public rebellion, not merely private temptation. Israel repeated the sins described in Judges 2:11–13, forsaking the Lord who had delivered them and serving the gods of the nations.

Child sacrifice displayed the dreadful end of idolatry: people made in God’s image were treated as offerings to false gods. The Law had expressly forbidden giving children to Molech and condemned the nations for such abominations (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31). Jeremiah identifies Judah’s offerings with Baal worship, showing how deeply pagan religion had been woven into the nation’s life. The people who should have taught their children to fear the Lord instead led them to destruction.

Its destruction portrayed the finality of God’s judgment upon Jerusalem and Judah after persistent idolatry, bloodshed, and rejection of His warnings. The coming Babylonian devastation would not be a passing hardship but a ruin from which the nation could not preserve itself.
