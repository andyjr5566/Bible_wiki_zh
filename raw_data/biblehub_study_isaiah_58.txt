True Fasts and Sabbaths

Isaiah 58:1

“Cry aloud, do not hold back!
God commands Isaiah to proclaim His indictment openly and urgently. Israel’s outward religion concealed covenant unfaithfulness, injustice, and self-serving worship. Like Ezekiel appointed as a watchman over Israel, Isaiah bore responsibility to warn God’s people of the danger of continuing in sin (Ezekiel 3:17–19).

Raise your voice like a ram’s horn.
The shofar produced a piercing sound used to summon Israel, announce sacred assemblies, signal warfare, and warn of approaching danger. God’s warning was not to be muted or confined to private counsel. The nation needed to hear the alarm before judgment came, just as trumpets called Israel to repentance and solemn worship.

Declare to My people their transgression
God still calls Israel “My people,” revealing that His rebuke arises from His covenant commitment rather than indifference. Their transgression involved rebellion against the God who had redeemed them and given them His law. Isaiah exposes their sin so that they may turn from empty religious observance to humble obedience, justice, and mercy.

The house of Jacob identifies the whole covenant nation descended from the patriarch Jacob. Israel’s privileged heritage did not excuse disobedience; greater light brought greater accountability. John the Baptist and Jesus likewise called God’s people to repent, for genuine faith must bear the fruit of a life submitted to God (Matthew 3:2, 8; Mark 1:15).

Isaiah 58:2

Israel maintained a regular pattern of religious observance through prayer, sacrifices, fasting, and worship at the temple. Their outward pursuit of God concealed hearts unwilling to submit to His covenant demands. The Lord sees beyond frequency and ceremony to the motives that govern worship.

God’s ways were revealed in the Law given through Moses, calling His people to holiness, mercy, faithfulness, and justice. Israel claimed pleasure in divine instruction while resisting the obedience that true knowledge requires. Jesus later exposed the same danger of honoring God with the lips while the heart remains far from Him.

Israel regarded itself as God’s chosen people and assumed that its religious identity proved its righteousness. The nation had been called to be holy and to display God’s character before surrounding peoples, yet its conduct contradicted that calling. Covenant privilege never excused covenant disobedience.

The God of Israel required justice, especially toward the poor, the oppressed, widows, and orphans. Their neglect of justice revealed that they had abandoned the practical demands of the worship they professed. God’s righteousness cannot be separated from His concern for those harmed by sin and human power.

Nearness to God is a blessing, but it cannot be separated from repentance and obedience. The Lord draws near to those who humble themselves before Him rather than those who use religious activity to conceal rebellion.

Isaiah 58:3

“Why have we fasted, and You have not seen?
The people complained that God had not rewarded their fasting with the deliverance or favor they expected. Fasting had become a means of seeking God’s benefits rather than a sincere turning from sin. The Lord sees beyond religious actions to the heart, and He will not be manipulated by outward devotion.

Their self-denial was presented as evidence that God owed them an answer. Yet genuine humility submits to God’s will and produces repentance, obedience, and mercy toward others. David recognized that God desires a broken and contrite heart rather than sacrifice offered without true repentance (Psalm 51:16–17).

They set aside food while continuing to pursue selfish interests, showing that their fast had not changed their desires or conduct. Such conduct violated the law’s commands to deal justly with hired laborers and revealed contempt for the Lord who defends the weak. Worship divorced from justice is empty, for love for God must be displayed in righteous treatment of one’s neighbor.

Fasting was appointed as a means of humbling oneself before God, grieving over sin, and seeking His mercy, not as a way to claim spiritual merit. Isaiah 58:7

Isn’t it to share your bread with the hungry,
True fasting joins devotion to God with mercy toward people in need. Bread, the daily staple of Israel, represents practical provision rather than mere sentiment. Generosity acknowledges that every provision comes from the Lord and directs His gifts toward those who lack them. The call anticipates Christ, the Bread of Life, who gives Himself for the life of the world and commands His people to reflect His compassion.

God required His people to care for the vulnerable rather than leave them exposed to hunger, danger, or public shame. Hospitality marked the righteous in Scripture, from Abraham’s welcome of strangers to Job’s refusal to let travelers remain in the street. Christian hospitality continues this concern, recognizing that believers receive others as those whom Christ welcomes.

Jesus later identified Himself with the needy, teaching that care given to the least of His brethren is received as service rendered to Him.

Israelites bore a particular responsibility to care for impoverished relatives and members of their covenant community. Neglecting one’s own kin contradicted the command to love one’s neighbor and exposed the emptiness of outward religious observance. The New Testament likewise requires believers to provide for their households and to bear one another’s burdens within the family of God.

Isaiah 58:13

If you turn your foot from breaking the Sabbath,
Israel was to cease trampling God’s appointed day by ordinary labor and self-directed activity. The Sabbath memorialized God’s rest in creation and Israel’s redemption from Egypt (Exodus 20:8–11; Deuteronomy 5:15). As a covenant sign between the Lord and Israel, it called the nation to acknowledge that its time, strength, and provision came from Him (Exodus 31:16–17).

from doing as you please on My holy day,
God rejects worship that preserves religious forms while leaving the heart devoted to personal advantage. Isaiah had already exposed fasting that coexisted with oppression, strife, and neglect of the needy (Isaiah 58:3–7). Sabbath observance required a willing pause from self-rule so that God’s holiness and mercy might govern the life of His people.

and I will make you ride on the heights of the land
God promises restoration, security, and triumph over the conditions of humiliation and oppression that had marked Judah’s disobedience. The image recalls Deuteronomy 32:13, where the Lord caused Israel to ride on the high places of the earth as He supplied and protected His people. Habakkuk also portrays the Lord as giving His servant feet like a deer to walk securely on the heights (Habakkuk 3:19).

and feed you with the heritage of your father Jacob.”
Jacob’s heritage includes the covenant inheritance promised through Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob: the land, God’s preserving presence, and the blessing of belonging to Him as His covenant people. God had reaffirmed these promises to Jacob at Bethel (Genesis 28:13–15). Faithful Israelites would know anew the covenant blessings that rebellion had forfeited in experience, while God’s purposes for Jacob’s descendants remained secure.
