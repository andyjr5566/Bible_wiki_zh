Job’s Former Blessings

Job 29:1

And Job continued his discourse:
Job resumed his final extended speech, recorded in Job 29–31, after his friends had exhausted their arguments. He would remember the favor and honor he once knew, grieve his present humiliation, and reaffirm his integrity before God. The formal character of his speech reflects the wisdom culture of the ancient patriarchal world, where truth and justice were publicly considered and defended. Job’s suffering had not erased his faith in God’s righteousness, though it had brought him to the limits of human understanding. His endurance anticipates the patience commended in James 5:11 and points forward to Christ, the righteous sufferer who endured rejection without surrendering obedience to the Father.

Job 29:2

“How I long for the months gone by,
Job begins a sustained remembrance of the life he knew before his calamities. His former days included abundant possessions, honored standing in the community, a thriving household, and freedom from the bodily anguish that now consumes him. His grief is intensified by memory, because he knows what has been lost. Job does not deny God’s former gifts; he remembers them with gratitude and sorrow. Like the psalmist who recalled worshiping among God’s people in Psalm 42:4, Job’s recollection exposes the painful contrast between past joy and present affliction.

Job understood his former prosperity as evidence of God’s preserving care, not merely the result of his own wisdom or effort. He now feels abandoned, though the opening chapters make clear that God has not surrendered him to Satan’s hand and has set limits on his suffering. Job’s lament reveals how severely suffering can obscure the assurance of divine nearness. The God who watched over Job remained sovereign even when His purposes were hidden. Scripture later presents Christ as the Good Shepherd who knows, guards, and lays down His life for His sheep, giving enduring security that is not measured by changing circumstances.

Job 29:25

I chose their course and presided as chief.
Job recalls the wisdom and authority God had granted him in former days. Men sought his counsel, and his judgment helped direct the community in matters of justice, protection, and peace. Like elders who sat at the city gate to settle disputes and govern the people (Ruth 4:1–2), Job exercised leadership that was respected because it was joined to righteousness. His place resembles Moses’ work of judging Israel and guiding the people according to God’s ways (Exodus 18:13–26). Wise counsel preserves a people from ruin, for “in the multitude of counselors there is safety” (Proverbs 11:14).

So I dwelt as a king among his troops,
Job was not claiming a royal title but describing the honor, influence, and security that surrounded him. A faithful king stood among his people as their defender and shepherd, bearing responsibility for their welfare. David was called to shepherd Israel as its ruler (2 Samuel 5:2), joining courage in battle with care for the nation. Job had known similar esteem among those under his influence, dwelling among them not as a distant tyrant but as one whose presence brought order and confidence. His remembrance heightens the grief of his present humiliation, when those who once honored him now despise him.

as a comforter of the mourners.
Job’s leadership included mercy toward the afflicted. He did not merely render judgments; he entered the sorrow of the grieving and gave strength to the brokenhearted. Such compassion reflects God’s own concern for those who mourn and anticipates the ministry of Christ, who proclaimed comfort for mourners and healing for the crushed in spirit (Isaiah 61:1–3). The Lord Jesus pronounced blessing upon those who mourn, promising that they would be comforted (Matthew 5:4). Job’s former life displayed the fruit of a man who feared God: authority was used to protect, counsel to guide, and strength to console.
