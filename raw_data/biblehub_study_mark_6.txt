The Rejection at Nazareth

Mark 6:1

Jesus continued His ministry after displaying His authority over disease and death in the healing of the woman with the hemorrhage and the raising of Jairus’s daughter. His movement from place to place fulfilled His mission to proclaim the kingdom of God throughout the towns of Israel (Luke 4:43). The Lord did not remain where His power had already been seen; He carried the message of repentance, faith, and salvation to those who had not yet heard.

Jesus returned to Nazareth, the Galilean village where He had been raised. Though the prophets foretold that the Messiah would be called a Nazarene (Matthew 2:23), Nazareth possessed no worldly prominence, and its people would struggle to receive the One they had known as a child. Their familiarity with Jesus would become an occasion for unbelief rather than faith, revealing the sorrowful truth that a prophet is often rejected among his own people (Mark 6:4).

The disciples traveled with Jesus as witnesses of His works, His teaching, and the growing opposition to His ministry. They were being prepared for their future calling to proclaim the gospel after His resurrection. Their presence also showed that Jesus was gathering a new people around Himself, bound not by hometown or family reputation but by faith and obedience to God.

The people of Nazareth could not explain how the carpenter they had known possessed such knowledge of God and His Word. Their question exposed the tension between Jesus’ humble earthly circumstances and His true identity as the eternal Son sent into the world.

His works of power confirmed that God’s kingdom had drawn near in Him. The healings and other miracles fulfilled messianic expectations, including the restoration promised in Isaiah 35:5–6, yet many in Nazareth saw the evidence without yielding to the One who performed it.

Mark 6:3

Isn’t this the carpenter,
Jesus spent much of His earthly life in ordinary labor before beginning His public ministry. His humble occupation accorded with Isaiah’s portrait of the Servant who possessed no outward majesty to compel human admiration (Isaiah 53:2). The One through whom all things were made willingly worked with His hands among the people of Nazareth, revealing the dignity of faithful labor and His power to restore what sin has broken.

Identifying Him by His mother may carry the townspeople’s contempt or skepticism concerning His birth, yet it also points to the reality of the virgin birth foretold in Scripture. James later became a leading elder in the Jerusalem church and wrote the Epistle of James, while Judas is commonly identified with Jude, author of the Epistle of Jude. His brothers did not initially believe in Him (John 7:5), but the risen Christ appeared to James, and His family later joined the believers in prayer (1 Corinthians 15:7; Acts 1:14).

Jesus’ sisters remained among the people of Nazareth, further confirming how thoroughly His life was known in the village. Their familiarity with His family and former work should have prepared them to consider His works carefully, but it instead became a barrier to faith.

The people of Nazareth rejected Jesus because they could not reconcile His humble background with His authority, wisdom, and mighty works. Their response fulfilled the pattern of the despised Servant described in Isaiah 53:3. Familiarity became contempt, and preconceived expectations blinded them to the presence of the Messiah in their midst.

Jesus stood in the line of God’s prophets, who often confronted unbelief and suffered rejection from those to whom they were sent. Nazareth’s response reflected the pattern foretold in Scripture: the Servant of the Lord would be despised and rejected, yet God’s saving purpose would not be hindered.

Mark 6:5

Nazareth’s unbelief did not diminish Jesus’ divine power; it revealed their settled rejection of Him. Having known Him as the carpenter and as a member of Mary’s household, many refused to receive the authority and identity displayed in His teaching. Their response fulfilled the pattern of Israel’s rejection of prophets sent from among them. Jesus ordinarily performed signs in settings where people came to Him in faith, but Nazareth’s hardness of heart left little openness to His ministry.

Even amid widespread unbelief, Jesus showed compassion to those who came with need. His laying on of hands personally conveyed care and blessing, and the healings demonstrated that His mercy and power remained unchanged. The few who were healed stood as witnesses that Nazareth’s rejection was not caused by any failure in Jesus, but by the people’s unwillingness to believe.

Jesus’ astonishment revealed the seriousness of Nazareth’s rejection. The people had heard His wisdom, witnessed His mighty works, and knew His family, yet familiarity hardened them against faith. Their response fulfilled the pattern of the rejected Servant, “despised and rejected by men” (Isaiah 53:3). Nazareth stands in sharp contrast to the centurion, whose faith caused Jesus to marvel (Matthew 8:10). Jesus’ words that a prophet is without honor in his hometown exposed the spiritual blindness that can arise when people judge God’s work by merely human expectations.

His persistence displayed both the breadth of God’s mercy and the urgency of repentance and faith. Jesus’ ministry among the villages also anticipated the later sending of His disciples to carry the gospel beyond familiar places and ultimately to all nations.

The Ministry of the Twelve

Mark 6:7

Then Jesus called the Twelve to Him
Jesus gathered the apostles He had appointed earlier, setting apart the twelve as representatives of renewed Israel and servants of His kingdom. Their mission proceeded from His presence and under His direction.

Luk 6:13 When daylight came, He called His disciples to Him…

Mrk 3:13 Then Jesus went up on the mountain and called…

and began to send them out two by two,
The apostles went as pairs, providing mutual encouragement, protection, and confirmation of their testimony according to the biblical principle that a matter is established by two witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15). Their work extended Jesus’ ministry into the surrounding towns.

Jesus entrusted His own authority to the Twelve so that their deliverance of the oppressed would testify that God’s kingdom had come near. Their power over demons demonstrated Christ’s supremacy over Satan and fulfilled His purpose in appointing them to preach and cast out demons (Mark 3:14–15).

Mark 6:8

Jesus sent the Twelve with urgency and dependence upon God. A staff provided ordinary support and protection for a traveler, yet it also recalled Moses, through whom God displayed His power in Egypt and the wilderness. Israel ate the Passover with staff in hand, prepared to depart at God’s command (Exodus 12:11). The apostles were to go as servants under Christ’s authority, trusting the Lord who sent them.

They were not to secure their own food in advance but to receive provision from God through those who welcomed their message. God had fed Israel with manna in the wilderness, and Jesus taught His disciples to ask the Father for daily bread. Their needs would become a visible testimony that the Lord sustains those engaged in His work.

They were not to carry a traveler’s bag filled with supplies or use their mission as a means of gathering material gain. Their simplicity distinguished them from religious wanderers who sought support for themselves. The apostles were to bring the good news of God’s kingdom, not pursue earthly security or wealth.

Without funds for lodging, food, or travel, the Twelve would learn humility and reliance upon the hospitality of receptive households. Those who received Christ’s messengers would participate in the work by caring for them. Jesus required His apostles to embody the message they proclaimed: the kingdom of God calls people away from self-reliance and toward faith in the Father’s faithful provision.

Sandals equipped the disciples for the roads and villages of Galilee, underscoring the mobility and urgency of their mission. They were to go as servants sent by Christ, prepared to travel wherever He directed rather than settling into comfort. Their readiness anticipated the believer’s call to stand and move in the peace of the gospel (Ephesians 6:15).

A second tunic would have provided additional comfort and security for an extended journey. Jesus limited their provisions so that they would learn dependence on God rather than self-sufficiency. Their needs would be met through the Lord’s care and through the hospitality of those who received His messengers. Like Israel receiving daily manna in the wilderness, the disciples were to trust God for each day’s provision (Exodus 16; Matthew 6:25–34).

Jesus sent the disciples as His authorized messengers, proclaiming repentance and the nearness of God’s kingdom. Hospitality carried serious moral weight in Jewish society, especially toward travelers and teachers of God’s word. Refusing the disciples’ welcome and refusing their message amounted to rejecting the One who sent them. Israel’s history included repeated rejection of prophets, as Jeremiah testified when the people would not listen to the servants God sent “day after day” (Jeremiah 7:25–27). Hearers remained responsible for their response to the truth brought before them.

Jesus employed the action as a solemn sign that a town rejecting God’s message had placed itself outside the blessings offered through the gospel. The disciples were not to remain where their witness had been decisively refused, nor were they to carry the burden of another person’s unbelief. Their duty was faithful proclamation; accountability for rejection belonged to those who heard.

The dust left behind would bear public witness that the disciples had brought God’s message and had been rejected. Such testimony anticipated the day when God judges every response to His revealed truth. Moses set before Israel life and death and called heaven and earth as witnesses to their choice (Deuteronomy 30:19). Paul and Barnabas later followed this instruction at Pisidian Antioch when opposition hardened against the gospel (Acts 13:51). God’s servants are called to speak faithfully, while those who reject Christ remain answerable to Him.

Their mission anticipated the wider calling of the church to make disciples among all nations.

The Beheading of John

Mark 6:14

Now King Herod heard about this,
King Herod was Herod Antipas, ruler of Galilee and Perea and son of Herod the Great. Though technically a tetrarch, he was commonly called king. Jesus’ public ministry had spread throughout the territory under Herod’s rule, including the miracles, authoritative teaching, and deliverance of many from disease and demons. The ruler who had silenced John the Baptist now could not ignore the greater witness arising through Jesus.

Mat 14:1 At that time Herod the tetrarch heard the reports…

Luk 23:8-9 When Herod saw Jesus, he was greatly pleased.…

for Jesus’ name had become well known,
Jesus was no longer known only in Nazareth or Capernaum. His works revealed the presence of God’s kingdom and compelled people throughout Galilee to consider who He was. His growing reputation brought faith to some, opposition to others, and troubled conviction to Herod.

Mrk 1:28 And the news about Jesus spread quickly…

Luk 4:14 Jesus returned to Galilee in the power of the Spirit…

and people were saying, “John the Baptist has risen from the dead!
Herod had ordered John’s execution after making a reckless oath at a banquet, yielding to Herodias’s hatred of John’s righteous rebuke. The report of Jesus’ miracles awakened Herod’s guilty conscience. John and Jesus were distinct persons, yet Herod’s fear showed that he recognized John as a true man of God whose message could not be silenced by death.

The people wrongly identified Jesus as John returned from the dead, but they correctly recognized that His works displayed supernatural power. Jesus’ miracles testified to His authority over sickness, evil spirits, nature, and death, fulfilling God’s promises that the coming Messiah would bring healing and restoration.

Elijah’s departure to heaven without dying led Israel to expect his return before the day of the Lord (2 Kings 2:11; Malachi 4:5–6). Jesus’ miracles, authority, and confrontation of corrupt religion stirred such expectations. John the Baptist had already come in the spirit and power of Elijah, preparing the way for the Messiah, yet Jesus Himself was greater than Elijah: He was the promised King whom Elijah’s ministry anticipated.

The people recognized that Jesus spoke God’s word with authority, called sinners to repentance, and displayed divine power through His works. Like the prophets, He exposed unbelief, confronted sin, and summoned Israel back to covenant faithfulness. Yet Jesus did not merely deliver messages from God; He was the Son sent by the Father, the final and fullest revelation of God.

Israel’s prophets had faithfully testified to God amid opposition and had pointed forward to the coming Redeemer. Comparing Jesus to them rightly connected His ministry with God’s long-standing purposes for His people, but it did not yet confess His true identity. Jesus was not simply another prophet in Israel’s history; He was the Messiah to whom the prophets bore witness.

Herod feared that God had raised John to confront him in judgment. His conclusion was mistaken, for Jesus was not John returned, but the promised Christ whom John had announced. Yet Herod’s fear testified that no ruler’s power can silence God’s truth or escape His justice.

His works and teaching had become widely known throughout Galilee. The residents of Gennesaret quickly spread the news and brought the sick from the surrounding area, confident that His presence brought healing. Their response contrasts with the unbelief He encountered in Nazareth and displays the signs associated with the promised Messiah, who would open blind eyes, strengthen the weak, and restore the afflicted.

Mark 6:56

And wherever He went—villages and towns and countrysides—
Jesus carried His ministry throughout every kind of settlement in Galilee and the surrounding region. His mercy reached rural villages, growing towns, and isolated farming areas alike, showing that no person or place lay beyond His concern. The Messiah proclaimed good news to the poor and bound up the brokenhearted, as Isaiah had foretold (Isaiah 61:1).

Marketplaces were the public centers of ancient communities, where people gathered for trade, news, and civic life. Families brought their afflicted relatives where Jesus would pass, openly confessing their need and trusting His power to heal. The healings displayed before entire communities spread the report of His authority and testified that the kingdom of God had drawn near.

and begged Him just to let them touch the fringe of His cloak.
The fringe referred to the tassels worn on the corners of an Israelite’s garment in obedience to Numbers 15:38–39. Those who sought even this small contact approached Jesus with the confidence that His power was sufficient for their need. Their actions recall the woman healed after touching His garment, when Jesus commended her faith and sent her away in peace (Mark 5:25–34).
