The Lord’s Prayer

Luke 11:1

One day in a place where Jesus had just finished praying,
Prayer marked Jesus’ earthly ministry. He regularly sought the Father in solitary settings, especially before major decisions and moments of ministry (Luke 5:16; 6:12; 9:28). The unnamed location directs attention away from sacred sites and toward continual fellowship with God. Though fully divine, the Son lived in obedient dependence upon the Father and showed His followers that prayer belongs at the center of faithful life.

The disciple had watched Jesus pray and recognized a depth of communion with God that he desired to share. Jewish disciples commonly received instruction from their teachers concerning the patterns and content of prayer. The request reveals humility, spiritual hunger, and confidence that Jesus could lead His followers into a relationship with God unlike mere religious routine.

“Lord” acknowledges Jesus’ authority over His disciples. Prayer is not merely a natural impulse; believers must be taught to approach God according to His revealed will. Jesus would provide a pattern that begins with the Father’s glory, seeks His kingdom, and brings every daily need, sin, and temptation before Him.

Jesus’ disciples sought a prayer that would identify them as His followers. The prayer Jesus gave them would unite them around the Father, the coming kingdom, forgiveness, dependence, and protection from temptation.

Luke 11:3

Give us each day
God’s provision is sought as a present and continuing need. Israel depended on manna in the wilderness, receiving enough for each day and learning that life is sustained by the Lord’s faithfulness rather than stored resources or human strength (Exodus 16:4–5). Jesus directs His disciples to trust the Father without anxious concern for tomorrow (Matthew 6:34).

Bread represents the necessities of bodily life, for which God is the giver. The petition is spoken in the plural, joining believers in dependence upon God and concern for one another’s needs. Jesus is also the bread of life, the One who alone gives enduring spiritual nourishment and eternal life to those who come to Him (John 6:35).

Luke 11:4

Sin is rebellion against God and leaves every person dependent on His mercy. Forgiveness rests ultimately on the atoning sacrifice of Jesus Christ, whose blood accomplishes what the sacrifices under the old covenant foreshadowed (Leviticus 16; Hebrews 9:22; 10:10). The disciple comes to the Father with confession, trusting His grace rather than personal merit.

God’s forgiven children are called to extend forgiveness to those who wrong them. Such forgiveness does not deny evil or remove the need for justice, but releases personal vengeance to God and seeks reconciliation where repentance permits. Jesus warned that an unforgiving spirit contradicts the mercy one claims to have received (Matt. 18:21–35; Ephesians 4:32).

Believers acknowledge their weakness and ask the Father to guard them from trials and circumstances in which they may be drawn toward sin. God never entices anyone to evil, yet He strengthens His people amid testing and provides a way to endure temptation (James 1:13; 1 Cor. 10:13). Jesus Himself faced Satan’s temptations in the wilderness and remained faithful, providing both an example and a faithful High Priest for His people.

A traveler arriving at night still required food and shelter, and the host bore responsibility to receive him honorably. Jesus uses the situation to teach that God’s children may bring genuine needs before their Father with persistence and confidence. Human insufficiency becomes an occasion for God’s provision, as Christ later demonstrated when He fed the multitudes with what appeared inadequate (Luke 9:10–17).

A House Divided

Luke 11:14

One day Jesus was driving out a demon that was mute.
Jesus confronted a real personal evil that had oppressed the man and manifested itself in his inability to speak. The Gospels distinguish demonic oppression from ordinary illness while also showing that Jesus has authority over every form of human brokenness. His expulsion of demons revealed the arrival of God’s kingdom and the defeat of Satan’s power. Isaiah had foretold that, in God’s coming salvation, “the tongue of the mute” would sing for joy (Isaiah 35:6).

Mat 12:22 Then a demon-possessed man who was blind and mute…

Mrk 9:25 When Jesus saw that a crowd had come running, He rebuked…

Mrk 1:34 And He healed many who were ill with various diseases…

And when the demon was gone, the man who had been mute spoke.
The man’s immediate recovery confirmed that Jesus had completely removed the source of his affliction. No ritual, medicine, or gradual recovery was involved; Christ commanded, and the oppression ended. The restoration of speech displayed the mercy of the Messiah, who came to proclaim liberty to captives and to bring deliverance to those under the power of darkness (Isaiah 61:1; Luke 4:18).

Mat 9:33 And when the demon had been driven out, the man…

Mrk 7:35 Immediately the man’s ears were opened…

Isa 35:6 Then the lame will leap like a deer, and the mute tongue…

The crowds were amazed,
The miracle astonished the witnesses because no one in Israel had demonstrated authority like Jesus’ authority over demons. Their amazement rightly recognized the greatness of what had occurred, yet the events that followed showed that amazement alone does not always lead to faith. Some would acknowledge God’s power at work, while others would harden themselves and accuse Jesus of acting by Satan’s authority.

Luke 11:15

The crowd was divided by Jesus’ deliverance of the demon-oppressed man. Rather than receiving the miracle as evidence that God’s kingdom had come near, certain opponents responded with hardened unbelief. Matthew identifies similar accusers as Pharisees, and Mark includes scribes from Jerusalem, revealing organized resistance among religious leaders.

“It is by Beelzebul,
Beelzebul, meaning “lord of the flies” or “lord of dung,” was a Jewish designation for Satan, the personal leader of the demonic realm. The name likely drew upon the contemptuous Jewish treatment of pagan idols and came to represent the supreme enemy of God. The accusation did not deny that Jesus had cast out demons; it deliberately assigned His holy work to an evil source.

Satan exercises real but limited authority over fallen angels and demons. His power remains subject to God’s sovereign rule, and his kingdom stands opposed to the kingdom proclaimed by Christ. Calling Satan the prince of demons intensified the charge by claiming that Jesus worked under the authority of God’s adversary.

Mat 9:34 But the Pharisees said, “It is by the prince of demons…

Eph 2:2 in which you used to walk when you conformed…

Jesus’ authority over demons demonstrated that He was the promised Messiah and that God’s kingdom was breaking into a world held captive by sin and evil. His works fulfilled the hope of restoration proclaimed in passages such as Isaiah 61:1. To attribute these deliverances to Satan was to reject clear divine testimony and to resist the work of the Holy Spirit through the Son of God.

A different group responded to Jesus with deliberate unbelief, seeking to challenge rather than receive Him. Their conduct resembled Israel’s testing of the Lord in the wilderness despite His demonstrated power and provision (Exodus 17:2, 7). Satan had likewise tested Jesus in the wilderness, attempting to divert Him from the Father’s will (Matthew 4:1–11). Jesus’ works of deliverance and healing had already revealed God’s kingdom at work, yet hardened hearts demanded further proof.

They sought a public, unmistakable display from above, perhaps like fire falling in Elijah’s day or manna given to Israel. Their request ignored the signs already before them and treated faith as dependent upon terms they would set. Jesus would identify His resurrection as the decisive sign given to an unbelieving generation (Matthew 12:39–40). God’s revelation in Christ calls for repentance and faith, not continual demands for proof.

Civil conflict destroys a nation from within. Israel’s division after Solomon produced the rival kingdoms of Israel and Judah, weakening both until Israel fell to Assyria and Judah later fell to Babylon (1 Kings 12; 2 Kings 17; 25). Satan could not advance his own rule by empowering Jesus to overthrow demons, since such warfare would bring ruin to his kingdom.

A divided household cannot endure because its members work against one another. Jesus’ deliverance of the demon-possessed revealed not Satan’s power at work, but the arrival of God’s kingdom against Satan’s domain (Luke 11:20). God’s people are likewise called to preserve the unity grounded in one Lord, one faith, and one Spirit (Ephesians 4:3–6).

The history of Israel’s divided monarchy illustrated how internal division brings decline and ruin (1 Kings 12). Satan’s kingdom cannot endure if its forces are working against one another; Jesus’ authority over demons therefore reveals the invasion of God’s kingdom, not cooperation with evil.

Some opponents attributed Jesus’ deliverance ministry to Beelzebul, a name associated with Satan and idolatrous opposition to God. Their accusation rejected the clear evidence of God’s power at work in Christ. Jesus’ works fulfilled His mission to destroy the works of the devil and to bring captives into freedom (1 John 3:8).

Jesus directly confronts the charge that His authority over demons came from Satan. Beelzebul was understood as the ruler of demons, so the accusation attributed God’s liberating work to the kingdom of darkness. Jesus’ miracles consistently revealed the arrival of God’s kingdom, setting afflicted people free and demonstrating His authority over Satan.

Jewish exorcists among the people and the Pharisees’ followers also claimed to cast out demons. If such works were accepted among them as evidence of God’s help, their condemnation of Jesus exposed prejudice rather than discernment.
