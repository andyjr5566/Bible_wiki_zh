Hezekiah Destroys Idolatry in Judah

2 Kings 18:1

In the third year of the reign of Hoshea son of Elah over Israel,
Hoshea was Israel’s final king, ruling during the last years before Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 BC. His third year was about 729 BC, when Assyrian power dominated the region and Israel’s long-standing rebellion against the Lord was nearing its appointed judgment. The northern kingdom’s collapse would soon demonstrate the covenant warnings given through Moses and repeated by the prophets: persistent idolatry and refusal to heed God bring national ruin (2 Kings 17:6–23).

Hezekiah son of Ahaz became king of Judah.
Hezekiah succeeded Ahaz, whose reign had led Judah deeply into idolatry and dependence upon Assyria rather than upon the Lord (2 Kings 16:2–18). While Israel moved toward exile, God raised up in Judah a king committed to restoring faithful worship. Hezekiah removed pagan shrines, destroyed idols, reopened the temple, and called the nation back to covenant obedience (2 Kings 18:3–6; 2 Chronicles 29–31). His reign preserved Judah during an hour of regional crisis and displayed the blessing of trusting the Lord rather than earthly powers. As a faithful Davidic king who sought the good of God’s people, Hezekiah points forward to the greater Son of David, Jesus Christ, whose righteous reign brings lasting salvation.

His early actions show that faithful obedience to God was not determined by the spiritual failures of his father.

Jerusalem remained the place where the Lord had set His name and where the temple stood as the center of covenant worship.

High places were elevated shrines used for pagan worship and, at times, for unauthorized worship of the Lord. God had commanded Israel to worship at the place He chose, ultimately Jerusalem and the temple (Deuteronomy 12:2–5). Hezekiah removed these rival centers of worship so that Judah would seek the Lord according to His revealed will rather than according to inherited custom.

Sennacherib king of Assyria
Sennacherib ruled the powerful Assyrian Empire from 705 to 681 BC. He inherited an empire committed to crushing rebellion and expanding its control through intimidation, siege warfare, deportation, and tribute. Assyrian records, including Sennacherib’s Taylor Prism, confirm his campaign in Judah and specifically boast of shutting Hezekiah up in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage.” Yet the Assyrian account does not claim the capture of Jerusalem, agreeing with the biblical record that the Lord preserved the city.

With the fortified cities fallen, Jerusalem stood exposed and humanly helpless. The devastation of Judah revealed the limits of military strength and set the stage for the Lord’s deliverance of His covenant people.
