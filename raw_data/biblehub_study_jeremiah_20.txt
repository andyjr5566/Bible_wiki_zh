His sacred office increased the seriousness of his opposition to Jeremiah, for he used religious authority to resist the word God had entrusted to the prophet. The name Pashhur means “freedom” or “liberation”.

Jeremiah’s Complaint

Jeremiah 20:7

You have deceived me, O LORD, and I was deceived.
Jeremiah pours out an anguished complaint to the Lord who called him before his birth and appointed him to proclaim judgment to Judah (Jeremiah 1:5). He does not charge God with falsehood; he describes how the compelling call of God led him into a ministry whose suffering exceeded what he had anticipated. Moses similarly questioned God when Pharaoh increased Israel’s oppression, and Elijah despaired after opposition from Jezebel (Exodus 5:22–23; 1 Kings 19:4). Jeremiah’s honesty reveals that faithful servants may bring their deepest grief directly to God without abandoning faith.

The Lord’s call proved stronger than Jeremiah’s fear, reluctance, and desire for a quiet life. Though he later tried to remain silent, God’s word became like a burning fire within him that he could not contain (Jeremiah 20:9). Jeremiah’s struggle resembles Jacob’s encounter with God, in which human resistance gave way to submission under divine authority (Genesis 32:24–30). God’s purposes for His servants are not defeated by weakness, opposition, or personal distress.

His message exposed Judah’s sin, idolatry, injustice, and empty religion, making him an object of continual derision. He endured beatings, imprisonment, threats, and public shame because he spoke the word entrusted to him (Jeremiah 18:18; 20:2; 37:15).

Birth was ordinarily received as a gift from God and an occasion for rejoicing. Jeremiah’s wish reveals how deeply persecution and loneliness had pressed upon him. His anguish anticipates the suffering endured by Christ, who faithfully declared God’s truth and was rejected by many. God did not abandon Jeremiah in despair, nor did Jeremiah’s pain nullify his calling; his continued ministry testified to the sustaining power of the Lord.

Jeremiah 20:15

Jeremiah’s anguish reaches back to the announcement of his own birth. After suffering rejection, beating, and imprisonment because of the Lord’s message, he momentarily wishes that the joyful news given to his father had never been delivered. His words closely parallel Job’s lament, revealing the depth of grief that faithful servants of God can endure without abandoning the reality of God’s calling.

Jeremiah 20:16

May that man be like the cities that the LORD overthrew without compassion.
Jeremiah invokes the fate of Sodom and Gomorrah, cities destroyed for persistent wickedness and rebellion against God (Genesis 19:24–25). His words arise from the anguish of one called to proclaim judgment while suffering rejection, imprisonment, and threats. The curse reveals the depth of Jeremiah’s despair rather than a denial of God’s righteousness. The overthrow of those cities stands as a lasting warning that divine judgment against unrepentant sin is real and final (2 Peter 2:6).

May he hear an outcry in the morning and a battle cry at noon,
Jeremiah pictures unrelieved terror, as though the cursed man lived under continual siege. Morning and noon encompass the day, leaving no interval of safety or rest. Such alarms recalled invading armies, devastated cities, and the covenant curses that would fall upon Judah for refusing to heed the Lord (Deuteronomy 28:49–57). Jeremiah’s language reflects the burden of seeing the coming Babylonian destruction and the personal cost of bearing God’s message to a hardened people.

Jeremiah 20:17

because he did not kill me in the womb
Jeremiah’s suffering drove him to wish that his life had ended before birth, echoing Job’s lament over the day of his birth (Job 3:11). The prophet does not reject God’s calling or deny God’s sovereignty over life; he exposes the depth of anguish produced by relentless opposition, imprisonment, and public shame. God’s formation of Jeremiah in the womb had also marked him for prophetic service (Jeremiah 1:5), making his despair especially poignant.

Jeremiah’s anguish echoes Job’s lament over his birth (Job 3:11). After enduring ridicule, threats, and persecution for proclaiming God’s word, he questioned the purpose of a life marked by such suffering. His calling had been appointed before his birth (Jeremiah 1:5), yet obedience did not spare him from pain.
