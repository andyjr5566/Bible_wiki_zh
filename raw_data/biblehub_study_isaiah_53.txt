The Suffering Servant

Isaiah 53:1

Who has believed our message?
Isaiah laments the unbelief that would greet God’s revelation of His suffering Servant. Israel repeatedly resisted the words of the prophets, as Jeremiah and Ezekiel testified, and many would reject the Servant because His humiliation did not fit expectations of a victorious Messiah. John applied Isaiah’s words to the widespread rejection of Jesus despite His signs (John 12:37–38). Faith receives God’s saving purpose even when His wisdom overturns human ideas of power, success, and deliverance.

Rom 10:16 But not all of them welcomed the good news.…

Psa 78:22 because they did not believe God or rely on His salvation.

And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?
The arm of the LORD portrays God’s mighty power at work to save, as in the exodus from Egypt and the deliverance celebrated in the Psalms. Isaiah reveals that God’s greatest act of power would be displayed through the Servant’s suffering, death, and ultimate exaltation rather than through earthly conquest. The same Jesus whom many dismissed revealed the Father’s power in His works, His atoning sacrifice, and His resurrection. Spiritual blindness prevents people from recognizing God’s salvation when it comes in the form of the crucified Messiah.

Israel anticipated a victorious royal Messiah, yet Jesus came in humility and was met with contempt by many of His own people. Religious leaders opposed Him, crowds turned against Him, and He was rejected despite His works and teaching (John 1:11; Mark 15:13–14). The Servant willingly bore the full burden of human weakness, sickness, and the ruin brought by sin. Matthew applies these words to Jesus’ healing ministry, showing that His miracles revealed both His compassion and His authority to undo the effects of the fall (Matthew 8:16–17). His bearing of human frailty reached its fullest meaning at the cross, where He gave Himself for sinners.

The Man of Sorrows did not remain distant from suffering but carried it in obedience to the Father and love for His people.

Isaiah 53:6

Sheep readily wander from the flock and become vulnerable without a shepherd’s care. Isaiah includes every person among the straying, portraying sin as departure from God’s rule and fellowship. Psalm 119:176 likewise confesses, “I have gone astray like a lost sheep,” while Psalm 23 reveals the LORD as the Shepherd who leads His people in righteous paths.

A path may appear right to a person, yet its end is death (Prov. 14:12). No one can excuse sin as merely the fault of others or of circumstances.

On the cross, Christ bore sins in His body (1 Pet. 2:24); though sinless, He was made the sin offering for sinners so that they might be made righteous before God (2 Cor. 5:21).

The Servant endured unjust persecution, humiliation, and physical suffering at the hands of those He came to save. Jesus faced false witnesses before the Sanhedrin, mockery from soldiers, scourging under Pilate, and crucifixion outside Jerusalem (Matthew 26:67–68; John 19:1–3). His suffering was not accidental but part of God’s redemptive purpose.

Though fully aware of the cross that awaited Him, He willingly gave Himself according to the Father’s will.

Isaiah 53:9

The Servant’s execution placed Him among condemned criminals, treated as though He were guilty of the evil for which He suffered. Jesus was crucified between two criminals, and His death bore the public shame reserved for the lawless (Luke 23:32–33). Though sinless, He willingly stood in the place of sinners and endured the judgment their rebellion deserved.
