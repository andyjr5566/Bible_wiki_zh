Sennacherib Threatens Jerusalem

Isaiah 36:1

In the fourteenth year of Hezekiah’s reign,
Around 701 BC, Judah faced the greatest crisis of Hezekiah’s rule. Hezekiah had led the nation in removing idolatry and restoring faithful worship of the LORD (2 Kings 18:3–6), yet obedience to God did not spare Judah from severe trial. The Assyrian invasion became the setting in which the LORD would display both His righteous judgment upon Judah’s vulnerabilities and His power to preserve David’s royal city.

2Ki 18:13 In the fourteenth year of Hezekiah’s reign…

2Ki 19:1 On hearing this report, King Hezekiah tore his clothes…

Sennacherib king of Assyria
Sennacherib ruled the dominant empire of his day from 705 to 681 BC. After revolts arose among Assyria’s western territories, he marched into the Levant to reassert control. His own inscriptions record this campaign and boast of shutting Hezekiah “like a bird in a cage” in Jerusalem. Assyrian records confirm the campaign’s historical reality, but they do not claim Jerusalem’s capture, matching the biblical account of the LORD’s deliverance.

2Ch 32:9 Later, as Sennacherib king of Assyria and all his forces…

attacked and captured all the fortified cities of Judah.
Assyria’s army overwhelmed Judah’s defended towns, cutting off resources, trade routes, and military support for Jerusalem. The capture of Lachish, Judah’s major fortified city southwest of Jerusalem, is portrayed in reliefs from Sennacherib’s palace at Nineveh and has been confirmed by archaeological evidence of its destruction. With the countryside devastated and Jerusalem apparently isolated, Judah’s hope could not rest in walls, weapons, or alliances. The crisis prepared the way for the LORD to show that Jerusalem stood secure only by His covenant faithfulness to David and for the honor of His own name (Isaiah 37:33–35).

Assyria offered an imitation of the peace God gives, using material comfort to conceal the cost of surrender. The promise was especially deceptive because the following words reveal that deportation, not lasting security, awaited those who yielded.

A private cistern represented stability and sufficient provision in a land often dependent on stored water. The Assyrian king offered temporary earthly security while drawing the people away from the Lord, the fountain of living waters (Jeremiah 2:13). God alone could preserve Judah and give the peace that no conquering empire could provide.

Isaiah 36:17

until I come and take you away
The Assyrian commander offered surrender on Assyria’s terms: the people would be removed from Judah after Jerusalem submitted. Assyria regularly deported conquered populations and resettled foreigners in their lands, as happened to the northern kingdom of Israel after Samaria fell (2 Kings 17:6, 24). Removal from the land God had given Israel was not a minor inconvenience but a covenant judgment and the loss of homes, inheritance, and national stability.

to a land like your own—
Assyrian policy sought to weaken resistance by promising a comparable homeland. Yet no foreign land could replace the land the Lord had given to Abraham’s descendants as their inheritance. The promise also concealed the reality that deported peoples lived under Assyrian control, separated from familiar worship, family lands, and civic life.

The commander described abundance in language that echoed God’s provision for His people in the promised land (Deuteronomy 8:7–9). Grain and new wine represented harvest, security, and joy, but Assyria could not grant the covenant blessing that comes from the Lord. Material provision without freedom to serve God was not true peace.

The offer was designed to turn Judah’s eyes from the Lord’s promises to the apparent strength of Assyria. Jerusalem’s deliverance in the following chapter showed that the Lord, not Sennacherib, determined the future of His people.

Rabshakeh addressed Judah’s people within hearing of Jerusalem’s wall, seeking to turn them against their king. Assyria used threats, boasts, and promises to weaken resistance before battle. Hezekiah had led Judah away from idolatry and called the nation to trust the LORD, making him a particular target of Assyrian propaganda.

Rabshakeh understood that Judah’s resistance depended upon faith in the LORD, so he attacked that faith directly. The LORD would answer the insult against His name by preserving Jerusalem and proving that He alone rules over kings and armies.

Hamath and Arpad were prominent Syrian cities overcome by Assyria. Their gods had not preserved them from conquest, and the Assyrian envoy points to their ruins as evidence of his empire’s power.

Sepharvaim also fell under Assyrian rule. Its people were later resettled in Samaria, bringing idols and practices that included the burning of children to their gods (2 Kings 17:24, 31). Such gods could neither save their worshipers nor provide life.

The Assyrian spokesman wrongly treats Samaria’s judgment as proof that the Lord was no different from the helpless idols of conquered nations. The Lord had given Samaria over because of its sin, but He would not surrender Jerusalem to Sennacherib’s pride. Assyrian victories were interpreted as evidence that Assyria and its gods were invincible. Yet the nations’ gods were powerless idols, while Yahweh is the living Creator and ruler of every kingdom.

Rabshakeh openly placed the LORD among the defeated gods and made Assyria’s threat a direct challenge to God’s covenant faithfulness. The Lord answered through Isaiah and struck the Assyrian army, proving that no earthly empire can stand against Him. Isaiah 36:21

But the people remained silent
Hezekiah’s representatives and the people refused to be drawn into the Rabshakeh’s public challenge. Their restraint honored the king’s command and prevented the Assyrian spokesman from turning fear, argument, or resentment into open rebellion against Jerusalem’s leadership.

The Assyrian threats were designed to weaken confidence in the LORD and persuade Judah to surrender without a siege. Silence was not helplessness; it was disciplined refusal to debate God’s faithfulness with a blaspheming enemy. Isaiah 36:22

Then Hilkiah’s son Eliakim the palace administrator,
Eliakim held the chief administrative office in Hezekiah’s court, overseeing the royal household and acting with the king’s authority. His leading role shows that the Assyrian crisis had reached the highest levels of Judah’s government. Isaiah had earlier foretold that Eliakim would replace the unfaithful Shebna in this office, portraying him as a servant entrusted with responsibility for Jerusalem (Isaiah 22:20–22).

Shebna served as the royal secretary, responsible for official correspondence and state documents. Though he had once occupied the higher office later given to Eliakim, he remained among Hezekiah’s officials. His presence reflects both the seriousness of the delegation and the accuracy required in reporting Assyria’s demands.
