Ahab did not merely tolerate private idolatry; he provided an established sanctuary for sacrifices, rituals, and the ministry of Baal’s prophets. The temple gave royal support and visibility to a religion that directly opposed the worship of the living God. 1 Kings 16:33

Then he set up an Asherah pole.
Ahab added the worship of Asherah to the Baal cult he had already promoted through Jezebel. Asherah was a Canaanite goddess, and her wooden cult symbol commonly stood beside pagan altars and high places. By establishing such an object in Israel, Ahab openly violated God’s covenant commands against other gods and carved images (Exodus 20:3–4). Israel’s worship was not merely weakened by foreign influence; it was being deliberately blended with the worship of the LORD. Later, Josiah removed Asherah objects and other pagan furnishings from the temple and throughout the land as part of his reforms (2 Kings 23:4–7).

Thus Ahab did more to provoke the LORD, the God of Israel, to anger
Ahab’s idolatry was intentional rebellion against the God who had redeemed Israel and given the nation its land. His marriage to Jezebel brought Phoenician Baal worship into the royal court, and his policies led many Israelites away from covenant faithfulness. Scripture portrays such unfaithfulness as spiritual adultery, for Israel belonged to the LORD as His covenant people (Jeremiah 3:6–10). Ahab’s sins therefore affected more than his own household; they shaped the spiritual life of the entire northern kingdom and prepared the way for God’s judgment through Elijah.

than all the kings of Israel before him.
Jeroboam had led Israel into sin through the golden calves at Bethel and Dan, and later kings continued that rebellion. Ahab surpassed them by giving official support to Baal and Asherah worship and by seeking to silence the LORD’s prophets. His reign marked a severe low point in Israel’s history and set the stage for Elijah’s confrontation with the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18). Ahab’s leadership showed how rapidly a nation could descend when its ruler rejected the word of God, a pattern that ultimately contributed to the northern kingdom’s exile (2 Kings 17:7–23).
