Sin Separates Us from God

Isaiah 59:1

Surely the arm of the LORD is not too short to save,
The LORD’s power to deliver has not weakened. Israel’s history testified to His mighty arm in the exodus from Egypt, the overthrow of enemies, and the preservation of His covenant people. Isaiah later links the revealed arm of the LORD with the suffering Servant, through whom God accomplishes His greatest salvation (Isaiah 53:1). Israel’s distress did not arise from any inability in God, but from the sin that had disrupted fellowship with Him (Isaiah 59:2). The Lord remains fully able to redeem, judge evil, and fulfill every promise.

nor His ear too dull to hear.
The LORD is neither distant nor indifferent to the cries of His people. Unlike the lifeless gods of the nations, He sees righteousness, hears prayer, and acts according to His holy character (Psalm 34:15). The following words expose sin, not divine neglect, as the barrier between Israel and God. Yet even where human rebellion has brought separation, the LORD Himself provides redemption, ultimately through Jesus Christ, whose intercession brings believers before God (Hebrews 7:25).

Isaiah 59:2

But your iniquities have built barriers between you and your God,
Sin does not alter God’s power or faithfulness, but it ruptures the fellowship for which humanity was created. Adam and Eve hid from God after their disobedience, revealing sin’s enduring pattern of alienation and fear (Genesis 3:8–10). Israel’s rebellion had raised a moral barrier, not because God was distant by nature, but because His holiness cannot approve evil. The sacrifices of the law, especially the Day of Atonement, taught that reconciliation required the shedding of blood (Leviticus 16). They anticipated Christ, whose sacrifice removes sin and brings believers near to God (Hebrews 9:11–14; Ephesians 2:13).

and your sins have hidden His face from you,
God’s face represents His favor, blessing, and covenant presence. The Lord had warned Israel that persistent idolatry and disobedience would result in His hiding His face from them (Deuteronomy 31:17–18). Such withdrawal was not arbitrary abandonment but righteous judgment intended to expose sin and call His people to repentance. Yet God’s purpose in judgment was never merely to condemn; He promised restoration to those who humbled themselves and sought Him (2 Chronicles 7:14).

God hears every word, but He does not grant favorable response to prayers offered while sin is cherished and repentance is refused (Psalm 66:18; Proverbs 28:9). The obstacle lies not in God’s inability to hear but in the sinner’s unresolved rebellion. Confession and repentance restore open fellowship with God through Jesus Christ, the sinless High Priest and Mediator who grants His people confident access to the Father (Hebrews 4:14–16; 1 John 1:9).

The depth of this corruption prepares for Isaiah’s declaration that only the Lord Himself can provide the righteousness and redemption His people lack.

Israel’s sin had brought moral and spiritual darkness upon the nation. Though God had given His law, sent His prophets, and repeatedly revealed His covenant faithfulness, the people could no longer find a sure path because they had resisted His truth. The image recalls those who seek direction in darkness by clinging to a wall for support. Israel needed the guidance of the Lord as surely as the wilderness generation needed the pillar of cloud and fire (Exodus 13:21–22). Scripture repeatedly portrays refusal to hear God as blindness (Isaiah 42:18–20).

Sin had obscured their understanding and left them dependent, vulnerable, and without direction. Samson’s blindness after his fall illustrates the humiliation and weakness associated with losing sight (Judges 16:21). Israel’s condition anticipated the need for the Messiah, who came as the Light of the world and gives spiritual sight to those who believe in Him (John 9:39; 12:46).

Isaiah 59:17

He put on righteousness like a breastplate,
God enters judgment as a righteous warrior, protecting and displaying His own perfect justice. A breastplate guarded the heart and vital organs in battle, portraying righteousness as the sure defense of all that is true and holy. Israel could not produce the justice needed to overcome sin and oppression, so the Lord Himself acted. Jesus Christ perfectly embodies that righteousness and provides it to those who believe in Him. Paul draws on this imagery when he commands believers to wear “the breastplate of righteousness” (Ephesians 6:14), standing in the righteousness God gives and living faithfully before Him.

and the helmet of salvation on His head;
The helmet pictures God’s certain purpose to save His people and defeat every enemy of His kingdom. Salvation guards against hopelessness because God’s deliverance rests on His power and faithfulness rather than human strength. Isaiah’s vision includes rescue from immediate evil and the ultimate redemption accomplished through Christ. Paul applies the image to believers, calling them to take “the helmet of salvation” and to live in the hope of final deliverance (Ephesians 6:17; 1 Thessalonians 5:8).

The Covenant of the Redeemer

Isaiah 59:18

So He will repay according to their deeds:
God’s judgment is perfectly righteous, giving to every person and nation according to rebellion or obedience. No evil escapes His sight, and no act of faithfulness is forgotten. Romans 2:6 and Psalm 62:12 affirm that the Lord repays each person according to his work, revealing the consistency of His justice throughout Scripture.

Rom 2:6 God “will repay each one according to his deeds.”

fury to His enemies,
God’s fury is His holy and settled opposition to sin, violence, idolatry, and all resistance to His rule. His enemies include nations that afflict His people and all who knowingly set themselves against His covenant purposes. The Lord’s wrath is never uncontrolled or unjust; it is the response of the righteous Judge who defends truth and punishes evil. Nahum 1:2 likewise portrays Him as avenging wrongdoing and protecting those who trust in Him.

God will bring deserved judgment upon those who persist in hostility toward Him. Israel’s own unfaithful people stood under this warning alongside the foreign powers that oppressed them, for covenant privilege did not excuse covenant rebellion. The blessings and curses of Deuteronomy 28 establish that God’s people are accountable to Him, while the prophets repeatedly announce judgment on surrounding nations for pride, cruelty, and idolatry.

Isaiah 59:20

“The Redeemer will come to Zion,
The promised Redeemer is the Messiah, Jesus Christ, who delivers His people from sin, judgment, and death. Zion, centered in Jerusalem, represents the dwelling place of God among His covenant people. Isaiah’s promise points to the King foretold in Isaiah 9:6–7 and the ruler born in Bethlehem in Micah 5:2. Paul applies this prophecy to God’s future mercy toward Israel in Romans 11:26–27.

Jacob represents Israel, the people chosen through God’s covenant with the patriarchs. The Redeemer’s salvation is joined to repentance: those who receive His mercy turn from rebellion and return to the Lord. Isaiah 59:21

“As for Me, this is My covenant with them,” says the LORD.
The Lord pledges Himself to the redeemed people of Zion, whom He has delivered from transgression through the coming Redeemer (Isaiah 59:20). His covenant rests upon His faithfulness and continues His saving purpose revealed through Abraham, Israel, and David. Israel’s repeated failures do not nullify God’s purpose; He remains committed to preserving a people for His name and fulfilling His promises.

“My Spirit will not depart from you,
God promises an abiding presence rather than the temporary empowerments often seen under the old covenant. The Holy Spirit would sustain His people in faith, obedience, worship, and witness. The promise anticipates the new covenant, in which God gives His Spirit to dwell with His people permanently through the work of Christ.
