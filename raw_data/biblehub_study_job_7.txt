Job Continues: Life Seems Futile

Job 7:1

“Is not man consigned to labor on earth?
Human work belonged to God’s good design before sin entered the world, but the Fall brought painful toil, frustration, and mortality into labor (Genesis 3:17–19). Job speaks from the misery of a world under the curse, where daily existence often feels like compulsory service. His suffering intensifies that burden, yet his question recognizes a condition shared by fallen humanity. Christ receives the weary and burdened, giving rest to those who come under His yoke (Matt. 11:28–30).

Are not his days like those of a hired hand?
A hired laborer worked through difficult days while waiting for wages and release from service. Job compares human life to such temporary, exhausting employment, marked by limited control and longing for the day’s end. The image anticipates the believer’s hope that present labor and suffering will not endure forever. God promises His people an abiding rest, secured through Christ, where their labor in Him is not in vain (Hebrews 4:9–10; 1 Cor. 15:58).

Job 7:2

Like a slave he longs for shade;
Slaves and laborers in the ancient Near East often worked through the heat of the day with little relief. Job portrays his suffering as exhausting, relentless labor and longs for the shelter that shade provides. Scripture also presents the Lord as the protective shade of His people (Psalm 121:5), making Job’s distress more poignant: he feels exposed beneath affliction and waits for God’s comfort.

like a hireling he waits for his wages.
A hired worker depended upon his daily wage, which the Law required employers to pay promptly (Leviticus 19:13; Deut. 24:14–15). Job compares himself to one who has completed hard labor and waits for the day’s end and its due reward. He is not claiming that God owes him blessing, but expressing his yearning for his appointed season of pain to end and for the righteous Judge to bring relief and restoration.

Job sees his prolonged suffering as a burden assigned within God’s sovereign rule. His misery has not lasted merely for a day or a crisis; it has stretched into months, draining hope and making life seem empty of purpose. Like Joseph in prison and David during years of exile, Job endures hardship without yet seeing God’s deliverance.

Job 7:5

My flesh is clothed with worms and encrusted with dirt;
Job’s body had become a visible picture of death, decay, and humiliation. His sores drew worms, and the ash and dust in which he sat clung to his diseased skin (Job 2:8). Once honored among his people, Job now endured a condition that made him repulsive and isolated. Human strength, wealth, and reputation could not shield him from mortality’s curse or from the anguish that followed the fall. Yet Job’s physical ruin did not mean that God had abandoned him, despite the accusations of his friends.

my skin is cracked and festering.
Open sores repeatedly split, drained, and closed only to break again, leaving Job without rest by day or night. Such suffering explains his longing for death and his struggle to understand God’s purposes. The law’s instructions concerning serious skin diseases show how bodily affliction could separate a person from ordinary community life (Leviticus 13–14), intensifying Job’s loneliness. Job’s wounds also anticipate the greater suffering of Christ, who bore griefs and wounds without deserving judgment, so that sinners might receive healing and peace with God (Isaiah 53:5).

Job 7:6

Job sees his life racing toward its end like the shuttle that flies across a loom. The familiar work of weaving illustrated both the speed and the ordered course of human days. Each pass of the shuttle advances the fabric, yet the worker cannot recall the thread once it has been woven. Job’s suffering has made the brevity of life painfully immediate, echoing the Scripture’s wider witness that human life is fleeting before God (Psalm 39:5; James 4:14).

Job speaks from the depths of affliction, believing that death will end every possibility of relief, restoration, and joy in this present life. His words reveal the darkness that can settle over a suffering believer without denying the reality of God. Later, Job will confess confidence that his Redeemer lives and that he will ultimately stand before God (Job 19:25–27). Human life apart from God’s saving mercy ends in hopelessness, but the Lord gives enduring hope through His promises and, ultimately, through resurrection life in Christ.

Job appeals to God as the eternal One who knows the frailty of human life. His suffering has made the shortness and uncertainty of life painfully clear. Scripture repeatedly portrays human life as fleeting before God, yet every breath remains under His sovereign care (Psalm 39:5; James 4:14).

Job believes that his losses have ended all prospect of earthly good. Health, children, wealth, and honor had been removed, leaving him unable to imagine restoration. His lament records the depth of genuine grief, not the final outcome of his life. God later restored Job, and the larger biblical hope reaches beyond temporary relief to the day when God will remove sorrow and death forever (Job 42:10–17; Revelation 21:4).

Job foresees his removal from the company of the living. Friends, servants, and those who once knew his former prosperity will no longer look upon him, because death ends his earthly presence. His anguish reflects the brevity of life, which Scripture elsewhere compares to a vapor that appears briefly and vanishes (James 4:14).

Job addresses God, whose eyes remain upon every human life. He does not mean that death places him beyond God’s knowledge or power, but that he will no longer be found in his present place among the living. The lament anticipates the separation death brings, while the wider testimony of Scripture points beyond the grave to resurrection and life through the Redeemer (Job 19:25–27; John 11:25–26).

Job 7:9

As a cloud vanishes and is gone,
A cloud may briefly darken the sky and then dissolve under the sun and wind, leaving no trace of its former shape. Job uses this familiar sight to portray the swift passing of human life under the burden of suffering. Human strength, plans, and earthly prominence fade with a fragility that humbles every person before God.

Sheol is the realm of the dead, pictured as beneath the earth and marked by silence, separation from the activities of the living, and the end of one’s earthly labor. Job speaks from the anguish of a man who expects death to end his present life and remove him from his home, family, and visible worship of God. Death comes to all humanity and exposes the limits of earthly hope.

Death ends a person’s earthly participation in home, labor, family, and community. Job speaks from the anguish of suffering, recognizing that the dead do not resume their former earthly lives. Ecclesiastes likewise portrays death as ending one’s share in the activities of life under the sun (Ecclesiastes 9:5–6). Job’s grief does not deny God’s power over death; later he confesses confidence that his Redeemer lives and that he will ultimately see God (Job 19:25–27).

Those who belong to Christ have an enduring inheritance, and their names are written in heaven (Luke 10:20; 1 Peter 1:3–4).

Job 7:11

Job refuses silence in the face of overwhelming suffering. His lament follows the biblical pattern of bringing grief directly before God rather than withdrawing from Him. David, Jeremiah, and many psalmists likewise poured out troubled hearts before the Lord. Honest prayer does not deny faith; Job continues to address the God whom he cannot yet understand.

Job 7:13

Job looks to sleep as the ordinary refuge of a suffering man. Yet his disease, grief, and inward anguish have made even the night unable to provide relief. Human rest is a good gift from God, but it cannot finally quiet a conscience, heal a broken heart, or answer the mystery of suffering. The peace Job seeks finds its fullest answer in the Lord, who gives His people safety and rest in His presence (Psalm 4:8). Christ receives the weary and burdened, promising the rest that suffering bodies and troubled souls cannot secure for themselves (Matthew 11:28).

His experience reminds believers to bring their anxieties openly to God, who cares for them (1 Peter 5:7), while looking toward the day when He will remove every sorrow, pain, and tear (Revelation 21:4).

For soon I will lie down in the dust;
Job sees death as near and speaks in the terms of Genesis 3:19: humanity returns to the dust from which it was formed. His mortality intensifies his appeal. He longs for reconciliation with God before his life ends, knowing that earthly opportunity is brief and suffering can make death seem imminent.

Psa 22:15 My strength is dried up like a potsherd…

Gen 3:19 By the sweat of your brow you will eat your bread,…

Psa 104:29 When You hide Your face, they are terrified;…

You will seek me, but I will be no more.”
Job fears that God’s favorable attention may come too late, after death has taken him beyond the reach of earthly restoration. His words reveal the pain of one who still addresses God personally, even while feeling abandoned. Though Job cannot yet see the outcome, God does seek and vindicate him. The wider biblical hope reaches beyond Job’s restoration to the resurrection life secured by Christ, who is “the resurrection and the life” (John 11:25).
