Jeremiah 23:2

Therefore this is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says about the shepherds who tend My people:
The LORD speaks as Israel’s covenant God and rightful King. Israel’s shepherds included kings, priests, prophets, and other rulers entrusted with the spiritual and civil welfare of God’s people. Their authority was delegated, not absolute; they were to lead the nation in obedience to the Law, defend the vulnerable, and preserve true worship. Instead, many leaders in Judah used their positions for gain, affirmed rebellion, and failed to warn the people of coming judgment.

“You have scattered My flock and driven them away, and have not attended to them.
The people belonged to the LORD, not to the leaders who governed them. Corrupt rule and false prophecy had driven Judah from covenant faithfulness, leaving the nation exposed to idolatry, injustice, and the consequences of sin. The scattering anticipated the Babylonian exile, when Jerusalem would fall and many of its inhabitants would be carried away. Ezekiel likewise condemned Israel’s shepherds for feeding themselves rather than caring for the flock (Ezekiel 34:1–10). Human leaders had neglected God’s people, but the LORD had not abandoned them.

The God whom Judah’s leaders refused to honor would personally call them to account. Their failure was not merely political incompetence but evil against God and injury to His people. Judgment would fall upon those who had misused sacred responsibility, while the LORD would later gather His scattered flock and provide a righteous Shepherd-King from David’s line (Jeremiah 23:3–6).

The LORD contrasts His faithfulness with the corrupt kings and leaders who scattered and neglected His people. Though judgment had left only a remnant, God had not abandoned the covenant people He calls His flock. The promise anticipates Israel’s restoration after exile and ultimately points to the care of Christ, the Good Shepherd, who gathers His sheep and gives them eternal life (John 10:11, 27–28).

Israel’s dispersion came under God’s righteous judgment for persistent idolatry, injustice, and rejection of His word. Babylon was the principal place of exile, yet survivors were also scattered among surrounding nations (2 Kings 25; Jeremiah 40–43). The God who sent His people away because of sin retained authority over every land and would bring them home according to His covenant mercy.

Their pasture is the land God gave to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, a place intended for secure life under His rule. The return from Babylon began under Cyrus of Persia and was carried forward through leaders such as Zerubbabel, Ezra, and Nehemiah. Restoration to the land also called for renewed worship, obedience, and dependence upon the LORD. Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34 portray God as the Shepherd who supplies, protects, and leads His people.

God would restore life and increase to His people, recalling His blessing at creation and His promises to the patriarchs (Genesis 1:28; 17:6). The restored remnant would become evidence that God keeps His promises despite human unfaithfulness, while the fuller spiritual fruitfulness of God’s people is found through abiding in Christ (John 15:5).

Jeremiah does not view false prophecy as a minor error. He grieves because God’s people are being deceived, God’s holiness is being dishonored, and judgment is approaching those who refuse the truth. Jeremiah 23:33

“Now when this people or a prophet or priest asks you,
Judah’s entire religious life stood under indictment. Ordinary people, prophets, and priests alike had rejected the Lord’s truth, while many religious leaders strengthened rebellion by proclaiming peace where God had announced judgment (Jeremiah 5:31; 23:16–17).

Ezk 7:26 Disaster upon disaster will come, and rumor after rumor.…

Mal 2:7 For the lips of a priest should preserve knowledge…

‘What is the burden of the LORD?’
God’s word had become an object of careless curiosity and likely ridicule. The people treated the warnings of judgment as a repeated and unwelcome burden rather than receiving them with repentance. Their contempt revealed hearts unwilling to hear the Lord, even while demanding another message from Him. The term “burden” (Hebrew: “massa”) can mean a prophetic oracle or a heavy message. In this context, it refers to a message from God that is often one of judgment. The people’s inquiry about the “burden” suggests a desire for a word from God, yet it is likely insincere or mocking, given their rebellious state. This reflects a cultural context where prophetic messages were common, but true messages from God were rare due to the people's disobedience (Amos, meaning “burden-bearer,” 8:11-12).

Jeremiah was not to gratify their hardened demand for an oracle. The question turns their words back upon them and exposes the offense of treating God’s revelation lightly. Those who persistently reject the truth cannot presume upon continued access to comforting words from God.

God’s abandonment was not arbitrary; it followed generations of idolatry, injustice, and rejection of His word.

This phrase addresses the religious leaders of Israel, specifically prophets and priests, who were expected to guide the people in truth. In the historical context of Jeremiah, many prophets and priests were leading the people astray with false messages. The role of a prophet or priest was significant in Israelite society, as they were seen as intermediaries between God and the people. This highlights the gravity of their responsibility and the severity of their misconduct.

Judah needed God’s answer concerning sin, judgment, and the path of repentance, not assurances designed to silence conviction.
