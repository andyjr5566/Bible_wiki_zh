Ezra 6:3

In the first year of King Cyrus,
Cyrus began ruling Persia in 559 BC, but his first year over Babylon came in 538 BC after he conquered the city. In that year God moved the Persian king to permit the Jewish exiles to return. Cyrus’s action fulfilled the Lord’s word through Isaiah, who named Cyrus as the ruler who would command Jerusalem and the temple to be rebuilt (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1-4). It also marked the end of the seventy-year period of Babylonian domination foretold by Jeremiah (Jeremiah 25:11-12; 29:10).

Cyrus authorized more than a return to an ancestral homeland; he authorized the restoration of worship at the place God had chosen for His name. Nebuchadnezzar had destroyed Solomon’s temple in 586 BC, leaving the sacrificial system and much of Israel’s public worship interrupted. The decree preserved in Ezra 1:1-4 called for returning exiles to rebuild, while this record supplies details from the Persian archives concerning the construction itself.

The decree provided generous dimensions for the temple, demonstrating royal authorization and Persian financial support. A cubit measured roughly eighteen inches, making the stated height and width about ninety feet. The measurements may represent permitted construction limits rather than every final architectural detail. Whatever its outward comparison with Solomon’s temple, the rebuilt house testified that the Lord had not abandoned His covenant people and had opened the way for worship to be restored in Jerusalem.

The sixth year of Darius I was 516–515 BC, approximately seventy years after the destruction of Solomon’s temple by Babylon. Darius upheld the earlier decree of Cyrus, protected the rebuilding effort, and supplied provisions for its sacrifices (Ezra 6:1–12). The completion of the temple displayed God’s sovereign rule over Persian kings and fulfilled His promise to restore His people after judgment.

The Returned Exiles Keep the Passover

Ezra 6:19

On the fourteenth day of the first month,
Nisan 14 was the appointed day for Passover, established in Exodus 12 as the memorial of the Lord’s deliverance from Egypt. The returned community ordered its worship according to God’s revealed calendar, joining the dedication of the rebuilt temple with remembrance of redemption. Israel’s new beginning in the land rested on the same saving mercy that had formed the nation at the exodus.

Those who had returned from Babylon identified themselves with the covenant people whom God had redeemed. The sacrificed lamb also points forward to Christ, the true Passover Lamb, whose death delivers His people from sin and brings them into fellowship with God.
