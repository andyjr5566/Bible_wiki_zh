The Year of the LORD’s Favor

Isaiah 61:1

The Spirit of the Lord GOD is on Me,
Isaiah announces the coming Servant who bears God’s presence and power. The Spirit’s work marks divine selection and equips the Messiah for His saving mission. Jesus applied these words to Himself in Nazareth, declaring their fulfillment after the Spirit descended upon Him at His baptism and led Him into ministry (Luke 4:18–21; 3:22).

Luk 4:18 The Spirit of the Lord is on Me, because He has anointed Me.…

Mat 12:18 Here is My Servant, whom I have chosen…

because the LORD has anointed Me
The LORD has consecrated the Messiah for a holy office. Kings, priests, and prophets were anointed for service, and Jesus fulfills all three roles perfectly. He is the Christ, the Anointed One, sent by the Father to reveal God, offer Himself for sin, and reign over His people.

to preach good news to the poor.
God’s good news comes to those burdened by need, affliction, and helplessness. The poor include the materially oppressed and those who recognize their spiritual poverty before God. Jesus proclaimed the arrival of God’s kingdom, forgiveness for sinners, and grace for all who come to Him in faith.

He has sent Me to bind up the brokenhearted,
The Messiah brings healing to hearts wounded by sin, grief, loss, and the consequences of a fallen world. God does not despise the crushed and sorrowful; He draws near to restore them. Jesus’ ministry displayed such compassion, and His saving work provides lasting comfort and future wholeness.

Psa 147:3 He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds.

Psa 34:18 The LORD is near to the brokenhearted; He saves the contrite…

to proclaim liberty to the captives
Israel knew the pain of exile and bondage, yet the promised deliverance reaches beyond political captivity. Sin enslaves the human heart, and Satan holds people in darkness and fear. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus frees those who belong to Him from sin’s dominion and brings them into the liberty of God’s kingdom.

and freedom to the prisoners,
The Messiah opens what human strength cannot open. His salvation releases believers from guilt, condemnation, and the hopelessness of spiritual bondage. The freedom Christ gives calls His people to live under His gracious rule, awaiting the final restoration when every remnant of suffering and captivity will be removed.

Isaiah 61:2

to proclaim the year of the LORD’s favor
The announced season of grace recalls the Jubilee, when liberty was proclaimed, debts were released, and inherited land was restored (Leviticus 25:10). Isaiah’s promise reaches beyond social restoration to God’s deliverance of His spiritually impoverished people. Jesus applied these words to His own ministry in Nazareth, declaring their fulfillment in His coming (Luke 4:18–19). In Him, sinners receive forgiveness, captives are freed from bondage to sin, and the restored people of God enter the blessings of salvation.

Luk 4:19 to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favor.”

Lev 25:10 So you are to consecrate the fiftieth year…

Psa 102:13 You will rise up and have compassion on Zion,…

and the day of our God’s vengeance,
God’s favor does not lessen His holiness or His commitment to judge evil. The day of the LORD brings His righteous judgment upon nations and individuals who persist in rebellion (Isaiah 13:6; Joel 2:31). For Zion’s oppressors, God’s vengeance meant that He would answer the violence, pride, and injustice inflicted upon His people. The Messiah’s work includes both salvation for those who receive Him and final judgment for those who reject His rule.

Isa 34:8 For the LORD has a day of vengeance, a year of recompense…

2Th 1:8 in blazing fire, inflicting vengeance…

to comfort all who mourn,
God promises consolation to those grieving over sin, exile, loss, and the desolation of Jerusalem. The comfort foretold includes the restoration of Zion and the reversal of her shame, themes announced earlier in Isaiah (Isaiah 40:1–2). Christ also brings this comfort to all who mourn before God, giving hope that present sorrow will yield to His final restoration (Matthew 5:4; Revelation 21:4).

Mat 5:4 Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.

Isaiah 61:3

to console the mourners in Zion—
Zion, Jerusalem and the covenant people centered there, had known devastation, exile, and the grief caused by sin and judgment. The Servant announced comfort not by denying their sorrow but by bringing God’s promised restoration. Jesus applied Isaiah 61 to His own ministry in Nazareth, declaring that the promised good news and deliverance had arrived in Him (Luke 4:16–21).

Isa 51:3 For the LORD will comfort Zion and will look with compassion…

Mat 5:4 Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.

to give them a crown of beauty for ashes,
Ashes marked mourning, humiliation, and repentance throughout the Old Testament. The Lord would reverse Zion’s disgrace, replacing the signs of grief with honor, dignity, and renewed joy. His restoration would not merely return what had been lost; it would display His grace in a transformed people.

the oil of joy for mourning,
Anointing oil was associated with celebration, welcome, consecration, and God’s blessing. God promised to lift the burden of mourning and give His people cause for deep, enduring gladness. The joy granted by the Lord reaches beyond changed circumstances, flowing from His saving presence and faithful covenant love.

and a garment of praise for a spirit of despair.
Garments visibly identified a person’s condition, and mourners commonly wore clothing associated with sorrow. God would clothe those crushed by grief with praise, turning hearts once weighed down by despair toward thankful worship. Isaiah later describes Zion as clothed with salvation and righteousness by the Lord Himself (Isaiah 61:10).

Those restored by God would become like strong, established trees rather than fragile people overwhelmed by calamity. Their righteous lives would bear witness to the Lord’s work in them, recalling the blessed man planted by streams of water in Psalm 1:3. Strength and fruitfulness would arise from dependence on God, not from human achievement.

the planting of the LORD,
The Lord Himself would establish, preserve, and cultivate His people. Their renewal would be the result of His sovereign grace, as a gardener intentionally plants a tree for growth and fruit. Believers likewise belong to God’s field, formed and sustained for His purposes (1 Corinthians 3:9).

that He may be glorified.
The comfort, restoration, and righteousness of God’s people reveal His mercy, power, and faithfulness. Zion’s renewal would direct attention to the One who saves and restores. Every work of redemption ultimately serves the glory of God, from whom, through whom, and to whom are all things (Romans 11:36).

Isaiah 61:4

They will rebuild the ancient ruins;
Those redeemed by the Lord will participate in restoring what Babylon destroyed. Jerusalem and the towns of Judah lay in ruins after the exile, yet God would bring His people home and enable them to rebuild. Zerubbabel, Ezra, and Nehemiah became instruments of this restoration, rebuilding the temple, renewing worship, and securing Jerusalem’s walls.

The return from exile would reverse years of judgment and abandonment. Restoration included more than buildings: God called His people back to covenant faithfulness, justice, worship, and ordered community life. What human sin had devastated, God’s mercy would heal.

Cities once marked by God’s judgment would again become inhabited places of peace, work, and worship. The rebuilding of Judah testified that the Lord had not abandoned His covenant promises. Christ’s redeeming work likewise creates a people renewed by grace and built together as God’s spiritual house.

The damage extended across generations, but God’s promise reached deeper than immediate recovery. He would address the consequences of rebellion and preserve His people for His redemptive purposes. The restoration begun after exile points forward to the Messiah’s kingdom and the final renewal of all things.

Isaiah 61:5

The nations that once oppressed Israel will acknowledge the Lord’s rule and contribute to Zion’s restoration. After exile and humiliation under foreign powers, God promises a future in which His people enjoy security, provision, and honor. Shepherding portrays steady care for the resources God has entrusted to His people.

Plowing and tending vineyards represent the ordinary labor required for abundance in the land. The promise anticipates a restored Israel freed to serve the Lord as “priests of the LORD” (Isaiah 61:6), while the nations share in the blessing promised through Abraham’s offspring (Genesis 12:3). Its fullest realization belongs to Messiah’s righteous reign, when hostility is replaced by submission to God and the nations come to Zion in peace.

Isaiah 61:6

But you will be called the priests of the LORD;
Israel’s restored people will bear a sacred identity among the nations, serving the Lord in a manner that reflects His holiness and reveals His truth. Under the Mosaic covenant, priestly service belonged to Aaron’s descendants within Levi, yet the nation had already been called to be “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (Exodus 19:6). Isaiah looks forward to the day when Israel’s calling before the world will be openly recognized. The promise also anticipates the spiritual privilege shared by believers in Christ, who are called “a royal priesthood” and are to proclaim God’s excellencies (1 Peter 2:9).

The nations will acknowledge that Israel belongs to the Lord and serves Him. Those once despised, scattered, and oppressed will be known for worship, obedience, and ministry before God. The restored people will not exist merely for national prosperity but for the honor of the God who redeemed them. Their service will display the truth that the Lord’s covenant purposes for Israel have not failed.

Nations that once plundered Israel will bring their resources into the sphere of Israel’s blessing and worship. The provision reflects the Lord’s faithfulness to His covenant promises and points toward the righteous order of the Messiah’s kingdom, when the nations will honor the Lord and His chosen people.

The riches of the nations will become evidence of God’s faithfulness, as earthly glory is directed toward His purposes. Isaiah’s vision reaches beyond return from exile to the coming kingdom, when the nations bring their honor before the Lord and His glory fills all things.

Isaiah 61:7

Instead of shame, My people will have a double portion,
Israel’s exile, devastated cities, and public disgrace would not be the final outcome of covenant discipline. The double portion recalls the inheritance of the firstborn son, who received a privileged share of the family estate (Deuteronomy 21:17). God promises not merely survival after judgment but abundant restoration, honor, and renewed favor.

Captivity had brought mockery from the nations and grief over the loss of Jerusalem and the land. God will reverse that humiliation by restoring His people to the inheritance He gave them under the Abrahamic covenant. Their joy will arise from recognizing that the Lord has remained faithful even after judging their sin.

God’s covenant grants to Abraham’s descendants were not canceled by exile, and the return to the land points beyond the initial return from Babylon to the fuller blessing of Messiah’s kingdom. The language of a double portion portrays security, fruitfulness, and honor under God’s righteous rule.

Isaiah 61:8

For I, the LORD, love justice;
The covenant God delights in what is right, impartial, and faithful to His holy standards. Israel’s restoration was never intended to separate worship from righteous treatment of neighbors, especially the poor, the powerless, and the oppressed. The Lord’s character establishes the pattern for His people, as Micah 6:8 declares: they are to do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly with God.

The Lord rejects gain acquired through oppression, dishonesty, or abuse of power. He will not accept religious service offered by hands that exploit others, a sin repeatedly condemned among Israel’s leaders and wealthy citizens. Outward sacrifices and public devotion cannot conceal injustice before the God who searches the heart.

Isaiah 61:9

Israel’s restoration will be visible beyond its own borders. Though the nation endured exile, oppression, and reproach, the Lord promises that its children will again possess an honored place among the peoples of the earth. The promise recalls God’s covenant with Abraham, whose descendants were to become a great nation and a channel of blessing to all families of the earth (Genesis 12:2–3). Israel’s future prominence will testify that the Lord has remained faithful to His covenant.
