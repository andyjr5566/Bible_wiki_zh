For the choirmaster.
The psalm was appointed for Israel’s gathered worship under the direction of the temple musicians. National distress, military loss, and repentance were brought before the Lord not merely in private grief but in the worshiping assembly.

Psalm 60:2

You have shaken the land and torn it open.
Israel’s military humiliation had exposed the nation’s weakness before surrounding enemies. David understood that such distress was not outside God’s rule; the Lord had permitted the nation to be shaken as discipline and as a summons to renewed dependence on Him. The superscription connects the psalm with conflicts against Aram and Edom, when Israel faced severe pressure before God granted victory in the Valley of Salt.

National healing required more than military strength: Israel needed God’s favor, unity, and renewed faithfulness. The plea anticipates the Lord’s promise to heal His people when they humble themselves, seek Him, and turn from sin.

David did not blame circumstances or enemies alone; he recognized God’s sovereign hand in the nation’s distress. The psalm arises amid wars against Aram and Edom, when Israel’s security seemed shaken despite God’s promises to David.

Christ ultimately drank the cup of judgment for His people, bearing the wrath sinners deserved so that those who trust Him may receive mercy and restoration.

David appeals to the Lord for immediate intervention against the enemies threatening Israel. God’s right hand represents His sovereign power, displayed in the Exodus when He shattered Pharaoh’s forces and throughout Israel’s history when He gave His people victory. Human strength and military resources could not secure the nation; deliverance depended on the God who rules over every enemy and every battlefield.

Psalm 60:6

God’s word proceeds from His holy dwelling with unquestionable authority. The sanctuary testified that the Lord had chosen to dwell among Israel and to reveal His will to His covenant people (Exodus 25:8–9). David receives assurance that Israel’s security rests not in military strength but in the decree of the holy God.

I will parcel out Shechem
Shechem stood in the central hill country of Canaan, where the Lord first promised the land to Abraham (Genesis 12:6–7). Joshua later gathered Israel there to renew the covenant (Joshua 24:1–25). God’s authority to distribute Shechem declares that the land belongs to Him and that His promises to Abraham’s descendants remain secure.

Succoth lay east of the Jordan near the Jabbok River, where Jacob settled after meeting Esau (Genesis 33:17). By naming both Shechem west of the Jordan and Succoth east of it, the Lord claims dominion over the whole inheritance of Israel. No region, boundary, or enemy can prevent God from accomplishing His covenant purposes for His people.

Psalm 60:10

Have You not rejected us, O God?
Israel’s military distress appeared to contradict God’s covenant promises and past deliverance. David brought the nation’s sense of defeat directly before the Lord, recognizing that no enemy could prevail unless God permitted it. The question reflects the painful discipline that often followed Israel’s sin, yet it is not unbelief; lament appeals to the God whose faithfulness remains the only hope. Christ entered the deepest measure of abandonment at the cross, bearing the judgment sinners deserved so that all who trust Him would never be finally forsaken.

Will You no longer march out, O God, with our armies?
Israel’s victories depended upon the Lord’s presence rather than numbers, weapons, or military skill. During David’s conflict with Edom, associated with the Valley of Salt, the nation needed God to lead them as He had led Israel in earlier battles. The Lord is not a tribal deity enlisted for human ambition, but the sovereign King who grants victory according to His purposes. His people must seek His direction, confess their dependence, and trust Him when circumstances suggest that He has withdrawn.

Psalm 60:12

With God we will perform with valor,
Israel’s hope rested not in military strength but in the presence and help of the covenant God. In the setting reflected by the psalm’s title, David faced conflict with Aramean forces and Edom, yet he recognized that even a capable army could not secure victory apart from the Lord. God’s earlier rejection and discipline had brought national distress, but His promises to possess the land and preserve His people remained sure. Courage grows from trusting God’s power and faithfulness, as Joshua was commanded to be strong because the Lord was with him. Believers likewise serve and endure through the strength Christ supplies.

David’s confidence anticipated the victories God would grant over Edom and other hostile nations. The imagery also points beyond Israel’s immediate conflicts to the final defeat of sin, death, and Satan through Christ, who crushed the serpent’s head and will place every enemy beneath His feet.
