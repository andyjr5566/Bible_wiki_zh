Proverbs 26:2

Like a fluttering sparrow or darting swallow,
Sparrows and swallows move quickly through the air without settling for long. Their restless flight pictures a spoken curse that has no rightful place to land. Human malice may be active and persistent, but it cannot establish a lasting claim against one whom God declares innocent.

an undeserved curse
False accusations, hostile wishes, and malicious words were serious matters in the ancient world, yet Israel knew that God alone governs blessing and judgment. Balaam could not curse Israel because the Lord had blessed them (Numbers 23:8; Deuteronomy 23:5). The wicked may speak against the righteous, but their words cannot overturn God’s purpose or nullify His protection.

The proverb does not deny that slander and hatred can bring temporary pain; it assures believers that unjust condemnation will not prevail before the righteous Judge. Jesus Christ bore the true curse deserved by sinners on the cross (Galatians 3:13), securing final freedom from condemnation for all who belong to Him.

Proverbs 26:3

Donkeys carried burdens faithfully, yet they required restraint and guidance along the road. Submission to God’s leading keeps stubbornness from turning into ruin.

Proverbs 26:4

Do not answer a fool according to his folly,
A fool in Proverbs is morally resistant to the fear of the Lord and unwilling to receive correction (Proverbs 1:7; 9:7–8). Entering such a person’s argument on his own dishonest, contemptuous, or irrational terms only strengthens folly rather than serving truth. Wisdom recognizes when silence, restraint, or a brief answer is more faithful than prolonged dispute. Jesus displayed such discernment when He refused to be drawn into the corrupt accusations and manipulative questions of His opponents (Matthew 27:12–14).

Pro 23:9 Do not speak to a fool, for he will despise the wisdom…

2Ti 2:23-24 But reject foolish and ignorant speculation,…

Tit 3:9 But avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, arguments,…

or you yourself will be like him.
Foolish speech readily produces anger, pride, mockery, and reckless words in those who answer it. A wise person must not surrender self-control or integrity merely to win an argument. Proverbs 26:5 balances this instruction by showing that there are times when folly must be answered plainly, lest the fool remain convinced of his own wisdom. The difference lies in refusing to adopt the fool’s manner while, when needed, giving an answer governed by truth, grace, and the fear of the Lord.

Pro 13:20 He who walks with the wise will become wise,…

1Co 15:33 Do not be deceived: “Bad company corrupts good character.”

Proverbs 26:5

Answer a fool according to his folly,
Wisdom sometimes requires exposing foolish reasoning rather than allowing it to pass unchallenged. Proverbs 26:4 forbids joining a fool in his irrational or sinful manner; Proverbs 26:5 commands an answer suited to reveal the folly of his claims. The wise person does not imitate the fool, but responds truthfully and appropriately so that error is uncovered. Jesus answered the Pharisees’ traps with questions and Scripture, revealing both their hypocrisy and their ignorance of God’s purposes (Matthew 22:15–46).

2Ti 2:23-25 But reject foolish and ignorant speculation,…

lest he become wise in his own eyes.
Silence can be mistaken for agreement and may strengthen a proud person in self-deception. Scripture repeatedly condemns those who regard themselves as wise while refusing the fear of the Lord (Isaiah 5:21; Proverbs 3:7). A discerning correction may restrain folly, protect others from deception, and call the sinner toward humility. Such an answer must be governed by truth, self-control, and love, recognizing that genuine wisdom is found in Christ, “in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Colossians 2:3).

Proverbs 26:6

Dependence on a fool produces damage as severe as self-mutilation. Feet enabled a person to travel, labor, conduct business, and fulfill responsibilities; losing them meant helplessness and loss of usefulness. A person who entrusts an important matter to someone without wisdom cripples his own purposes. Proverbs similarly compares reliance on an unreliable person to a broken tooth or a slipping foot (Proverbs 25:19).

Sending a fool invites the consequences of folly into one’s own life. Drinking ordinarily supplied refreshment and strength, yet violence taken as a drink brings injury inward rather than nourishment. The one who makes such a choice bears responsibility for exposing himself and others to avoidable harm. Fellowship with fools leads toward ruin, while walking with the wise promotes wisdom and safety (Proverbs 13:20).

In the ancient world, a messenger often carried vital instructions, legal agreements, military reports, or personal communications across significant distances. The sender’s intentions depended upon the messenger’s faithfulness, accuracy, and judgment. A fool may distort the message, neglect it, reveal what should remain confidential, or act carelessly in ways that defeat the sender’s purpose. God’s servants are therefore called to be trustworthy in every responsibility, recognizing that faithful stewardship reflects the character of the Lord who gives wisdom to those who seek Him (James 1:5).

Proverbs 26:7

Like lame legs hanging limp
Lame legs cannot bear weight or carry a person toward a worthy destination. The image portrays helplessness and uselessness, not because the legs were made without purpose, but because they cannot perform their intended function. Scripture often presents the healing of the lame as evidence of God’s restoring power, anticipating the day when “the lame man shall leap like a deer” (Isaiah 35:6).

is a proverb in the mouth of a fool.
A fool may repeat wise words, yet his rebellion against God prevents those words from guiding his life or helping others. Biblical folly is a moral refusal of instruction rather than a mere lack of intelligence. Wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord (Proverbs 1:7), and a proverb bears fruit only in a heart willing to obey it. Empty recitation cannot substitute for faithful practice; God calls His people to be doers of His word, not hearers only (James 1:22).

Proverbs 26:8

Like binding a stone into a sling
A sling was effective only when its stone could be released at the proper moment. Securing the stone so that it could not fly would frustrate the weapon’s purpose and could endanger the one using it. David’s victory over Goliath demonstrates the opposite: he used his sling with skill, faith, and decisive purpose (1 Samuel 17:49–50).

Honor grants influence, credibility, and often authority. A fool, marked by rebellion against wisdom and disregard for the fear of the Lord, is likely to misuse such trust for further harm. Elevating an ungodly person can mislead others, weaken righteous judgment, and reward conduct God condemns. As snow in summer and rain at harvest disrupt their proper seasons, honor bestowed on a fool brings disorder where wisdom and integrity should govern (Proverbs 26:1).

Proverbs 26:9

Like a thorn that goes into the hand of a drunkard
A drunkard may seize a thorn without recognizing the danger or responding wisely to the pain. His impaired judgment turns what should prompt caution into further injury. Scripture repeatedly associates drunkenness with deception, loss of restraint, and ruined discernment (Proverbs 20:1; 23:29–35; Ephesians 5:18). The thorn pictures trouble handled recklessly by one unable to govern himself.

is a proverb in the mouth of a fool.
A fool may repeat words of wisdom while remaining untouched by their truth. Like the drunkard who mishandles the thorn, he possesses something that should lead to correction but uses it without understanding, humility, or obedience. Biblical wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord, not with the ability to recite wise sayings (Proverbs 1:7). Words of truth become spiritually fruitful only when they are received and practiced (James 1:22).

Careless judgment harms people beyond the one immediately involved. In warfare, an unskilled or reckless archer endangered allies as well as enemies, turning a weapon meant for defense into a source of disorder. Wisdom directs power, speech, and responsibility toward righteous ends; folly releases consequences without regard for whom they strike.

God calls His people to exercise discernment in leadership, counsel, and responsibility, seeking those whose faithfulness and wisdom have been tested.

Proverbs 26:11

As a dog returns to its vomit,
Dogs in the ancient world commonly roamed as scavengers and were associated with uncleanness and degradation. The revolting image portrays the irrationality of returning voluntarily to what has already brought sickness and shame. Peter applies the proverb to those who have known the way of righteousness yet turn back to corruption, revealing hearts unchanged by the truth (2 Pet. 2:20–22).

2Pe 2:22 Of them the proverbs are true: “A dog returns to its vomit,”…

A fool is not merely uninformed but morally resistant to God’s wisdom. Though consequences expose the emptiness of sinful choices, the fool returns to the same rebellion rather than receiving correction. Wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord (Prov. 1:7), and repentance breaks the destructive cycle by turning the heart from sin to God.

Proverbs 26:12

Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes?
Wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord, not with confidence in personal judgment. A person convinced of his own insight resists correction, dismisses counsel, and becomes unable to recognize sin or error. Ancient Israel esteemed humility before God because human understanding is limited and the Lord alone sees rightly. Isaiah likewise pronounced woe on those who were wise in their own eyes (Isaiah 5:21). Such pride stands opposed to the teachable spirit commended throughout Proverbs.

Isa 5:21 Woe to those who are wise in their own eyes…

A fool may yet be brought to repentance when confronted with his need, but self-satisfied pride shuts the door against instruction. The fool who hears correction can gain wisdom; the person who trusts his own wisdom sees no need to listen. God gives grace to the humble and opposes the proud (James 4:6). Hope begins when a person abandons self-reliance, receives godly counsel, and seeks the wisdom that comes from the Lord.

The slacker is marked by habitual avoidance of duty rather than by occasional weariness or limitation. Proverbs repeatedly contrasts such a person with the diligent, whose faithful labor reflects wisdom, responsibility, and fear of the Lord (Proverbs 6:6–11; 10:4). A door moves repeatedly yet remains fixed in the same place. Ancient household doors commonly turned on pivots set into sockets in the threshold and lintel, allowing motion without progress. The picture exposes activity that accomplishes nothing and contrasts sharply with the wisdom literature’s call to diligence, responsibility, and purposeful labor.

Proverbs 26:15

The slacker buries his hand in the dish;
Shared meals commonly involved reaching into a common bowl or dish. The proverb portrays laziness at its most irrational: food is within reach, yet the slacker remains inactive. Proverbs repeatedly contrasts such self-neglect with the diligent labor that ordinarily leads to provision, wisdom, and usefulness (Proverbs 10:4; 6:6–11). The same picture appears in Proverbs 19:24, emphasizing the seriousness of a settled habit of indolence.

it wearies him to bring it back to his mouth.
The deliberate exaggeration exposes the enslaving power of laziness. A person who refuses even the smallest necessary effort will suffer want and squander opportunities placed before him (Proverbs 24:30–34). God supplies spiritual nourishment through His Word, prayer, fellowship, and the work of Christ, but these gifts are not to be met with apathy. Genuine faith bears fruit in obedient action, for faith without works is dead (James 2:17). Christ completed the work given Him by the Father with steadfast devotion, providing the perfect pattern of willing obedience (John 4:34; 19:30).

Proverbs 26:16

The slacker is wiser in his own eyes
Laziness often joins itself to self-deception. Rather than receiving correction or facing the consequences of neglected responsibility, the sluggard persuades himself that his ways are reasonable and superior. Proverbs repeatedly warns that such indolence leads to poverty, ruin, and shame (Proverbs 6:6–11; 24:30–34). Pride closes the heart to instruction and prepares the way for downfall (Proverbs 16:18). True wisdom begins with humility before the Lord and a willingness to seek His counsel (Proverbs 9:10; James 1:5).

Proverbs 26:17

Grabbing the ears of a dog invites an immediate and dangerous response. Dogs in ancient Israel often roamed as scavengers or guarded property, and such an act would provoke an animal that could not be safely controlled. The image portrays needless self-endangerment: the one who seizes the dog creates a crisis from which he may not easily withdraw. Proverbs repeatedly warns against folly that brings trouble upon oneself.

A passerby has no rightful place or responsibility in the dispute. Lacking the history, facts, and relationships known to those involved, an outsider is poorly equipped to judge the matter or bring peace. Wisdom recognizes the limits of one’s knowledge and authority.

A madman in battle acts without restraint, judgment, or concern for the ruin he causes. Scripture portrays such loss of soundness as dreadful, whether arising from judgment, oppression, or moral folly. Firebrands were burning missiles capable of spreading flames through homes, fields, defenses, and supplies. Their use turned a single attack into widespread destruction. Solomon compares careless and deceitful behavior to a weapon that unleashes consequences beyond the speaker’s control. Words spoken to deceive, ridicule, or provoke may ignite anger, distrust, and lasting division.

God calls His people to speak truthfully and to exercise self-control, recognizing that speech can wound as surely as weapons in the hands of a violent man.

Proverbs 26:20

Without wood, a fire goes out;
Fire required a continual supply of fuel in every Israelite home, whether for cooking, warmth, light, or protection. Its disappearance when wood was withheld provided a clear picture of strife: contention does not usually sustain itself without being fed. Angry responses, repeated accusations, pride, and the eager circulation of offenses supply the fuel that keeps disputes alive. Wisdom calls God’s people to remove such fuel through restraint, humility, forgiveness, and truthful reconciliation. James likewise warns that the tongue, though small, can kindle great destruction (James 3:5–6).

Gossip spreads private matters, suspected motives, and damaging reports among people who are not able or responsible to resolve the matter. Such speech fractures trust, hardens resentment, and turns a personal offense into a wider division. Proverbs repeatedly identifies a whisperer as one who separates close friends (Proverbs 16:28; 18:8). Peace often begins when believers refuse to repeat harmful reports and instead follow Christ’s command to address sin directly and privately when possible (Matthew 18:15). The Prince of Peace calls His people to speak truth in love, pursue reconciliation, and preserve the unity of those whom He has joined together.

Proverbs 26:22

The words of a gossip
Gossip spreads private or damaging information in ways that divide people and erode trust. Israel’s covenant life depended on truthfulness, justice, and neighborly faithfulness; therefore Leviticus 19:16 forbids going about as a slanderer among the people. A gossip may present words as concern, insight, or harmless news, yet such speech often wounds reputations and stirs conflict. Proverbs repeatedly identifies the gossip as one who separates close friends (Proverbs 16:28).

Gossip appeals to fallen desires for secret knowledge, self-importance, and judgment of others. Like a delicacy eagerly consumed, it can be received with pleasure even when its hearer knows it is wrong. The temptation resembles Eve’s attraction to the forbidden fruit, which appeared desirable before its destructive consequences became evident (Genesis 3:6). Proverbs 26:27

He who digs a pit will fall into it,
Pits served as traps for animals and enemies, making the image a fitting picture of concealed malice. God often permits the consequences of wicked plans to return upon those who devise them. Psalm 7:15–16 describes the violent man falling into the hole he made, and Haman’s execution on the gallows prepared for Mordecai provides a historical example of such judgment (Esther 7:9–10). Evil may appear carefully planned, but no scheme escapes the righteous rule of God.

Proverbs 26:28

A lying tongue hates those it crushes,
Deceit is not a harmless fault but an assault on the people it injures. False witness violated God’s law in Israel because lies could destroy a person’s reputation, property, freedom, or life (Exodus 20:16; Deuteronomy 19:15–21). The liar may seek advantage, conceal guilt, or escape consequences, yet such speech treats its victim with hatred. Satan, identified as the father of lies, uses deception to oppose God’s truth and bring death (John 8:44). God delights in truthful speech because truth preserves justice, trust, and peace among His people (Proverbs 12:22).

Pro 10:18 The one who conceals hatred has lying lips…

Psa 5:6 You destroy those who tell lies; the LORD abhors…

and a flattering mouth causes ruin.
Insincere praise disguises manipulation as kindness. Flatterers often tell people what they want to hear in order to gain influence, secure favor, or lead them into error. Such words can foster pride, weaken discernment, and draw people away from wise counsel. Proverbs warns that flattery spreads a net for its victim (Proverbs 29:5), while the apostles cautioned that smooth speech can deceive the unsuspecting (Romans 16:18). The tongue possesses power to wound openly through lies or quietly through praise detached from truth; therefore, believers must speak with honesty, love, and reverence for God.
