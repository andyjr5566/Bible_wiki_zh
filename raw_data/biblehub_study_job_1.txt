Job’s Character and Wealth

Job 1:1

There was a man in the land of Uz
Uz lay east or southeast of Israel, likely in or near Edom, the territory associated with Esau’s descendants (Lamentations 4:21). Job’s setting outside the covenant nation shows that the true God was known and worshiped beyond Israel. The suffering, righteousness, wisdom, and divine justice revealed in Job speak to all humanity.

whose name was Job.
Job, whose name may mean persecuted or hated, appears without a genealogy or connection to Israel’s patriarchs, allowing his account to stand as the record of a real man whose faith was tested under extraordinary suffering. Ezekiel names Job with Noah and Daniel as an example of righteousness (Ezekiel 14:14, 20), and James points to Job’s endurance and the Lord’s compassion (James 5:11).

And this man was blameless and upright,
Job lived with genuine integrity before God and people. His blamelessness did not mean that he had never sinned, for no fallen person is sinless, but that his life was marked by wholehearted devotion and moral faithfulness. The trials that follow did not arise from hidden hypocrisy or a pattern of rebellion.

fearing God and shunning evil.
Job honored God’s holiness, submitted to His authority, and deliberately turned away from what God condemns. Reverence for God produced a practical hatred of evil, as wisdom teaches (Proverbs 8:13). Job’s character establishes the central tension of the book: a righteous servant may suffer deeply without suffering as punishment for personal wrongdoing.

Job 1:2

He had seven sons and three daughters,
Job’s large household testified to God’s abundant blessing. In the ancient world, children were a source of joy, strength, inheritance, and security, while seven sons especially marked a man as prosperous and honored. The seven sons and three daughters form a total of ten children, presenting Job’s family as full and flourishing. Their later deaths would therefore represent not merely financial loss but the devastation of Job’s entire earthly legacy. After Job’s testing, God restored to him seven sons and three daughters (Job 42:13), showing that the Lord had not abandoned His faithful servant and that His power to restore exceeded the destruction brought through suffering.

when suddenly a mighty wind swept in from the desert
A violent wind from the wilderness struck without warning, bringing devastation through a force of creation that no human power could restrain. The desert, often associated with danger, testing, and desolation, became the source of Job’s final and most grievous loss. Though Satan was the immediate agent permitted to afflict Job, the event remained under God’s sovereign limit (Job 1:12). Job could not yet see the heavenly conflict behind his suffering.
