Do Not Boast about Tomorrow

Proverbs 27:1

Do not boast about tomorrow,
Tomorrow belongs to God’s providence, not human control. Wise planning is commended, but confidence that assumes continued life, health, and opportunity reveals pride. James warns against those who make confident plans while failing to say, “If the Lord wills” (James 4:13–16). Jesus likewise exposed the folly of the rich fool, whose plans for many years ended on the night God required his life (Luke 12:16–21).

Jas 4:13-16 Come now, you who say, “Today or tomorrow…

Luk 12:16-21 Then He told them a parable: “The ground of a certain rich man…

Pro 16:9 A man’s heart plans his course, but the LORD determines…

for you do not know
Human knowledge is limited, while God alone knows the end from the beginning (Isaiah 46:9–10). The uncertainty of the future calls God’s people to humility, prayer, and daily dependence upon Him. Jesus taught His disciples to seek the Father for daily bread, training them to trust God’s present care rather than presume upon tomorrow (Matthew 6:11).

Ecc 8:7 Since no one knows what will happen, who can tell him…

Ecc 11:5 As you do not know the path of the wind,…

Psa 39:4-5 Show me, O LORD, my end and the measure of my days.…

what a day may bring.
A day may bring blessing, loss, danger, or an unforeseen opportunity to serve the Lord. Ancient Israel knew the vulnerability of life through drought, war, illness, and crop failure, yet the same uncertainty remains in every generation. Believers are therefore called to live faithfully in the present, storing treasure in heaven and remaining ready for Christ’s return (Matthew 6:19–21; 25:1–13).

Proverbs 27:2

Let another praise you,
Honor that has weight is recognized by others rather than announced by oneself. A godly life produces fruit that can be seen in faithfulness, wisdom, service, and integrity. The Lord exalts the humble in His time, while human self-exaltation invites correction and shame (James 4:10; Matthew 23:12).

Pro 31:28 Her children rise up and call her blessed;…

3Jn 1:12 Demetrius has received a good testimony from everyone…

and not your own mouth—
Self-praise reveals a heart tempted to seek glory apart from God. Scripture repeatedly warns that pride precedes destruction because it blinds a person to dependence upon the Lord (Proverbs 16:18). Believers are called to speak truthfully about God’s work without making themselves the object of admiration.

Pro 25:27 It is not good to eat too much honey or to search…

Rom 12:3 For by the grace given me I say to every one of you:…

a stranger,
Commendation from someone outside one’s household or close circle carries particular credibility because it is not merely the loyalty of family or friends. In Israel’s honor-conscious society, a reputation was established publicly through observable character. Such recognition points beyond personal ability to the grace of God at work in a person.

1Ti 3:7 Furthermore, he must have a good reputation with outsiders,…

2Co 8:18 Along with Titus we are sending the brother…

and not your own lips.
Restraint in speech guards the heart from vanity and preserves a testimony of humility. Jesus did not seek His own glory but honored the Father who sent Him (John 8:50). Faithful servants may receive praise from others, yet they return all honor to God, from whom every good gift and every lasting commendation comes.

2Co 10:17 Rather, “Let him who boasts boast in the Lord.”

Proverbs 27:3

A stone is heavy
Stones served as building materials, boundary markers, memorials, and instruments of judgment in biblical life. Their weight made them a familiar picture of a burden that cannot be easily moved or carried. Joshua’s memorial stones taken from the Jordan reminded later generations of the Lord’s faithfulness in bringing Israel into the land (Joshua 4:20–24).

Exo 18:18 Surely you and these people will wear yourselves out…

Num 11:14 I cannot carry all these people by myself;…

Psa 38:4 For my iniquities have overwhelmed me;…

and sand is a burden,
Sand seems light when held in the hand, yet its vast accumulation becomes crushingly heavy. Job compared the magnitude of his grief to the sand of the seas (Job 6:3). The image includes both sudden, obvious burdens and countless smaller pressures that gather until they overwhelm a person.

but aggravation from a fool outweighs them both.
A fool rejects the fear of the Lord and resists correction, bringing disorder, conflict, and grief to those nearby. Such a person can impose a burden greater than physical weight because folly wounds relationships and often leads others into sin or needless trouble. Scripture warns that encountering a fool in destructive folly is more dangerous than meeting a bear deprived of her cubs (Proverbs 17:12). Wisdom, received in humility and fulfilled in Christ, brings the stability and peace that folly continually disrupts.

Proverbs 27:4

Wrath is cruel
Human wrath readily becomes harsh, vindictive, and destructive. Cain’s anger toward Abel ripened into murder when he refused God’s warning to master sin (Genesis 4:5–8). Such wrath wounds others and reveals a heart yielding to sinful desire rather than righteous judgment.

Gen 49:7 Cursed be their anger, for it is strong,…

and anger is like a flood,
Anger can sweep through a person with force that devastates judgment, speech, and relationships. Scripture calls believers to put away wrath and to resist giving the devil an opportunity through unresolved anger (Ephesians 4:26–27, 31).

Jealousy can work more secretly and persistently than open rage, feeding resentment against another person’s blessing, position, or affection. Joseph’s brothers allowed jealousy to govern them until they sold him into slavery (Genesis 37:11, 18–28). Love rejects envy and seeks another’s good, but jealousy turns the heart against both neighbor and God’s wise providence (1 Corinthians 13:4).

Proverbs 27:5

Better an open rebuke
Loving correction is more valuable than silence that allows sin or destructive folly to continue. Nathan’s direct confrontation of David exposed the king’s guilt and led him toward repentance (2 Samuel 12:1–14). Faithful rebuke seeks restoration rather than humiliation, speaking truth with love and calling a brother or sister back to obedience (Leviticus 19:17; Galatians 6:1).

Gal 2:11 When Cephas came to Antioch, however, I opposed him…

Pro 28:23 He who rebukes a man will later find more favor…

than love that is concealed.
Affection that remains unspoken or refuses to address serious wrong fails to give needed help. Genuine love acts for another’s spiritual good, even when honest correction is uncomfortable. Christ openly confronted hypocrisy and unbelief while giving Himself sacrificially for sinners, showing that love is both truthful and active.

Proverbs 27:6

The wounds of a friend are faithful,
A true friend loves enough to speak words that may hurt for a moment but guard the soul from greater harm. Biblical friendship involves loyalty, honesty, and a shared concern for obedience to God, as seen in the covenant bond of David and Jonathan (1 Samuel 18:1–4). Faithful correction seeks restoration rather than humiliation. Nathan’s confrontation of David exposed grievous sin, yet it became the means by which David was brought to repentance (2 Samuel 12:1–13). Such counsel reflects the mutual sharpening God intends among His people (Proverbs 27:17) and must be received with humility by those who desire wisdom (Proverbs 9:8–9).

Psa 141:5 Let the righteous man strike me; let his rebuke…

Lev 19:17 You must not harbor hatred against your brother…

Pro 28:23 He who rebukes a man will later find more favor…

but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.
Outward warmth can conceal a heart set against another’s good. In the ancient world, a kiss commonly conveyed greeting, affection, honor, or covenant loyalty, making Judas’s kiss in the garden a particularly calculated act of betrayal (Matthew 26:48–49). Flattery and false reassurance may feel kinder than correction, but they lead away from truth and safety. Believers are called to test words and motives by God’s revealed truth rather than by appearances or pleasant speech (1 John 4:1; Proverbs 26:24–26). Jesus proved Himself the faithful Friend who spoke hard truth to His disciples and gave His life for them (John 15:13–15).

Proverbs 27:7

The soul that is full loathes honey,
Honey represented one of the choicest blessings of the land God promised Israel, a land “flowing with milk and honey” (Exodus 3:8). Yet physical fullness can make even a delicacy seem undesirable. The proverb exposes the danger of self-satisfaction: abundance may dull gratitude and produce indifference toward gifts once treasured. Spiritual complacency similarly blinds people to their need for God. The Laodicean church claimed to be rich and in need of nothing, while Christ declared them spiritually poor, blind, and naked (Revelation 3:17). Those satisfied with themselves often lose their appetite for Scripture, prayer, correction, and fellowship with God.

Pro 25:16 If you find honey, eat just what you need,…

Ecc 6:7 All a man’s labor is for his mouth, yet…

Hos 13:6 When they had pasture, they became satisfied;…

but to a hungry soul, any bitter thing is sweet.
Hunger gives value even to food that would otherwise be refused. A person conscious of need receives what God provides, including correction, hardship, and discipline. Those who hunger and thirst for righteousness are promised satisfaction by Christ (Matthew 5:6). Job’s suffering and Israel’s wilderness trials reveal that God may use bitter circumstances to expose the heart, humble His people, and teach dependence upon Him. The bitter experiences of life do not become good in themselves, but God uses them for the growth and endurance of those who seek Him. Spiritual hunger welcomes God’s truth even when it wounds pride, because it leads to life and deeper fellowship with Him.

Proverbs 27:8

Like a bird that strays from its nest
A nest provides shelter, rest, and protection from danger. A bird that abandons it exposes itself to hazards and leaves behind the place suited to its care and preservation. The picture warns against restless discontent that rejects God-given boundaries and responsibilities.

Home in Israel was the setting of covenant instruction, family duty, inheritance, and worship. To wander from it without righteous cause was to forsake stability and become vulnerable to temptation, folly, and loss. The warning reaches beyond a dwelling place: those who drift from faithful commitments and from fellowship with God resemble the prodigal son, who found misery away from his father’s house before repentance led him home.

Oil refreshed the body, furnished light, and consecrated priests and kings for service to the Lord. Incense filled the sanctuary with a pleasing fragrance as Israel approached God in worship, while its rising smoke pictured prayer offered before Him. Such gifts reminded God’s people that His presence brings gladness, renewal, and favor.

A faithful friend strengthens the inner life through truthful, loving counsel. Jonathan’s covenant loyalty toward David illustrates friendship marked by devotion, courage, and encouragement. Wise counsel does not merely flatter; it directs the heart toward righteousness and helps a person walk faithfully before God.

Proverbs 27:10

Do not forsake your friend or your father’s friend,
Loyalty honors the enduring relationships God has established across generations. A father’s faithful friend may carry proven wisdom, protection, and covenant kindness, and should not be discarded when circumstances change. David and Jonathan modeled such steadfast love, binding themselves in covenant despite personal cost (1 Samuel 18:1–3). Respecting faithful friends of one’s parents also accords with the honor due to father and mother (Exodus 20:12).

Family obligations remain real, yet a relative living at a distance may be unable to offer immediate help in a crisis. Wisdom recognizes the value of relationships cultivated before trouble comes. God often provides care through the ordinary faithfulness of those nearby, as the Good Samaritan showed when he aided a suffering stranger whom others passed by (Luke 10:25–37).

A present and faithful neighbor can bring prompt aid, counsel, and protection when distant family cannot. Israel’s life depended upon such local bonds, especially when travel and communication were difficult. Believers likewise are called to live in practical fellowship, bearing burdens and sharing resources (Acts 2:42–47; Galatians 6:2). Christ is the truest faithful Friend, never distant from those who belong to Him and present in every day of calamity (John 15:15; Hebrews 13:5).

Proverbs 27:11

Wisdom in Proverbs is the obedient application of the fear of the LORD to every area of life. A father’s instruction to his son reflects God’s design for wisdom to be taught within the covenant family and embodied before the next generation (Deuteronomy 6:6–7). The son’s choices carry consequences beyond himself, revealing whether he has received and followed godly instruction.

A child who walks in truth brings deep joy to a faithful parent, for such obedience displays the fruit of God’s work in the heart. Biblical joy is rooted not merely in success or reputation but in seeing those entrusted to one’s care live according to God’s ways. John expressed the same delight in hearing that his spiritual children were walking in the truth (3 John 1:4).

The wise conduct of the son vindicates the father’s instruction before those who mock it. Scoffers may despise godly wisdom, but a life marked by righteousness provides a compelling answer to their accusations. Faithful living does not silence every opponent, yet it testifies that the fear of the LORD produces integrity, discernment, and enduring hope.

Proverbs 27:12

The prudent see danger and take cover,
Wisdom recognizes danger before it becomes disaster. In ancient Israel, travelers faced bandits, hostile terrain, warfare, and sudden storms; prudent preparation could preserve life. Proverbs 22:3 repeats this teaching, showing its enduring importance. God calls His people to exercise discernment rather than presume upon His protection. Such caution includes avoiding sinful influences, heeding godly counsel, and acting on warnings revealed in Scripture. The Lord remains the true refuge and fortress of His people, yet trust in Him does not excuse careless choices.

The simple refuse to learn from warning signs and continue without discernment. Their suffering is often the consequence of ignoring what wisdom has made plain. Proverbs repeatedly teaches that choices have moral and practical results: those who reject instruction eventually bear the cost of their stubbornness. Jesus gave the same warning in the contrast between the wise builder who obeyed His words and the foolish builder whose house fell in the storm. God’s wisdom directs believers away from ruin and toward the safety found in obedient faith.

Proverbs 27:13

Take the garment of him who posts security for a stranger;
A man who guarantees another person’s debt accepts responsibility if the borrower fails to pay. Taking a garment as collateral provided a concrete safeguard against loss, since an outer cloak could be a person’s most valuable possession. Israel’s law required compassion when such a pledge was taken, commanding that a poor man’s cloak be returned by nightfall because it also served as his covering for sleep (Exodus 22:26–27). Prudence does not exclude mercy; wise stewardship recognizes both the reality of risk and the duty to treat people justly.

get collateral if it is for a foreigner.
Financial obligations outside the protection of family, clan, and covenant community could be difficult to enforce. The warning addresses the folly of assuming another person’s debt without sound knowledge of that person’s character or ability to repay. Proverbs repeatedly condemns rash suretyship because generosity without discernment can harm households and hinder faithful stewardship (Proverbs 6:1–5; 22:3). A believer should be willing to help those in need while refusing commitments that bind him to another’s irresponsibility or ungodliness.

A constant dripping on a rainy day
Pictures water repeatedly falling through a leaking roof, an aggravation familiar in Israel’s rainy season. Flat roofs of timber, brush, and packed clay required regular maintenance; neglected leaks could damage the house and make daily life miserable. The unceasing sound and inconvenience portray a trouble that cannot be set aside or ignored. Proverbs 19:13 uses the same household image to show how persistent conflict can turn a place meant for rest into a place of distress.

and a contentious woman are alike—
Continual quarrelling produces a similar effect within a household, steadily weakening peace, trust, and fellowship. The warning addresses a recurring pattern of strife rather than excusing harshness or neglect by a husband; Proverbs also condemns anger, deceit, and contentiousness wherever they appear. Marriage is intended to reflect faithful companionship and mutual wisdom, so persistent conflict calls for repentance, humility, patient speech, and the pursuit of peace.

Proverbs 27:17

In the ancient world, iron tools and weapons required repeated honing through hard contact. The friction was necessary to restore usefulness and strength. Solomon pictures godly relationships as a means by which the Lord exposes dullness, corrects weakness, and cultivates wisdom, courage, and perseverance.

Proverbs 27:18

Whoever tends a fig tree will eat its fruit,
Fig trees supplied a valued source of food in Israel and required sustained care before producing an abundant harvest. The farmer who guarded, pruned, and cultivated the tree could rightly expect to enjoy its yield. God establishes a moral order in which faithful labor ordinarily brings fitting reward, though patience is often required before the fruit appears. The image also recalls Israel’s spiritual condition, for the prophets used fig trees to portray the nation’s fruitfulness or barrenness before God (Hosea 9:10; Jeremiah 24). Diligence in ordinary responsibilities reflects faithful stewardship of what God has entrusted.

A servant who protected his master’s interests, managed his household well, and served with integrity could receive public favor and greater responsibility. Joseph’s faithful service in Potiphar’s house and later under Pharaoh illustrates such honor (Genesis 39:4–6; 41:39–41). Believers serve the Lord Christ in every rightful duty, working wholeheartedly because their ultimate inheritance comes from Him (Colossians 3:23–24). Faithfulness may not always receive immediate recognition from men, but the Master who sees every act of obedient service will honor those who remain devoted to Him.

James likewise compares hearing God’s Word without obeying it to looking at one’s face in a mirror and forgetting what was seen (James 1:23–24).

The heart reveals the inner person through desires, words, choices, and conduct. God does not judge by outward appearance alone but sees the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). Jesus taught that evil words and actions proceed from within (Matthew 15:18–19), while a good person brings forth good from the treasure of his heart (Luke 6:45). Since the fallen heart is deceitful and desperately sick (Jeremiah 17:9), lasting righteousness requires God’s inward work, producing a heart that sincerely fears Him and walks in obedience.

Proverbs 27:20

Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied;
Sheol represents the grave and the realm of the dead, while Abaddon depicts destruction and ruin. Both portray death’s continual claim upon human life; no generation escapes it, and the grave is never filled. Job declares that Sheol and Abaddon lie open before the Lord (Job 26:6), reminding readers that even death’s hidden realm is fully known and governed by God. Revelation names Abaddon as the angel of the abyss (Revelation 9:11), linking destruction with the judgment that God permits and ultimately overcomes. Death’s appetite is real, yet it is not sovereign. Christ entered death, rose bodily from the grave, and holds the keys of death and Hades (Revelation 1:18).

Pro 15:11 Sheol and Abaddon lie open before the LORD…

Isa 5:14 Therefore Sheol enlarges its throat and opens wide…

so the eyes of man are never satisfied.
Human desire often resembles the grave’s relentless appetite. The eyes continually seek more possessions, pleasures, beauty, recognition, and power, yet created things cannot give lasting rest to the soul. Ecclesiastes observes that the eye is not satisfied with seeing (Ecclesiastes 1:8), and Jesus warns that covetousness can flourish even amid abundance (Luke 12:15). Desire becomes idolatry when people look to earthly gain for what only God can provide. Contentment grows through faith, gratitude, and submission to Christ, whose living water satisfies those who come to Him (John 4:13–14).

Proverbs 27:21

A crucible for silver
In ancient metallurgy, a crucible was a container used to melt and purify metals. The process involved intense heat to separate impurities from the desired metal. This imagery is used throughout the Bible to describe purification and testing (e.g., Malachi 3:3). The crucible’s purpose is to refine, symbolizing how trials and challenges can purify a person’s character.

Psa 12:6 The words of the LORD are flawless, like silver…

Psa 66:10 For You, O God, have tested us; You have refined us…

Mal 3:3 And He will sit as a refiner and purifier of silver;…

and a furnace for gold,
Similar to the crucible, a furnace was used to refine gold, requiring even higher temperatures due to gold’s melting point. The furnace represents a more intense form of testing and purification. In biblical terms, gold often symbolizes purity and value (Revelation 3:18). The refining process is a metaphor for spiritual growth and maturity, as seen in Job 23:10, where Job speaks of coming forth as gold after being tested.

Job 23:10 Yet He knows the way I have taken; when He has tested me…

Zec 13:9 This third I will bring through the fire;…

1Pe 1:7 so that the proven character of your faith…

Proverbs 27:22

Though you grind a fool like grain with mortar and a pestle,
Mortar and pestle were familiar household tools for crushing grain into meal. The picture portrays the most severe and thorough effort to break down stubborn folly through pressure, discipline, or painful consequences. A fool rejects the fear of the Lord and refuses correction, so outward punishment alone cannot produce wisdom.

yet his folly will not depart from him.
Persistent folly arises from a resistant heart, not merely from ignorance or lack of experience. God’s correction is meant to lead sinners to repentance, but hardened people may endure consequences without forsaking their rebellion. Lasting change requires a renewed heart, which God gives through repentance, faith, and the transforming work of His Spirit.

Proverbs 27:23

Be sure to know the state of your flocks,
Flocks were a principal source of food, clothing, sacrifice, and income in ancient Israel. Their condition could not be assumed; a faithful owner observed their health, numbers, grazing, and protection. Such diligence reflects the stewardship God assigned to humanity in caring for what He provides. Scripture repeatedly uses shepherding to portray watchful, sacrificial leadership, supremely in the Lord’s care for His people (Psalm 23:1; John 10:11).

Cattle and other livestock required steady oversight, especially in a land where drought, disease, predators, and theft could quickly diminish a household’s livelihood. Wisdom calls God’s people to responsible attention rather than careless dependence on present prosperity. The same watchfulness belongs in spiritual life and service, as believers guard what God has entrusted to them and care faithfully for those under their influence (1 Peter 5:2–3, 8).

Proverbs 27:24

for riches are not forever,
Material wealth can vanish through loss, disaster, theft, war, or changing circumstances. Proverbs calls God’s people to diligent stewardship without allowing possessions to become their security. Jesus likewise warned against storing treasure only on earth, where it can be destroyed or stolen, and directed His followers toward treasure in heaven. Riches may serve useful purposes, but they cannot preserve life, secure the future, or replace wisdom and righteousness before God.

1Ti 6:7 For we brought nothing into the world…

Ecc 5:15 As a man came from his mother’s womb, so he will depart…

nor does a crown endure to every generation.
Royal power and family dynasties are also temporary. Israel and Judah witnessed the collapse of kingdoms, the removal of kings, and the end of ruling houses because the Lord judges nations and rulers according to His purposes. Even the strongest earthly throne cannot guarantee permanence for its heirs. The fading of human crowns directs hope to Jesus Christ, the Son of David, whose kingdom will never pass away and whose reign endures forever.

The cutting of hay marked the wise use of a season’s provision. In Israel’s dry climate, fodder gathered at the proper time sustained flocks and herds when pasture failed. Diligent stewardship recognized that present abundance was not to be wasted but prepared for future need.

Proverbs 27:26

the lambs will provide you with clothing,
Sheep supplied wool for garments and were central to Israel’s household economy. Careful shepherding brought enduring provision rather than temporary gain. Lambs also recall the sacrifices God appointed, culminating in Jesus Christ, the Lamb of God, whose righteousness clothes those who trust in Him (Isaiah 61:10; John 1:29).

Goats provided milk, meat, hides, and offspring, making them valuable assets that could be exchanged for land. A well-managed flock could secure a family’s future and establish lasting stability. Proverbs joins material diligence with wise stewardship, teaching that God’s ordinary provisions are to be received, guarded, and used faithfully.

Proverbs 27:27

You will have plenty of goats’ milk to feed you—
Goats supplied a dependable source of milk in Israel’s agrarian economy, sustaining families through daily labor and seasonal uncertainty. Proverbs 27:23–26 calls for careful attention to flocks, fields, and changing circumstances, since material provision often comes through faithful stewardship rather than neglect. God’s wisdom directs His people to receive such provision with gratitude and to labor responsibly with what He entrusts to them.

food for your household
A household included immediate family, extended relatives, and servants living under the authority of the home. Providing food for them was a solemn duty and a visible evidence of diligent leadership. Scripture condemns indifference toward those dependent upon one’s care, and 1 Timothy 5:8 affirms the obligation to provide for one’s own household. Earthly provision also points beyond itself to the Lord’s faithful care for those who walk in His wisdom.

and nourishment for your maidservants.
Female servants belonged to the household and were not to be treated as expendable labor. Their nourishment and well-being fell under the master’s responsibility before God. Israel’s law required just treatment of workers and servants, forbidding oppression and neglect (Deuteronomy 24:14–15). Such care reflects the pattern of Christ, who taught that true authority serves rather than exploits (Mark 10:42–45).
