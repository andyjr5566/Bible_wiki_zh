Pekah ruled the northern kingdom during a period of deep instability, rebellion, and increasing Assyrian domination. The dating by Pekah’s reign underscores that Judah’s history unfolded alongside Israel’s accelerating decline toward exile.

2 Kings 16:16

So Uriah the priest
Uriah served in the temple at Jerusalem during Ahaz’s reign, but Scripture does not identify him as high priest. His name, meaning “Yahweh is my light,” stands in sharp contrast to his willingness to assist the king’s departure from faithful worship. God had given clear instructions for the altar and sacrifices through Moses, and priests were responsible to guard that worship rather than reshape it according to royal preference. Uriah’s compliance shows how spiritual leaders can fail when fear of human authority outweighs obedience to God.

Ahaz ordered the construction of an altar modeled after one he had seen in Damascus, the capital of Assyria, and placed it at the center of temple worship. 2 Kings 16:17

King Ahaz also cut off the frames of the movable stands
The movable stands were among the bronze furnishings Solomon made for the temple, each supporting a basin used in the cleansing connected with the sacrifices (1 Kings 7:27–39; 2 Chronicles 4:6). Their detailed workmanship testified to the holiness and order God had established for worship. Ahaz treated these consecrated objects as disposable, continuing the unfaithfulness that marked his reign.

Ahaz reigned over Judah during a period of severe regional pressure from Aram, Israel, Philistia, Edom, and the expanding Assyrian Empire. The account has recorded his appeal to Tiglath-pileser III, his plundering of the temple, and his adoption of an Assyrian-style altar. His policies brought temporary military relief but placed Judah under Assyrian domination and further corrupted its worship.
