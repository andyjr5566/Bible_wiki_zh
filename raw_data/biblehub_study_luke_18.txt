Widows often lacked financial security and legal protection, making them especially vulnerable to exploitation. God’s law repeatedly commanded Israel to care for widows and warned against oppressing them (Exodus 22:22–24; Deuteronomy 10:18). Her presence in the town makes the judge’s neglect of justice all the more serious, since he was refusing aid to one whom God especially defended.

The Pharisee and the Tax Collector

Luke 18:9

To some who trusted in their own righteousness
Jesus directs this parable toward people who considered their obedience to the law, religious disciplines, and moral reputation sufficient to secure acceptance before God. Pharisees especially embodied this danger when outward devotion became grounds for confidence in themselves rather than dependence on divine mercy. Scripture declares that no one is righteous apart from God’s grace, and even humanity’s best works cannot remove sin or justify the guilty before Him (Isaiah 64:6; Romans 3:10–12).

Rom 10:3 Because they were ignorant of God’s righteousness…

Php 3:9 and be found in Him, not having my own righteousness…

Isa 64:6 Each of us has become like something unclean,…

and viewed others with contempt,
Self-righteousness produces pride and contempt because it measures others by external standards while overlooking personal sin. Such an attitude contradicted the law’s command to love one’s neighbor and ignored God’s concern for the humble, needy, and repentant. Tax collectors, Gentiles, and those regarded as ceremonially unclean were often treated as morally inferior, yet Jesus repeatedly received those who recognized their need for forgiveness.

He also told this parable:
The account that follows exposes the contrast between religious confidence and genuine repentance. Jesus uses the familiar setting of temple prayer to show that God does not justify those who present their own merit before Him, but those who come humbly, confessing their sin and seeking His mercy.

Jerusalem stood on elevated terrain, and pilgrims commonly spoke of going “up” to the city and its temple. The temple was the appointed center of Israel’s worship, where sacrifices were offered and where worshipers gathered during regular hours of prayer. Jesus places the parable in the holiest setting of Jewish life, showing that God sees the heart even where religious devotion is most visibly practiced.

Pharisees were widely regarded as serious observers of the Law and its traditions. Their concern for holiness and obedience could be commendable, yet many had come to measure righteousness by outward performance and to regard others with contempt. The Pharisee represents the danger of trusting religious achievement rather than seeking mercy from God.

Tax collectors served the Roman system and were often known for extortion, making them objects of public shame among the Jewish people. A tax collector would have appeared morally and socially inferior to a Pharisee in the eyes of Jesus’ hearers. By placing these men together before God, Jesus reveals that social standing, reputation, and religious appearance do not determine who is righteous before Him.

Pharisees were widely regarded as careful observers of the Law, its traditions, and ceremonial purity. Yet his posture reveals confidence in personal righteousness rather than dependence upon God’s mercy. Public prayer was customary, but his words do not seek forgiveness, guidance, or grace. Jesus has already warned against praying for human notice rather than before God alone (Matthew 6:5–6).

Jewish tradition commonly associated regular fasts with Mondays and Thursdays. Fasting could express repentance, mourning, prayer, or dependence upon God, but the Pharisee uses it to distinguish himself from other people. His prayer reveals confidence in his own record rather than humble reliance on God’s mercy. Jesus taught that fasting should be directed toward God, not used to gain human recognition or support self-righteousness (Matthew 6:16–18).

One man recites his works before God; the other acknowledges his sin. God receives those who come to Him in humility, not those who trust in their religious achievements.

Parents brought their infants to Jesus because they trusted His compassion and sought His blessing over lives too young to understand or speak for themselves. In a society that granted children little public status, their presence before Jesus revealed the parents’ faith in His authority and care. Jesus received those whom others considered insignificant, showing that no person is beneath the concern of the kingdom of God.

Jesus Heals a Blind Beggar

Luke 18:35

As Jesus drew near to Jericho,
Jesus approached one of the oldest cities in the land, situated in the Jordan Valley near the route leading up to Jerusalem. Jericho stood prominently in Israel’s history as the first Canaanite city conquered under Joshua after the Lord brought down its walls (Joshua 6). Jesus’ journey through Jericho occurred as He traveled toward Jerusalem to fulfill His saving mission through His death and resurrection. The city was known for its fertile surroundings, palm trees, trade, and wealth. Its prosperity formed a striking setting for the need of a destitute blind beggar. The Gospel accounts indicate that Jesus encountered the blind man in the Jericho area as He passed between the older city and the newer Herodian settlement.

a blind man was sitting beside the road, begging.
Blindness left a man especially vulnerable in the ancient world, often dependent upon family members or public charity for survival. Many people wrongly connected physical suffering with personal sin or divine judgment, though Jesus rejected such conclusions (John 9:1–3). The blind man occupied a place of social weakness beside a crowded road, yet he was near the path of the Savior. His need would soon reveal Jesus’ compassion and divine authority, while his persistent appeal would display faith that recognized in Jesus the promised Son of David.
