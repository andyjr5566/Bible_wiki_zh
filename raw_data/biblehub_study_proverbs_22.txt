A Good Name

Proverbs 22:1

A good name
In biblical times, a person’s name was more than just a label; it represented their character and reputation. The importance of a good name is emphasized throughout Scripture, as seen in Ecclesiastes 7:1, which states that a good name is better than fine perfume. The concept of a “good name” is tied to one’s integrity and moral standing within the community. In the ancient Near East, maintaining a good reputation was crucial for social and economic interactions.

Ecc 7:1 A good name is better than fine perfume,…

Pro 3:4 Then you will find favor and high regard…

1Ti 3:7 Furthermore, he must have a good reputation with outsiders,…

is more desirable than great riches;
This phrase highlights the value of character over material wealth. In a culture where wealth was often seen as a sign of God’s blessing, this proverb challenges the reader to prioritize ethical living and reputation over financial gain. The Bible frequently warns against the pursuit of wealth at the expense of righteousness, as seen in 1 Timothy 6:10, which warns that the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil. The emphasis is on the eternal value of a good name compared to the temporary nature of riches.

Pro 15:16 Better a little with the fear of the LORD…

Pro 28:6 Better a poor man who walks with integrity…

Pro 16:8 Better a little with righteousness than great gain…

favor is better than silver and gold.
Favor, or grace, refers to the approval and goodwill of others, which is often a result of having a good name. This favor can open doors and provide opportunities that wealth cannot. The Bible often speaks of God’s favor as a blessing, as seen in Psalm 5:12, where the Lord blesses the righteous with favor as a shield. The comparison to silver and gold underscores the idea that divine and human favor are more valuable than even the most precious earthly treasures. This aligns with the teachings of Jesus, who emphasized storing up treasures in heaven rather than on earth (Matthew 6:19-21).

Proverbs 22:2

The rich and the poor have this in common:
Wealth and poverty create real differences in earthly circumstance, opportunity, and influence, yet neither condition changes a person’s standing as a creature before God. The rich must not trust in possessions, and the poor must not measure their worth by what they lack. God forbids favoritism toward either class and calls His people to deal justly with every neighbor (Leviticus 19:15; James 2:1–9). Death also levels human distinctions, for no one carries earthly wealth beyond the grave.

The LORD is Maker of them all.
The covenant Lord created every human being in His image and sustains every life by His providence (Genesis 1:27; Acts 17:24–28). Human dignity does not arise from social rank, productivity, or possessions, but from God’s creative purpose. The wealthy therefore owe humility and generosity, while the poor may rest in the knowledge that they are neither overlooked nor insignificant before their Maker. God’s common creation of all people calls for compassion, justice, and reverence for the One who formed each person.

Proverbs 22:3

The prudent see danger and take cover,
Wisdom recognizes threats before they become disasters and responds with obedient action. Joseph displayed such foresight when God revealed the coming famine through Pharaoh’s dreams, storing grain during years of abundance so that Egypt and Jacob’s family might live (Genesis 41). Prudence does not deny God’s sovereignty; it trusts Him enough to heed His warnings and use the means He provides. Jesus likewise commanded His disciples to watch and be ready for His return (Matthew 24:42–44).

Those who reject counsel often press ahead as though warnings do not apply to them. Israel repeatedly heard God’s prophets call the nation to repentance, yet many continued in idolatry and injustice until judgment came. The foolish virgins also failed to prepare while there was still time, revealing that outward association with the wedding party could not replace personal readiness (Matthew 25:1–13).

Sin and folly bring real consequences in personal, family, and national life. Scripture’s principle of sowing and reaping shows that people cannot persist in rejecting God’s wisdom without eventually meeting the fruit of their choices (Galatians 6:7–8). The warning invites humble attention to God’s Word, timely repentance, and faithful preparation rather than careless confidence.

The way of moral rebellion is marked by both visible hardship and concealed danger. Thorns recall the curse upon the ground after Adam’s sin, revealing that sin brings pain, frustration, and fruitlessness into human life (Genesis 3:18). Snares portray traps that catch those who move carelessly, showing how evil habits and corrupt counsel entangle the heart. Those who reject God’s wisdom do not travel a safe road; their chosen path leads toward ruin.

Guarding the soul includes refusing the company, practices, and influences that lead away from righteousness. In Israel, the home was the primary place of spiritual formation, where God’s commandments were taught diligently in daily life (Deuteronomy 6:6–7). Such training involves dedicating a child to the Lord and directing his heart toward wisdom, obedience, and the fear of God.

Proverbs 22:7

The rich rule over the poor,
Wealth commonly brought social influence, legal advantage, and power over laborers and tenants in ancient Israel. Solomon observes a fallen reality rather than approving it. God repeatedly commanded His people to defend the poor, judge impartially, pay workers promptly, and refuse oppression (Deuteronomy 15:7–11; Isaiah 1:17). Earthly wealth can magnify a person’s power, but it does not establish righteousness or secure lasting honor before God.

Amo 8:4-6 Hear this, you who trample the needy…

and the borrower is slave to the lender.
Debt places a borrower under obligation and can restrict freedom, choices, and future labor. In Israel, severe indebtedness could lead a person to sell himself into service, though the Law required humane treatment and provided limits and release (Leviticus 25:39–43; Deuteronomy 15:12–15). The proverb commends prudence, contentment, and careful stewardship rather than financial presumption. Debt also illustrates humanity’s deeper bondage to sin, from which Christ alone provides deliverance. Through His death, He canceled the debt against His people and set them free to serve righteousness (Colossians 2:13–14; Romans 6:18).

God delights in those who use their resources to meet the needs of others. The blessing promised is not a formula for material wealth, but the favor of God upon a life that reflects His mercy. Scripture consistently joins generosity with trust in the Lord, who provides for His people and sees every act of compassion. Jesus likewise taught that giving to others accords with the character of the Father and stores treasure in heaven.

Bread represented daily sustenance in Israel, so sharing it meant giving from one’s own provision rather than offering what cost nothing. Care for the poor upheld their dignity as fellow image-bearers and demonstrated covenant faithfulness. Christ embodied such compassion by feeding the hungry and ultimately giving Himself as the Bread of Life for needy sinners.

A mocker openly despises correction, ridicules what is right, and provokes others to join in contempt. Such a person does more than hold private doubts; he actively spreads strife and resists the fear of the Lord that is the beginning of wisdom. Israel’s leaders were responsible to guard the covenant community from corrupting influences, and later the church received similar responsibility to exercise discipline when persistent, unrepentant sin threatened the body (1 Corinthians 5:13). Removing a divisive person is not permission for personal vengeance, but a necessary act of righteous protection and a summons to repentance.

Proverbs 22:16

Oppressing the poor to enrich oneself
This phrase highlights a common theme in biblical wisdom literature: the condemnation of exploiting the vulnerable. In ancient Israel, the poor were often landless laborers or those who had fallen into debt. The Law of Moses explicitly forbade the oppression of the poor (Exodus 22:21-27, Leviticus 19:13). Prophets like Amos and Isaiah also spoke against such injustices, emphasizing God’s concern for the marginalized (Amos 2:6-7, Isaiah 10:1-2). The act of oppressing the poor to gain wealth is seen as a violation of God’s justice and mercy, which are central to His character. This principle is echoed in the New Testament, where Jesus warns against storing up treasures on earth at the expense of others (Matthew 6:19-21).

Pro 14:31 Whoever oppresses the poor taunts their Maker,…

Jas 5:4-6 Look, the wages you withheld from the workmen…

Amo 5:11-12 Therefore, because you trample on the poor…

or giving gifts to the rich
In the cultural context of the ancient Near East, giving gifts to the rich was often a means of currying favor or securing one’s social standing. This practice is criticized here as it perpetuates inequality and ignores the needs of the poor. The Bible consistently teaches that true generosity is directed towards those who cannot repay (Luke 14:12-14). The act of giving to the rich is seen as futile and counterproductive, as it does not align with God’s heart for justice and equity. This principle is further illustrated in the parable of the rich fool (Luke 12:16-21), where hoarding wealth and seeking favor with the affluent is shown to be ultimately worthless.

Jas 2:1-4 My brothers, as you hold out your faith…

God’s moral order overturns wealth built through injustice and favor-seeking. Generosity toward the needy brings blessing, while selfish gain ultimately proves empty (Proverbs 11:24–25; Galatians 6:7).

Saying 2

Proverbs 22:22

Do not rob a poor man because he is poor,
Poverty must never be treated as an opportunity for exploitation. Those with little wealth, social standing, or legal influence are especially vulnerable to dishonest creditors, merchants, and powerful neighbors. The Lord’s law required His people to deal justly with the poor, refusing partiality either for or against them (Leviticus 19:15). Israel was to remember that God had redeemed them from helplessness in Egypt, and therefore they were to show mercy and fairness to the needy among them (Deuteronomy 15:7–11). Wealth does not grant permission to take advantage of another person, nor does need diminish a person’s dignity before God.

Lev 19:13 You must not defraud your neighbor or rob him.…

Deu 24:14-15 Do not oppress a hired hand who is poor and needy…

Pro 14:31 Whoever oppresses the poor taunts their Maker,…

and do not crush the afflicted at the gate,
City gates served as public gathering places where elders heard disputes, witnessed agreements, and rendered judgments. An afflicted person could be crushed there when influential men used bribery, false testimony, or legal power to deny justice. God repeatedly condemned such corruption and commanded judges to defend the weak, the fatherless, the widow, and the poor (Psalm 82:3–4; Isaiah 1:17). The warning carries special weight because the next proverb declares that the Lord Himself will plead the cause of the oppressed and repay those who prey upon them (Proverbs 22:23). Human courts may fail, but the Judge of all the earth sees every injustice and upholds the cause of those who cannot defend themselves.

Proverbs 22:23

for the LORD will take up their case
This phrase emphasizes God's role as the ultimate judge and advocate for the oppressed. In the ancient Near Eastern context, legal systems often favored the wealthy and powerful, leaving the poor and marginalized without recourse. The Bible consistently portrays God as a defender of the downtrodden, as seen in passages like Psalm 68:5, which describes God as a "father to the fatherless" and a "defender of widows." This advocacy is not just a promise of future justice but an assurance of God's active involvement in the present. The phrase also reflects the covenantal relationship between God and His people, where He promises to uphold justice and righteousness. This is a recurring theme throughout the Old Testament, particularly in the prophetic books, where God is depicted as taking up the cause of the oppressed against corrupt leaders and systems.

Jer 50:34 Their Redeemer is strong; the LORD of Hosts is His name.…

Psa 72:4 May he vindicate the afflicted among the people……

Psa 82:3-4 Defend the cause of the weak and fatherless;…

and will plunder those who rob them.
This part of the verse underscores the principle of divine retribution. In biblical history, God often intervened to bring justice to those who were wronged, as seen in the stories of the Exodus, where God delivered the Israelites from Egyptian oppression. The term "plunder" suggests a reversal of fortunes, where those who exploit others will themselves be stripped of their ill-gotten gains. This is consistent with the biblical theme of sowing and reaping, as articulated in Galatians 6:7, "Do not be deceived: God cannot be mocked. A man reaps what he sows." The imagery of plundering also connects to the prophetic literature, where God is described as executing judgment on nations and individuals who act unjustly. This phrase can be seen as a type of Christ, who in His earthly ministry, consistently stood against injustice and ultimately, through His death and resurrection, defeated the powers of sin and death, bringing about a new order of justice and righteousness.

Saying 3

Proverbs 22:24

Do not make friends with an angry man,
Close companionship shapes character, habits, and judgment. Proverbs repeatedly links uncontrolled anger with folly, conflict, and destructive speech (Proverbs 14:17; 29:22). An angry person may draw others into quarrels, retaliation, and decisions that dishonor God. Wisdom therefore calls for caution in choosing intimate friends, not contempt toward those who struggle with anger. Believers are to pursue peace and to encourage repentance, while refusing relationships that steadily entice them toward sin.

The warning intensifies because a quick-tempered person can spread strife through a household or community. God calls His people to be slow to anger, ready to hear, and governed by righteousness rather than impulse (James 1:19–20). Christ’s gentleness provides the pattern for relationships marked by patience, self-control, and peace.

Proverbs 22:27

If you have nothing with which to pay,
This phrase addresses the issue of debt and financial responsibility, a common theme in biblical wisdom literature. In ancient Israel, debt was a serious matter, often leading to servitude or loss of property. The Mosaic Law provided guidelines for lending and borrowing, emphasizing fairness and compassion (Exodus 22:25-27, Leviticus 25:35-37). The Bible consistently warns against the dangers of debt (Proverbs 22:7) and encourages living within one’s means. This phrase highlights the importance of considering one’s ability to repay before incurring debt, reflecting a principle of stewardship and responsibility.

A bed represented one of the most basic possessions of a household, providing rest, warmth, and security. Its loss portrays the humiliating end of careless borrowing or rash pledges. Though creditors could lawfully seek repayment, the Mosaic Law placed limits upon collateral, protecting necessities and requiring compassion toward the debtor (Deuteronomy 24:12–13). Wisdom therefore joins responsibility with mercy: people must avoid obligations they cannot bear, while those with power over debtors must remember the dignity and need of their neighbor.

Saying 5

Proverbs 22:28

Do not move an ancient boundary stone
Boundary stones marked inherited allotments and protected families from fraud. Moving one secretly enlarged a dishonest neighbor’s field at another family’s expense, making the act a form of theft and oppression. Israel’s law expressly condemned such conduct and placed a curse on anyone who altered a neighbor’s boundary marker (Deuteronomy 19:14; 27:17). The command upholds justice, contentment, and respect for rights established under God’s providence. It also warns against gaining advantage by violating the moral limits God has set.

Pro 23:10 Do not move an ancient boundary stone…

Deu 19:14 You must not move your neighbor’s boundary marker…

Deu 27:17 Cursed is he who moves his neighbor’s boundary stone.…

which your fathers have placed.
The boundaries had often been established by earlier generations after the division of the land among Israel’s tribes and clans. Honoring them preserved family inheritance, social order, and the memory of God’s provision. Wisdom receives the faithful instruction of godly forebears rather than discarding established truth for personal gain. Believers are likewise called to contend for the faith once for all delivered to the saints (Jude 3) and to remain within the righteous boundaries revealed in God’s Word.
