Psalm 42:1

For the choirmaster.
The psalm was entrusted to the leader responsible for Israel’s sacred music, indicating its use in gathered worship. Private anguish and longing for God were therefore brought into the life of the covenant community, where the people sang truth together before the Lord.

A Maskil of the sons of Korah.
The sons of Korah were Levitical servants who became singers and guardians in temple worship. Though Korah rebelled against Moses and perished under God’s judgment, his descendants did not die and later served faithfully before the Lord (Numbers 26:11; 1 Chronicles 9:19). Their ministry testifies to God’s grace and to the enduring call to worship Him with reverence.

The psalmist longs for God with the urgency of a traveler craving water in a barren land. Earthly comforts, security, and human companionship cannot satisfy the soul created to know and worship its Maker. David voiced the same desire when he sought God in a dry and weary land (Psalm 63:1).

Israel’s God is not an idol of stone, wood, or human invention, but the sovereign Creator who speaks, acts, saves, judges, and keeps covenant with His people. When shall I come and appear in God’s presence?
The question reflects grief over separation from the sanctuary and from the gathered worship of God’s people. Appearing before God was associated with drawing near at the place He chose for His name, especially during Israel’s appointed feasts. The psalmist seeks more than relief from trouble; he desires restored fellowship with God. Through Christ, believers may now draw near with confidence, because He has opened the way into God’s presence (Hebrews 10:19–22).

Psalm 42:3

My tears have been my food both day and night,
Mourning has become so consuming that tears seem to replace ordinary nourishment. Fasting commonly accompanied grief and prayer, and the unbroken sorrow of day and night recalls Jerusalem’s lament in Lamentations 2:18–19. Job likewise described eyes darkened by weeping (Job 16:16). Separation from the joy of worship and the apparent delay of God’s help have left the psalmist deeply afflicted.

while men ask me all day long, “Where is your God?”
Enemies interpret suffering as proof that the Lord has abandoned His servant. Similar taunts appear in Psalms 79:10 and Joel 2:17, where the nations challenge the God of Israel when His people are humbled. The question anticipates the mockery directed at Jesus during His crucifixion, when His enemies doubted the Father’s care for Him (Matthew 27:43). God’s unseen work does not mean His absence; the psalmist’s continued cry reveals faith struggling for renewed assurance.

The psalmist brings unguarded anguish to the God with whom he stands in covenant. Despair does not silence faith; it drives him toward the Lord as his only refuge. The Psalms repeatedly show God’s people crying out from profound affliction while continuing to trust His mercy, as Psalm 22 also demonstrates. “My God” anticipates the cry of Jesus on the cross, who bore abandonment and judgment so that His people would not be forsaken.

Far from Jerusalem and its appointed worship, the psalmist deliberately recalls the Lord’s character and saving works. The Jordan marked Israel’s entrance into the promised land under Joshua, testifying to God’s faithfulness to bring His people through barriers they could not overcome. Distance from familiar places of worship cannot remove a believer from God’s presence or prevent faithful remembrance.

God remains the psalmist’s secure refuge even while sorrow and confusion press upon him. The title “my Rock” joins personal faith to the Lord’s unchanging power, recalling God’s deliverance of Israel and His protection of those who seek Him. Faith does not deny distress; it brings distress directly to the One whose character does not change.

Psalm 13 and other laments give similar voice to believers who wait for God’s help amid prolonged trouble. Psalm 42:10

Like the crushing of my bones,
The psalmist describes grief so severe that it reaches the center of his strength and vitality. His suffering is not superficial; reproach has wounded him inwardly, as bodily pain penetrates to the bones. Job likewise spoke of pain that gnawed at his bones in the night (Job 30:17), revealing how affliction can overwhelm both body and soul.

my enemies taunt me,
The adversaries add cruelty to suffering by treating the psalmist’s distress as evidence of defeat. In Israel’s honor-conscious culture, public mockery brought shame, yet the deeper injury was their contempt for the God whom he trusted. The righteous often endured such scorn because their confidence in the Lord exposed the rebellion of those who opposed Him.

Their repeated question challenges God’s faithfulness and suggests that His apparent silence proves His absence. The same accusation confronts God’s people throughout Scripture when deliverance seems delayed. Jesus Himself endured mockery at the cross from those who demanded that He save Himself (Matthew 27:39–44). The psalmist’s pain is real, but the question of his enemies does not determine reality: God remains present, and His salvation will ultimately silence every accusation.
