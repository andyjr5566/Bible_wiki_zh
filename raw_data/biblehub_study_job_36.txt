Elihu Describes God’s Power

Job 36:1

And Elihu continued:
Elihu resumes his final speech with sustained confidence that God’s justice can be defended. His words lead into an extended meditation on God’s greatness in creation, providence, and judgment. Although Elihu speaks with zeal and insight beyond that of Job’s three older friends, he does not provide the final answer to Job’s suffering. His speech prepares the way for the Lord Himself to speak from the whirlwind, revealing that true wisdom belongs to God alone.

Elihu appeals beyond the limited judgments of Job’s friends and the immediate circumstances of Job’s suffering. He seeks to speak of God’s works, character, and government of the world, recognizing that true wisdom comes from the Lord, who reveals His glory in creation and His truth in His word.

Elihu rightly affirms that God is the Creator and righteous Judge, whose dealings are never corrupt or arbitrary. Job’s suffering had raised agonizing questions, but it had not diminished God’s justice. The Lord’s righteousness is ultimately displayed in Christ, who bore sin’s judgment so that God might remain just while justifying those who believe.

Job 36:4

Elihu presents his coming defense of God’s justice as a truthful witness rather than empty speculation. His confidence stands against the inadequate explanations offered by Job’s three friends, who had repeatedly assumed that suffering always proves personal sin. Elihu insists that God’s dealings are righteous even when human beings cannot immediately discern their purpose. God is utterly faithful and incapable of falsehood, and every human claim about Him must be measured by His revealed truth.

Elihu speaks boldly of his own understanding, claiming that Job has before him a man prepared to speak accurately about God. Yet perfect knowledge belongs ultimately to the Lord alone, whose wisdom governs creation, providence, judgment, and redemption. The claim also directs attention beyond Elihu to Jesus Christ, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge (Colossians 2:3). Christ alone perfectly reveals the Father and speaks without error, bringing truth and grace to those whose understanding is limited.

Job 36:5

Elihu directs Job’s attention to the God whose power sustains creation and rules every human circumstance. Divine strength is never limited by suffering, opposition, or human inability. The Lord’s sovereignty over Job’s trials does not mean that Job’s pain is unnoticed; it means that no part of his life has escaped God’s authority or purpose.

God’s greatness does not make Him distant, cruel, or indifferent toward the weak. He judges without favoritism and regards both rulers and sufferers with perfect justice. Job’s affliction was not evidence that God had rejected him. The Lord hears the cry of the needy and calls His people to reflect His concern for those whom the world overlooks.

God’s judgment against evil is certain, even when the wicked appear secure for a time. Elihu affirms that the Lord does not ignore violence, oppression, or rebellion against His rule. Job’s suffering must not be used to deny God’s justice, nor should the temporary prosperity of evil be mistaken for divine approval. Psalm 37:28 likewise declares that the Lord loves justice and does not forsake His faithful ones. God’s moral order will ultimately expose and judge all wickedness.

The Lord sees those who are oppressed, wounded, and powerless before human authorities. He is a refuge for the oppressed in times of trouble, as Psalm 9:9 declares, and He hears the cries of those whom others disregard. God’s care for the afflicted does not always mean immediate deliverance, as Job’s own life shows, but it guarantees that suffering is not hidden from Him and that His righteous judgment will prevail. Jesus Christ displayed this divine concern throughout His earthly ministry by receiving the poor, healing the suffering, confronting oppressors, and proclaiming good news to the afflicted.

God’s watchful care rests on those who fear Him and seek to walk in His ways. Job’s suffering had not placed him beyond God’s sight; even the righteous may endure severe affliction while remaining under the Lord’s attentive providence. Job 36:8

Elihu portrays people brought low by severe restraint, whether through literal captivity, oppression, or circumstances beyond their power to escape. Israel knew such bondage in Egypt and later in Babylon, where national sin brought divine judgment yet did not cancel God’s covenant purposes. Chains also picture sin’s mastery over those who yield themselves to it (Romans 6:16). God alone can break such bondage and bring His people into true freedom (Galatians 5:1).

Elihu will explain that God may use suffering to expose pride, call sinners from evil, and turn them again to Himself (Job 36:9–10). Job’s ordeal, however, warns against assuming that every sufferer is being punished for a particular sin. The Lord disciplines His children in love, not to destroy them, but to produce repentance and holiness (Hebrews 12:6). Those overwhelmed by trouble may call upon Him, for He is able to deliver from the cords of death and distress (Psalm 116:3–4).

Job 36:9

then He tells them their deeds
God does not afflict without purpose or leave sufferers in darkness about their spiritual condition. Elihu presents the Lord as the righteous Judge who exposes conduct that people may excuse, overlook, or hide. God’s knowledge reaches every thought and action, and His disclosure of sin is meant to bring repentance rather than destruction. Through conscience, discipline, Scripture, and faithful messengers, the Lord calls His people to reckon honestly with their ways.

Pride turns sin into defiance because it refuses God’s authority and trusts human judgment above His word. Elihu identifies arrogant rebellion as a serious cause for divine correction, especially when prosperity or strength has made a person forget dependence upon the Lord. Israel repeatedly faced this danger, and the prophets exposed such pride in order to summon the nation back to covenant faithfulness. God humbles the self-exalting, yet He receives those who repent and submit themselves to Him.

God uses affliction to awaken those who have become hardened or careless, making them attentive to His righteous ways. Elihu teaches that suffering may serve as divine discipline, exposing pride and calling the afflicted to humble obedience (Job 36:8–9). God’s correction expresses His fatherly care rather than arbitrary cruelty, as Proverbs 3:11–12 declares. The Servant of the Lord received God’s instruction with willing obedience (Isaiah 50:5), providing the pattern of submission that sinful people need.

Israel’s prophets repeatedly gave this summons, warning that continued sin would bring judgment while repentance would bring mercy (Isaiah 1:16–18; Jeremiah 25:5). Jesus likewise began His public ministry with the command to repent and believe the gospel (Mark 1:15). God’s discipline seeks restoration, but those who refuse His instruction remain accountable for their iniquity.

Job 36:11

Elihu describes those whom God has humbled through affliction and instructed concerning sin (Job 36:8–10). Obedience requires turning from self-will to God’s revealed commands, while service expresses faithful devotion to Him. Israel’s covenant life likewise joined love for God with obedience and wholehearted service (Deuteronomy 11:13–15). God’s discipline is meant to restore His people, not merely to expose their weakness.

Such prosperity includes the security, provision, and peace that come from God’s favor, not merely an abundance of possessions. Job’s suffering, however, shows that earthly circumstances cannot always be read as a direct measure of personal righteousness. The Lord ultimately vindicated Job and restored him, yet the book directs faith beyond temporary blessings to trust in God Himself.

God’s discipline calls people to humble submission rather than hardened resistance. Elihu presents obedience as the proper response to God’s righteous rule, echoing the covenant warnings of Deuteronomy 28. Israel displayed such resentment in the wilderness when hardship led them to accuse God and desire a return to Egypt (Numbers 14:2–4). Cain likewise allowed anger to rule his heart rather than receive God’s warning and turn from sin (Genesis 4:5–8). A heart closed to God interprets His righteousness as opposition rather than mercy.

God’s restraints are never outside His authority, and even His judgments reveal His right to rule. He does not delight in the death of the wicked but calls them to repent and live (Ezekiel 18:23).

Job 36:14

They die in their youth,
Elihu warns that persistent rebellion against God leads to ruin rather than life. His words portray an early, dishonorable death as the end of those who refuse divine correction and harden themselves in ungodliness. Scripture consistently teaches that sin brings death, while the fear of the Lord leads to life (Romans 6:23; Proverbs 9:10–11). Job’s suffering itself must not be reduced to a simple punishment for personal sin, yet Elihu rightly declares that continued resistance to God’s discipline is spiritually deadly.

Elihu describes the wicked as ending in disgrace among those devoted to practices God condemns, warning that rebellion ultimately joins a person to shame rather than to the living God.

Job 36:15

God can use suffering to humble, correct, and restore His people rather than merely to punish them. Elihu presents affliction as a means by which the Lord draws the suffering person away from self-reliance and toward repentance, wisdom, and renewed trust. Job’s trials do not prove that he is secretly wicked, but they reveal that God’s purposes in suffering may extend beyond immediate human understanding. Hebrews 12:6 likewise teaches that the Lord disciplines those He loves, and Romans 5:3–5 shows that suffering can produce perseverance, proven character, and hope.

Isaiah describes the faithful servant as one whose ear is opened to receive God’s instruction (Isaiah 50:4–5). Christ’s suffering supremely displays that affliction is not outside God’s redemptive purpose, for through His obedience unto death He opened the way for sinners to hear, receive, and obey God’s call.

Elihu believed that God used hardship to turn people from sin and to lead them toward restoration. Psalm 18:19 likewise celebrates the Lord’s rescue of David from enemies and calamity.

Elihu warns Job that his bitter words and resistance to God’s dealings resemble the outlook of the wicked, who stand under divine judgment. Job’s sufferings did not prove that he was a wicked man, for the Lord had identified him as blameless and upright (Job 1:1, 8). Yet Job needed to guard against allowing affliction to draw him into accusing God of injustice. Suffering may expose sin, but it may also refine faith and display God’s wisdom beyond human understanding.

For those who trust in Christ, the judgment deserved for sin was borne by Him, who suffered in the place of sinners and brought them to God (Isaiah 53:5–6; 1 Peter 3:18).

Job’s former wealth had marked him as blessed in the eyes of his contemporaries, yet his suffering had exposed the error of measuring God’s favor by material abundance. Riches can become a substitute for trust in God and can tempt a suffering person to seek relief apart from righteousness. Elihu warns Job not to let prolonged suffering lead him into sinful resentment, self-justification, or rebellion against God. Affliction tests whether faith will remain anchored in God’s righteousness when His purposes are not understood. Scripture repeatedly calls God’s people to guard their steps and refuse the path of evil (Proverbs 4:27). Behold, God is exalted in His power.
Elihu directs Job’s attention from suffering and unanswered questions to the Lord’s sovereign greatness. God’s power created and sustains all things, governs the forces of nature, and accomplishes His righteous purposes without weakness or injustice. His exaltation is not merely a position above creation but the rightful honor owed to the Creator who rules with perfect wisdom. Job could trust God even when he could not understand the reasons for his affliction.

Job 36:23

Who has appointed His way for Him,
Elihu declares that no creature has set the course God must follow. The Lord governs His works by His own wisdom, purpose, and righteousness. Job’s suffering had raised painful questions, but neither Job nor his friends possessed the knowledge or authority to direct God’s administration of the world. Isaiah likewise asks who has directed the Spirit of the Lord or taught Him counsel (Isaiah 40:13–14). God’s purposes are never shaped by human ignorance, pressure, or judgment.

or told Him, ‘You have done wrong’?
No one can bring a valid charge of evil against the holy God. His ways may be hidden from human sight, yet His character remains perfectly just, faithful, and upright (Deuteronomy 32:4). Elihu does not deny the reality of suffering; he denies that suffering gives man grounds to condemn his Maker. The Creator owes no explanation to His creatures, while He remains wholly righteous in all He does. Humble faith submits to God’s wisdom and trusts that His purposes are good even when His providence is difficult to understand.

Job 36:24

Remember to magnify His work,
Elihu directs Job’s attention away from unanswered questions and toward the evident greatness of God’s deeds. God’s work in creation, providence, judgment, and mercy displays His wisdom and sovereign authority. Suffering does not diminish His goodness or place Him under human scrutiny. Job had rightly feared God, yet his grief had drawn him into defending himself rather than adoring the Lord. Elihu calls him back to worshipful remembrance. Psalm 34:3 likewise summons God’s people to exalt the Lord together, while creation itself continually bears witness to His power and majesty (Psalm 19:1–4).

God’s mighty acts have been preserved and proclaimed through worship across generations. Moses and Miriam sang after the Lord delivered Israel through the Red Sea (Exodus 15), and the Psalms repeatedly call believers to sing of His creation, faithfulness, salvation, and righteous judgments. Praise does not deny sorrow; it places sorrow beneath the greater reality of God’s unchanging character. Singing of His works joins personal faith to the testimony of all who have known His deliverance and seen His hand at work.

Job 36:25

God’s works are displayed openly in creation, providence, and the ordering of the world. Elihu calls Job to remember that no person lives beyond the witness of God’s power. The heavens, rain, seasons, and sustaining order of the earth testify to the Creator’s wisdom and rule. Romans 1:20 likewise teaches that God’s eternal power and divine nature are evident in what He has made, leaving mankind responsible to honor Him.

Humanity can observe God’s works, yet cannot fully comprehend His counsels or measure His greatness. Elihu’s words prepare for the Lord’s later questions to Job concerning creation, where Job learns that God’s wisdom exceeds human understanding. The distance is not merely physical but spiritual and intellectual: fallen people see the evidence of God’s hand without naturally grasping His purposes. In Christ, God has drawn near and made Himself known, for the Son reveals the Father while still calling His people to humble reverence before His unfathomable wisdom.

Job 36:27

For He draws up drops of water
God sovereignly governs the waters of the earth, lifting them from seas, rivers, and land into the atmosphere. Elihu points to an observable work of creation that displays wisdom beyond human control. The Lord who separated the waters in Genesis 1:7 continually sustains the order He established. Rain does not arise by chance but comes through His providential care for the world. Psalm 104:13 likewise praises God for watering the mountains and satisfying the earth with the fruit of His works.

The process reveals both God’s power and His mercy, for He provides what the earth cannot produce for itself. Rain often signifies divine blessing in Scripture, while its cleansing quality also illustrates the need for spiritual renewal. Isaiah 1:16–18 calls sinners to be washed and made clean through God’s gracious forgiveness. Jesus fulfills the deeper need for life when He offers living water to all who believe in Him (John 4:10–14).

As Romans 11:33 declares, His judgments and ways are beyond tracing out, while His faithful care remains evident in all He has made.

Job 36:29

Furthermore, who can understand how the clouds spread out,
Elihu points to the vast canopy of clouds as evidence that God’s works exceed human mastery. God gathers, directs, and disperses the clouds according to His wisdom, using them for rain, mercy, discipline, and judgment. The cloud that led Israel in the wilderness and the clouds from which God is portrayed as riding declare His active rule over creation (Exodus 13:21–22; Psalm 104:3). Human inability to explain every work of God calls for humility before the Creator rather than accusation against His providence.

God’s pavilion is the storm-cloud canopy from which His thunder resounds, hidden yet unmistakably powerful. Scripture repeatedly identifies thunder with the majestic voice of the Lord, before whom creation trembles (Psalm 29:3–9). Elihu’s description prepares for the Lord’s own appearance in the whirlwind, where Job is confronted not with a human explanation for suffering but with the wisdom and sovereignty of God. The God who speaks through the storm has finally made Himself known through His Son, the eternal Word, who reveals the Father perfectly (John 1:1–14).

The forces that nourish the earth can also restrain human pride, expose helplessness, and bring judgment upon peoples who persist in sin. God governs every nation with perfect righteousness, as Abraham confessed: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?” (Genesis 18:25). His judgments are never arbitrary, but reveal His holiness and summon men to repentance.

Elihu portrays the Lord as personally directing the forces of the storm. Lightning, feared as a sign of overwhelming power in the ancient world, remains under God’s hand rather than operating by chance. The God who formed the heavens sends lightning as He wills, displaying both majesty and judgment (Psalm 18:14; Job 37:3–5). His power over the storm anticipates the authority Jesus displayed when He commanded the wind and sea to be still (Mark 4:39).

God’s word accomplishes all that He intends (Isaiah 55:11), and His rule over the smallest details of nature assures Job that divine wisdom is never random, delayed, or misdirected.
