Taste and See That the LORD Is Good

Psalm 34:1

Of David, when he pretended to be insane before Abimelech, so that the king drove him away.
Psalm 34 grew out of David’s escape from Gath while fleeing Saul. First Samuel 21:10–15 identifies the Philistine king as Achish; Abimelech was likely a royal title, as Pharaoh was in Egypt. David had entered Gath carrying Goliath’s sword and was recognized by Achish’s servants as Israel’s celebrated warrior. Fearing for his life, he acted insane until Achish expelled him. God preserved David even in a dangerous situation marked by fear and weakness. The psalm looks back on that deliverance and turns a season of danger into public testimony of God’s faithfulness.

I will bless the LORD at all times;
David resolved to honor the LORD not only after deliverance but through every changing condition of life. Praise did not depend on safety, comfort, or outward success. Such worship rests on the character of God, who remains worthy when His servants face threats, uncertainty, and distress. David’s determination anticipates the New Testament call to rejoice always, pray continually, and give thanks in every circumstance (1 Thessalonians 5:16–18).

David’s worship was meant to be spoken openly and continually. The lips reveal the settled devotion of the heart, especially when God has answered cries for help. Believers offer God the continuing sacrifice of praise through grateful confession of His name (Hebrews 13:15). David’s deliverance became a reason to speak of the LORD’s goodness so that others might learn to trust Him.

Having escaped danger among the Philistines, David bears witness that God alone preserves His servants. Such glorying directs attention away from human achievement and toward the covenant faithfulness of God. Paul later applies the same truth to salvation: “Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 1:31).

Psalm 34:7

The angel of the LORD
This phrase often refers to a divine messenger who represents God Himself. In the Old Testament, the “angel of the LORD” is sometimes seen as a theophany, a visible manifestation of God. This figure appears in various significant moments, such as with Hagar in Genesis 16:7-13, Moses in Exodus 3:2, and Gideon in Judges 6:11-24. The presence of the angel of the LORD often signifies divine intervention and protection.

Isa 63:9 In all their distress, He too was afflicted…

encamps around
The imagery of encamping suggests a military or protective stance, indicating a readiness to defend. In ancient times, armies would encamp around a city or a person to provide security. This phrase implies a continuous and vigilant protection, much like a shepherd watching over his flock. It evokes the idea of God’s constant presence and safeguarding of His people.

This fear is not about being afraid but about honoring God and living in obedience to His will. Proverbs 9:10 states, “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom,” highlighting the importance of this attitude in a believer’s life. Those who fear God are promised His protection and guidance.

The promise of deliverance is a reassurance of God’s faithfulness and His ability to rescue His people from any peril. This phrase connects to the broader biblical narrative of redemption and salvation, culminating in the work of Christ.

David calls God’s people to know the Lord through trusting fellowship rather than distant observation. God’s goodness is displayed in His mercy, faithfulness, protection, and provision, as David had repeatedly experienced amid danger and deliverance. The imagery of tasting portrays a real encounter with God’s grace, like receiving nourishing food. God’s Word is likewise sweeter than honey to those who receive it in faith (Psalm 119:103). The Lord revealed His goodness to Moses as compassionate, gracious, patient, forgiving, and just (Exodus 34:6–7).

The Lord becomes a secure refuge for those who depend on Him, preserving them according to His wise purpose and sustaining them through trouble. Jesus fulfills this care as the Good Shepherd, who lays down His life for His sheep and keeps them in His saving protection (John 10:11).

Righteousness does not exempt anyone from trouble, because a fallen world resists God’s rule and those who belong to Him. Jesus warned His disciples that the world’s hatred of Him would extend to them (John 15:18–20). Afflictions may involve persecution, loss, illness, opposition, temptation, and inward grief, yet they do not prove that God has abandoned His people.

John identifies Jesus’ unbroken bones at the crucifixion as the fulfillment of Psalm 34:20 (John 19:36). Though Roman soldiers broke the legs of the men crucified beside Him to hasten death, Jesus had already died and was not subjected to that act. His death also fulfilled the Passover requirement that none of the lamb’s bones be broken (Exodus 12:46; Numbers 9:12), revealing Him as the perfect sacrificial Lamb whose offering was complete.

Psalm 34:21

Evil will slay the wicked,
God’s moral order ensures that rebellion eventually turns upon those who embrace it. Sin promises freedom but produces ruin, as seen in Haman’s execution on the gallows he prepared for Mordecai (Esther 7:10). The wicked may appear secure for a time, yet evil carries the seed of death, fulfilling the truth that sin’s wages are death (Romans 6:23).

Psalm 34:22

The LORD redeems His servants,
The LORD delivers those who belong to Him from guilt, distress, and the power of their enemies. He redeemed Israel from Egypt with mighty acts and brought them into covenant fellowship with Himself (Exodus 6:6). Boaz’s redemption of Naomi’s family inheritance and his marriage to Ruth also display God’s faithful provision for the vulnerable (Ruth 4:9–10). God’s servants are not self-reliant people but those who live under His authority in faith and obedience. Jesus Christ brings redemption to its fullest fulfillment by purchasing sinners through His blood and making them servants of righteousness (Ephesians 1:7; Romans 6:22).

and none who take refuge in Him
God Himself is the safe dwelling place of His people amid danger, accusation, and sorrow. The cities of refuge in Israel provided protection for one awaiting judgment, illustrating the value God places on shelter from destruction (Numbers 35:9–15). Throughout the Psalms, the LORD is called a rock, fortress, shield, and stronghold because His protection is personal and sure (Psalm 18:2). Christ receives all who come to Him in faith, giving rest and security to those burdened by sin (Matthew 11:28–30).
