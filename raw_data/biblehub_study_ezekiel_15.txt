A vine produces valuable fruit, but its wood is weak and unsuitable for building. Israel’s calling did not rest on natural strength, military power, or inherent superiority, but on God’s gracious choice and covenant purpose. Scripture often portrays Israel as God’s vine, planted to bear the fruit of righteousness and faithful worship.

Ezekiel 15:3

Can wood be taken from it to make something useful?
A vine is cultivated for its fruit, not for its wood. Its twisted, porous branches lack the strength needed for beams, furniture, or tools. Ezekiel’s image exposes the condition of Jerusalem and Judah after persistent covenant unfaithfulness: a people planted by God to bear the fruit of righteousness had become spiritually barren. Israel’s election did not make the nation valuable apart from obedience and faith. The Lord had brought His vine out of Egypt, planted it in the land, and cared for it, yet it produced corruption rather than justice (Psalm 80:8–16; Isaiah 5:1–7). Fruitless vine wood retained no practical value.

Psa 80:16 Your vine has been cut down and burned; they perish…

Or can one make from it a peg on which to hang utensils?
Even a small household peg required durable wood able to bear weight, but vine wood could not reliably serve such a simple purpose. Jerusalem had failed to fulfill the calling entrusted to her among the nations. Rather than displaying the holiness, justice, and faithfulness of the Lord, the city had embraced idolatry and violence. The image recalls the dependable peg in Isaiah 22:23–25, where security and responsibility are pictured by something firmly fastened; unfaithfulness, however, brings collapse. Jesus identified Himself as the true vine and called His disciples to abide in Him, for only those who remain in Him bear lasting fruit (John 15:1–8).

Can it be useful for anything?
A vine already unfit for craftsmanship becomes still more useless after burning. The question exposes the folly of assuming that Jerusalem’s covenant privileges, temple, or history would shield it from discipline while it continued in rebellion. Yet the judgment of the unfruitful vine also underscores God’s call for His people to abide in Him and bear the fruit that reflects faithful obedience.

Ezekiel 15:5

Even when it was whole, it could not be made useful.
Vine wood had little value apart from the fruit it produced. Israel’s calling was not to display earthly strength but to bear the fruit of faithfulness to the Lord and to reflect His holiness among the nations (Psalm 80:8–16; Isaiah 5:1–7; 42:6). Jerusalem had received God’s covenant, temple, priesthood, and promises, yet persistent idolatry and injustice revealed spiritual barrenness. A fruitless vine could not fulfill the purpose for which it was planted.

How much less can it ever be useful when the fire has consumed it and charred it!
The fire portrays the judgment God was bringing through Babylon against Jerusalem. Having rejected repeated warnings, the city would suffer siege, destruction, and exile (Ezek. 14:12–23). Burned vine wood could produce neither fruit nor any useful object, illustrating the ruin that rebellion brings. Yet God’s judgment did not cancel His covenant purposes. Jesus Christ is the true vine, and those who abide in Him bear the fruit Israel failed to produce in her unbelief (John 15:1–5).
